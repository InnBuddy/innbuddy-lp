11:T1db6,<p>“Staying in memory through scent” is not a metaphor. It is about the structure of the brain. That is why an inn’s scent is worth designing properly. But what belongs at the center of the design is not an aroma diffuser but the materials of the land. This article is about that two-tier approach.</p><h2>Only smell does not pass the checkpoint of reason</h2><p>Information that enters through the eyes and ears is first sorted at a relay point in the brain called the “thalamus” before reaching the areas of thought. But scent information entering through the nose does not go through the thalamus; it is delivered directly to the amygdala, which processes emotion, and the hippocampus, which governs memory. Of the five senses, only smell has this direct route. This structure is what lies behind the “Proust effect,” in which a passing scent revives distant memories along with their emotions. The name comes from the French writer Marcel Proust’s novel “In Search of Lost Time” (from 1913). It is the famous scene in which the scent of a madeleine dipped in tea triggers the narrator’s childhood memories, which come back complete with their emotions. Research has reported that memories evoked by smells tend to be older and more emotionally charged than memories evoked by sight or sound.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Of the five senses, scent information alone bypasses the thalamus and reaches the amygdala (emotion) and hippocampus (memory) directly</strong> — Smell memories are “older and more emotional” because of this brain structure (the Proust effect).</div><p>Translated into an inn’s work, this fact means: the inn’s descriptive text reaches the guest’s head, but the inn’s scent reaches the guest’s hippocampus. The moment they smell the same scent after returning home, the morning at that inn comes back complete with its emotions. Designing scent is not designing advertising but designing memory.</p><h2>What Grasse protected was not perfume but “the fields that grow the flowers”</h2><p>Grasse in southern France is a perfume-making town with a tradition dating back to around the 17th century. In November 2018, the region’s “skills related to perfume” were inscribed as UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage. The inscription consists of three pillars: the cultivation of perfume plants, knowledge of natural raw materials and their processing, and the art of perfume composition. In other words, what UNESCO recognized was not bottled perfume but the chain of land and hands that grows, picks, extracts, and blends the flowers. Grasse’s flower fields, which covered 1,300 hectares at the start of the 20th century, were pushed aside by cheap foreign raw materials, and by the early 2000s the growers had reportedly dwindled to just a handful. The momentum for inscription grew out of a sense of crisis over this decline, and flower cultivation is now on the road to revival. The town is also known as a place where Chanel and Dior have flower fields.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>Grasse’s “skills related to perfume” were inscribed as UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2018 (three pillars: cultivation, processing, composition)</strong> — What the capital of perfume protected at world-heritage level was not the product but the fields and skills that grow the flowers. It is also a story of revival from crisis.</div><p>Why does this story work for a small inn? Because even at the summit of the fragrance industry, the source of value was “growing flowers on the land.” The perfume an inn can buy contains no memory of the land. Meanwhile, some of the world’s huge hotel chains have systems that waft the same scent through every hotel in every country (Westin’s “White Tea” is famous). The more a chain’s scent is “the same everywhere,” the more a small inn’s weapon becomes “a scent found only in that land.” Turned around, an inn that sprays a store-bought scent through a diffuser is merely imitating a chain.</p><h2>The inn’s scent, made from the land’s materials</h2><ul><li><strong>Make it by drying</strong> — Yuzu and mandarin peel, offcuts of hinoki and sugi, seasonal flowers and herbs. Simply drying them keeps their scent for a year. Inns with orchards, inns in woodworking villages, and tea-field inns are each a source of materials.</li><li><strong>Have guests “make” scent sachets</strong> — Including the experience of filling them turns the souvenir from “something bought” into “a memory made.” It is a process children can do safely too.</li><li><strong>One line of scent guidance</strong> — “The morning kitchen smells of freshly cooked rice.” “In November, the corridor smells of yuzu being dried.” Give a smell a name, and it is treated the same as a view.</li><li><strong>Review the cleaning products above all</strong> — An inn’s “default scent” is actually its detergent and fabric softener. Choose consciously rather than unconsciously. Deodorizing and fragrance-free are fine designs of scent too.</li><li><strong>What not to do</strong> — Do not burn aromas in the lobby. Do not force scent on guest rooms. Strong-smelling artificial air fresheners are the thinnest investment, unlikely to become objects of the Proust effect.</li></ul><h2>Allergies, and the boundaries of scent</h2><ul><li><strong>Allergies and asthma</strong> — Scent can trigger headaches and respiratory symptoms. Have a system that lets you ask at booking, “Would you prefer that we not use strongly scented items?”</li><li><strong>Offering choices</strong> — Make scent sachets something guests “can choose” rather than something “placed.” Write the existence of fragrance-free guest rooms and corners in the guidance.</li><li><strong>Fire</strong> — Incense and aroma candles use fire. Make clear whether they may be used in guest rooms. Electric types also get hot on the surface.</li><li><strong>Identifying plants</strong> — When using wild flowers and grasses for scent, correct identification and confirmation of safety are essential. Do not use materials whose pesticide-spraying history is unknown.</li></ul><h2>InnBuddy VIEW</h2><p>Scent is the only one of the five senses that reaches memory without passing the checkpoint of reason. And what Grasse, the capital of perfume, protected through UNESCO was the skill of fields and hands.
