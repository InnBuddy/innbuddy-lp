1b:T19b3,<p>A property's staff walk the same corridors every day and ready the same rooms. The eye is a creature of habit. The stain on the wall disappears; the wiring becomes scenery; the accumulated small bothers go unconscious, like breathing. But the traveler's eye has never seen that room even once. That is why you photograph — an act of diagnosis: seeing the room for the first time, through the traveler's borrowed eyes. This article organizes the technique that diagnosis needs into two points, "composition" and "light." What to shoot (which single image of the room becomes the inn's face) belongs to "The Photo Is 90%"; here we handle only "how to shoot."</p>

<h2>Light — shoot in the morning, toward the window</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Sixty to ninety minutes after sunrise is the guide</strong> — Light from a low sun enters the room at a slant: soft, with long shadows. Shadows draw texture. The overhead light of midday flattens the room and kills the morning's fullness.</li>
<li><strong>Shoot toward the window</strong> — The angle at which natural light crosses the room diagonally. Not against the light — a stance that takes the flow of light inside the room.</li>
<li><strong>Turn the ceiling lights off</strong> — Light from interior fixtures alone mixes colors, flattens the room, and clouds the photo. Natural light as the lead, lamps as the supporting role. This principle is the first switch that decides the quality of light.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Composition — diagonal, level, margin</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Shoot from the room's diagonal (corner)</strong> — Stand at the corner farthest from the camera, and the room's longest distance enters the frame. This is virtually the only composition that conveys spaciousness. A room shot from the middle of a wall looks smaller than it is.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the horizontal level</strong> — A photo with tilted walls gives the viewer an unconscious unease. Turn on the camera's grid and set the verticals and horizontals of the walls and the bed parallel to the frame's edges.</li>
<li><strong>One third of the frame to margin</strong> — The view, the sky, a wall surface. Margin is not waste; it is the frame for what you are selling. A crowded room photo looks cheaper than the reality.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Styling before the shot — five points</h2>

<ol>
<li>Make the bed (smooth the sheets. Smooth them by hand, then once more).</li>
<li>Remove personal items and cleaning tools (the mop, the bucket — and my own bag).</li>
<li>Open the curtains fully (let the light in. Forget this and the morning light dies).</li>
<li>Light one stand lamp only (a supporting role for warmth. Keep the ceiling lights off).</li>
<li>Open the bathroom door, bright (a closed door photographs as the symbol of "a dark place").</li>
</ol>

<h2>The minimum shot list — six frames</h2>

<ol>
<li>The whole room (diagonal, wide, morning light) — the first image.</li>
<li>A close-up of the bed (the texture of the linen).</li>
<li>The bathroom (bright, clean).</li>
<li>The view (the world seen from the window).</li>
<li>The desk and work space (life after arriving).</li>
<li>One detail (amenities, art, materials).</li>
</ol>

<h2>To photograph is to diagnose</h2>

<p>Let me state plainly the principle behind everything so far. Some things become visible only when you look through the finder — a stain, a loose thread, wiring, a peel. The lens is a device that resets the accustomed eye. So on shooting day, build the inn habit of zooming into the photos on the spot and writing the "things that would not fit the photo" onto an improvement list. Next month's photos get better, and next month's rooms get tidier. Photography runs on the same cycle as the cleaning inspection. Keep it up half a year, and you become an inn where the rooms and the photos improve together.</p>

<h2>The other side of shooting</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Wide-angle exaggeration</strong> — A wide lens enlarges the near and widens the far. A photo made to look too roomy turns into disappointment on arrival and gets written into a review. Use the wide angle — from an honest position.</li>
<li><strong>The line on editing</strong> — Correct color and brightness only as far as "what it actually looked like." Over-saturated photos are remembered as fraud.</li>
<li><strong>The window changes with the seasons</strong> — Gather winter bookings with autumn-foliage photos and the winter guest is let down. Shoot the seasonal window twice a year.</li>
<li><strong>People in the frame</strong> — Keep other guests out; if staff appear, get their consent. Photos are published worldwide on the booking sites.</li>
<li><strong>The trap of photographing only the perfect vacant room</strong> — One photo of the room as it is actually lived in (where the luggage goes, where the outlets are, when the morning light comes in) conveys an inn's honesty. A room with no life visible looks like a room you cannot live in.</li>
<li><strong>Frequency of shooting</strong> — Change the linen, move the furniture, rehang the art — and the photos are already old. When the room changes, reshoot. Faster than any inn can manage, its photos grow old.</li>
</ul>

<p>Let me fold the technique up once, at the end. Light: morning, toward the window. Composition: from the diagonal, level, one third margin. Styling: five points. Shots: six at minimum. Everything in this article reduces to these three lines. Tomorrow morning, sixty minutes after sunrise, one room, fifteen minutes. The camera is the traveler's eye. The room that eye sees for the first time — you can see it once again today.</p>

<div class="note">This article is intended for sharing ideas. Times of day and compositions are practical guidelines; the optimum shifts with the room's shape and orientation. For the photos you publish, check each booking site's and platform's terms (size, count, handling of editing).</div>

<h2>1.光线——早上面向窗户拍照。</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>日出后60-90分钟</strong>是黄金时间（光线柔和，影子长）。</li>
<li>将照片对准窗户，让自然光以一定角度进入房间。</li>
<li><strong>关掉顶灯</strong>（只有室内照明才能使房间看起来平坦）。</li>
</ul>
<h2>2. 构图——对角线、水平线、边距</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>从房间的对角线（角落）</strong>在广教拍照。</li>
<li>保持地平线水平（墙壁和床不倾斜）。</li>
<li>绘画<strong>1/3作为保证金</strong>（景色、天空、墙壁）。</li>
</ul>
<h2>3.拍摄前造型（5分）</h2>
<ol>
<li>整理床铺（抚平床单上的皱纹）。</li>
<li>取出个人物品和清洁用品。</li>
<li>完全打开窗帘（让光线进来）。</li>
<li>只有一盏立灯亮起（暖光）。</li>
<li>打开浴室门并将其照亮。</li>
</ol>
<h2>4. 至少 6 个镜头的列表</h2>
<ol start="6">
<li>第一张照片（房间的角落和晨光）。</li>
<li>床的特写（纹理）。</li>
<li>浴室（明亮）。</li>
<li>查看（从窗口看到的东西）。</li>
<li>办公桌/工作空间。</li>
<li>1 个细节（便利设施/艺术/材料）。</li>
</ol>

<h2>为什么现在值得关注？</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>客人期待的变化：</strong>与五年前相比，客人对“如何拍摄客房照片-构图和光线”的期望发生了变化。过去“有了更好”的服务，如今已变成“没有就难以被选择”。适应这些变化，是住宿设施持续经营的前提。</li>
<li><strong>与地区关系：</strong>住宿运营的举措提升的不只是单个旅馆，也包括整个地区的价值。通过与当地生产者和周边商户合作，旅馆的行动会成为“当地故事”。社区共同参与，才更容易长期持续。</li>
<li><strong>不增加员工负担：</strong>新举措不应增加员工负担。通过建立机制，用一张表或每次15分钟的学习来运转，就能在不增加员工负担的情况下持续改进。</li>
</ul>

<h2>避免失败的3个要点</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>从小处开始——先做好一件事：</strong>一开始就想全部改变，往往难以持续。先从一件事开始。可以从一间客房、一个货架或一句话开始；即使效果不理想，影响也小，更容易调整。</li>
<li><strong>记录——用一张表看清楚：</strong>把行动记录在一张表里，效果就能被看见。你会知道哪些做法有效、哪些需要调整，记录也会成为下一轮改进的依据。</li>
<li><strong>持续——每周一次，每次15分钟：</strong>新行动需要持续。每周留出15分钟，逐渐形成习惯，最后才会成为团队文化。</li>
</ol>
