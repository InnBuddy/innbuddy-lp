11:T214b,<p>Talk of staff retention tends to turn into moralizing on the front line: "let's make a workplace with a good atmosphere." But before that, there are numbers to face. According to the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's Employment Trends Survey (2022), the separation rate in the lodging and food-service industries is 26.8% — far above the 15.0% all-industry average, the highest among the major industries. In this industry, roughly one in four people leaves the workplace within a year. Meanwhile, the hiring rate is also high, at 31.3%. Hire, quit, hire again — an industry-wide structure of people forever circulating is there, in the numbers. This article organizes, through the three basics of "wanting to keep working here" — growth, pride, and companions — what an individual inn can actually do inside that whirlpool.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>In the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's Employment Trends Survey (2022), the separation rate for the lodging and food-service industries is 26.8% against a 15.0% all-industry average — the highest among the major industries (hiring rate: 31.3%)</strong> — In an industry structure where roughly one in four leaves within a year, the differences in each inn's power to retain people carry real weight.</div>

<p>There is one more number worth pausing on. In the same ministry's General Survey on Working Conditions (2022), the paid-leave uptake rate in the lodging and food-service industries is 44.3% — also the lowest of all industries. The difficulty of taking days off exists not as a feeling but as statistics. Why this number comes first will become clear at the end of this article. First, the three basics that work — on top of that foundation — with as much force as pay and days off.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>In the Ministry's General Survey on Working Conditions (2022), the average paid-leave uptake rate in the lodging and food-service industries is 44.3%, the lowest of all industries</strong> — The difficulty of taking days off is not personal weakness but a structural reality of the industry.</div>

<h2>Growth — something new you can do, every week</h2>

<p>The first basic is growth. People do not easily leave a workplace where they feel the list of things they can do is growing. Put the other way, a workplace where every day is the same task at the same pace will, however pleasant the atmosphere, eventually have its exits scouted. A week is a sufficient unit of growth at an inn. This week, handling check-in alone. Next week, giving sightseeing guidance with a local map. Beyond that: pouring the local beer, running the morning meeting, greeting guests in English. Designing growth begins with writing the inn's skills into a "can-do list" — from a single cleaning pattern to a line of guest service to equipment checks. With the list, you can talk with each person about what to learn next. A workplace where the order of learning is visible retains learners at a different rate.</p>

<h2>Pride — being able to say what your work is, in one line</h2>

<p>The second basic is pride. Not "I work at an inn," but "I brew coffee with mountain water" or "I do the work of keeping the inn of this region's festival" — when the content of the work can be said in a single phrase, the work shifts from "a job I was hired for" to "mine." Pride grows from the internal dialogue of putting what the inn aspires to into words and sharing them with the front line (internal branding), and from working with the region. Use a local farmer's vegetables at breakfast, and have the staff able to say that farmer's name. That alone connects the context of the work to the region, and pride changes from "a role inside the inn" to "a role in the community."</p>

<h2>Companions — someone beside you when it is hard</h2>

<p>The third basic is companions. What decides retention is not the fun times but who is beside them in the hard ones. A run of frantic days, a night of unreasonable complaints. A staff member who is alone there browses the next job posting. Companions are built through hiring (a selection process that brings people you want to work with) and meetings (a morning meeting that, after the business, spends a little time on the humans). For a small inn, how to rescue a solo night shift is itself the design of companionship.</p>

<h2>Operation — a monthly one-on-one, and three questions</h2>

<p>Left alone, the three basics fade. The tool that maintains them is the monthly one-on-one (a short, face-to-face conversation). Ask only three things: "What did you become able to do this month?" "Which part of this work do you take pride in?" "When things get hard, is there someone you can rely on?" A staff member who answers all three without hesitation rarely leaves. If even one draws a blank, that is your work for the next month. If growth has stalled, choose the next item on the can-do list together. If pride is fading, retell the context of the work. If there is no one to rely on, revisit the design of shifts and greetings. The essence of the one-on-one is the asking itself. People do not leave a workplace where they are "heard" — not merely "watched."</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">DID YOU KNOW?</span><strong>In the Ministry's survey on job separations of new graduates, 49.7% of university graduates who joined the lodging and food-service industries had left within three years (March 2020 graduates) — the highest of all industries</strong> — How the first three years are designed is the vital point of an inn's retaining power.</div>

<h2>The other side of the three basics — don't paper over the foundation</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Pay and days off are the foundation, not a bargaining chip</strong> — This article cited the 44.3% paid-leave uptake rate. The three basics work on top of the foundation of wages and holidays. Management that tries to substitute the three basics for a raise will be seen through by the front line, and will lose trust.</li>
<li><strong>Don't let the one-on-one become a complaints dump</strong> — A one-on-one that only listens and ends is a device for collecting grievances and leaving them. When complaints about pay or days off surface, they are not a matter to be handled with the three basics; decide the response as a matter of the inn's management.</li>
<li><strong>If you cannot raise pay, say so honestly — and make another move</strong> — A small inn that cannot touch wages right away can show sincerity through smarter rest (proposing weekdays off, consecutive days in the seasonal trough) and through visibility (asking about desired days off first, in the one-on-one).</li>
<li><strong>The abuse of "we're like family"</strong> — Some workplaces use the word "family" to simultaneously create reasons not to rest and vagueness of boundaries. For building companionship, the words of companions are enough.</li>
<li><strong>Lies at hiring</strong> — If you sell "a homelike workplace," keep substantiating it in morning meetings and one-on-ones. A lie discovered right after joining is the leading reason for leaving.</li>
</ul>

<p>Picture your inn three years from now. The morning meeting runs with the same faces. Around it, "what I became able to do last week" goes from person to person, one line each. That begins tonight, with writing the first line of the can-do list. The numbers belong to the industry; the morning meeting three years from now belongs to your inn.</p>
