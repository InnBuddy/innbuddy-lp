お土産には、2つの血統があります。1つは西洋のスーベニア（souvenir）。フランス語で「思い出す」という動詞そのもので、旅の記憶を自分のために残す買い物です。もう1つは日本のお土産。語源は諸説ありますが、有力なのは「宮笥（みやけ）」— 神社のお札やお供え物を入れる容器だったという説です。つまり日本のお土産は、もともと「配るもの」として生まれました。宿のお土産作りは、この2つの血統を同時に満たす設計です。

伊勢講が残した「分ける」という原型

江戸の庶民にとって、伊勢参りは一生に一度の遠旅でした。費用を積み立てた「講」の代表者が参拝し、お札や縁起物を持ち帰って、御利益を仲間に分配します。お土産の習慣は、ここから根付いたとされているのです。行った証拠を、行けなかった人々に分けます。日本のお土産の原型は、記念品ではなく「共有」でした。一方で漢字の「土産」は、本来「どさん」と読む漢語で「土地の産物」を意味します。土地の産み出したものを、土地を離れて伝えるのです。この2重の意味 — 共有と土地 — が、お土産の設計図そのものです。

宿のケーキが、国のお土産になりました

1832年、オーストリアの宰相メッテルニヒの宮廷で、シェフが病に倒れました。代わりにデザートを作ることになったのは、16歳の見習いコック、フランツ・ザッハー。彼が生み出したチョコレートケーキは評判となり、1876年、息子のエドゥアルトがウィーンにホテル・ザッハーを開業すると、父のケーキはホテルの看板になりました。ザッハトルテです。その後、老舗菓子店デメルとの間で「オリジナル」の名をかけた長い法廷闘争が続き、1963年の和解で「オリジナル・ザッハトルテ」を名乗れるのはホテル・ザッハーの製品だけと決まりました。今も自社工場で手作業で作られ、年間数十万個が世界中に出荷される、ウィーンを代表するお土産です。

この話の核心は、ケーキの美味しさではなく「宿のお土産が国のお土産になった」ということです。宿で出していた菓子が、箱を持ち帰るお土産になり、街の名物になり、最後には「本物」の定義を法廷で争うまでになりました。あなたの宿の朝食やお茶請けの中にも、箱詰めにできるものはありませんか。

Souvenirs have two lineages. One is the Western souvenir. It is the French verb “to remember” itself, a purchase that preserves the memory of a trip for oneself. The other is the Japanese omiyage. There are various theories about its origin, but a leading one is that it comes from “miyake” (written with the characters for “shrine” and “container”) — a container that held shrine talismans and offerings. In other words, Japanese omiyage were originally born as “something to hand out.” Making souvenirs at an inn is a design that satisfies these two lineages at once.

The prototype of “sharing” left by the Ise-kō

For ordinary people in Edo, a pilgrimage to Ise was a once-in-a-lifetime long journey. A representative of a “kō” (a mutual association whose members pooled their savings for the trip) made the pilgrimage, brought back talismans and good-luck charms, and distributed the blessings among the members. The custom of omiyage is said to have taken root from here. Sharing the proof of having gone with the people who could not go. The prototype of the Japanese omiyage was not a keepsake but “sharing.” Meanwhile, the two kanji used to write omiyage (literally “land” and “produce”) were originally a Chinese-derived word read “dosan,” meaning “the products of the land.” Carrying what the land produced away from the land to share it. This double meaning — sharing and the land — is the very blueprint of omiyage.

An inn’s cake became a national souvenir

In 1832, at the court of the Austrian chancellor Metternich, the chef fell ill. The one who ended up making the dessert in his place was a 16-year-old apprentice cook, Franz Sacher. The chocolate cake he created won acclaim, and in 1876, when his son Eduard opened the Hotel Sacher in Vienna, his father’s cake became the hotel’s signature. It is the Sachertorte. A long legal battle then followed with the venerable confectioner Demel over the name “original,” and a 1963 settlement determined that only the Hotel Sacher’s product could be called the “Original Sacher-Torte.” It is still made by hand in the company’s own facility, with several hundred thousand shipped around the world each year, a souvenir that represents Vienna.

The core of this story is not how delicious the cake is but that “an inn’s souvenir became a national souvenir.” A confection served at an inn became a souvenir taken home in a box, became the city’s specialty, and in the end went as far as a court battle over the definition of “the genuine article.” Among your inn’s breakfast dishes or tea snacks, isn’t there something that could be boxed?

It is proof that a single confection at an inn can become, as a souvenir, the brand of a city and a country.

An inn’s souvenir that satisfies both lineages

  • One thing “only we have” — Things that can be bought at a roadside station or an airport do not make an inn’s souvenir. Seasonal preserves in jars, house-made tea, scent sachets of dried yuzu peel (the method from the article “Remain in Memory with Scent - Ideas for Remaining in Memory for Overseas Guests with Scent, Tea, Incense”). One item is enough.
  • Strengthening the souvenir side — A souvenir for oneself is “proof of the trip.” Hand guests an empty small box, a “travel box” to fill with chestnut leaves and cards picked up during the stay and take home. The guest makes the contents.
  • Strengthening the omiyage side — For guests with people to hand things out to, offer smaller items that are individually wrapped and keep well. Whether “where it was made” can be read in one line on the back of the bag makes the difference.
  • Packaging from the land — Rather than plastic bags, local washi and paper string, or wooden boxes. The few seconds until it is opened are part of the souvenir, too.
  • A three-line card — Maker, how to use, how to store. What dispels the awkwardness of shopping is three lines of written words.

Souvenirs come with laws and borders

  • Truth in labeling — Labeling of the origin and ingredients of products calling themselves “local specialties” follows food labeling standards. If you write “produced in ...,” it must be true.
  • Guidance for overseas guests — Meat products and fruit are prohibited or restricted from being brought into many countries. Adding one line to souvenir guidance — “Import restrictions may apply” — is an inn’s international courtesy.
  • Expiry dates and transport — Fresh confections expire partway through the trip. Confirm shelf life, temperature, and packing before selling. If a souvenir spoils, the memory of the trip spoils along with it.
  • Don’t oversell — When the lobby becomes a souvenir shop, the inn turns into “a sales floor.” Keep the souvenir shelf small and unobtrusive. Not eroding the value of the stay is the premise.

The souvenir is a device for one’s own memory; omiyage is distribution to others. The Sachertorte is an example of an inn’s confection growing until it became a national souvenir. Start small, by looking among what you serve at breakfast for one item that could be boxed. Making souvenirs is not the design of shopping but the design of the memory of a trip.
