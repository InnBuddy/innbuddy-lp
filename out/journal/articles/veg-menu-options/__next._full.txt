Vegetarian and vegan, plus lactose-free bodies, gluten-free, allergies, religious constraints (halal, kosher), and the pull toward shōjin ryōri, the plant-based cuisine of Japan’s own tradition. <strong>"We can’t accommodate" means losing that guest — and the whole party traveling with them.</strong> This article organizes the "design of options" that answers diverse food preferences, starting from a fact of the labeling system and from the origin of Japan’s food culture.</p>

<h2>What the system teaches — labeling sits "where nothing is mandatory"</h2>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Under the Food Labeling Act’s allergen rules, display of the eight "specified raw materials" — shrimp, crab, walnut, wheat, buckwheat, egg, milk, and peanut (walnut added in the March 2023 revision of the standards) — is mandatory only for processed foods in packaging. Dishes that a restaurant cooks and serves on its premises are outside the scope of the mandate, and whether to provide allergen labeling is left to the operator’s own judgment (the Consumer Affairs Agency’s system, as of writing)</strong> — In other words, no one is obligated to put the allergen table on a property’s menu.</div>

<p>This fact carries a deep meaning. "We can accommodate" itself becomes differentiation — one of the reasons lies here. In the place where the law obligates no one, only the properties that put the eight-item table on the menu can stand on the step of "we can accommodate, and we can prove it." With most properties ending at "we cannot" or "we don’t know," the difference of one table is bigger than you would think.</p>

<h2>Returning to an 800-year origin — the three minds of the "Tenzō Kyōkun"</h2>

<p>Plant-based eating is not "a new trend" in Japan. It has an 800-year lineage. In 1237, the Zen monk Dōgen wrote down the mindset of the "tenzō" — the officer in charge of meals at a training monastery — in the <strong>"Tenzō Kyōkun" (Instructions for the Cook)</strong>. What it teaches are the "three minds": <strong>the joyful mind</strong> (delighting that food can be made), <strong>the parental mind</strong> (standing in the eater’s place), and <strong>the magnanimous mind</strong> (a mind that does not waver). The shōjin ryōri passed down at Eiheiji takes stock from vegetable trimmings and uses every ingredient without waste — evidence that constraint has made cuisine richer, right here, for eight centuries.</p>

<p>So it can be thought of this way. Frame veg support as "an added burden," and the menu goes poor. Frame it as a return to this country’s origin — "constraint has always been the mother of the cuisine" — and the veg plate can become the most beautiful plate the property serves. The parental mind — standing in the eater’s place — is, precisely, an 800-year-old name for standing where the guest with diverse preferences stands.</p>

<h2>Design 1 — the 3-tier structure of "options"</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Tier 1: a base design that is naturally veg-ready</strong> — The foundation of the whole menu. Vegetables, grains, beans, and eggs in the leading roles; meat and fish separated as "toppings."</li>
<li><strong>Tier 2: designated vegetarian plates</strong> — Clearly set two or three dishes per meal, marked "V" on the menu.</li>
<li><strong>Tier 3: on request</strong> — The words "we can prepare" for allergies and religious constraints, and a path that prompts guests to make contact in advance.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Design 2 — managing cross-contamination (food safety)</h2>

<p>"We can accommodate" alone is not enough. The mechanism that "proves we do" is what builds trust.</p>

<ul>
<li>Separation of cooking areas and utensils (dedicated cutting boards, pots, labeled storage)</li>
<li>Allergen display (a table built on the system’s eight items; a fill-in-the-box format reads at a glance)</li>
<li>Teaching staff "why we separate" (the consistency of accommodation lives in people)</li>
</ul>

<h2>Design 3 — managing the cost</h2>

<ul>
<li>Expand the shared base (vegetable stock, grains, beans, eggs) — low-cost ingredients take the lead roles.</li>
<li>The "meat and fish as toppings" separation — one plate reused across several preferences.</li>
<li>The cost increase is around 5%, depending on design (a reference value). It can be woven into the pricing of direct-booking benefits or seasonal menus.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The other side — when accommodation exhausts the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The weight of "we can accommodate"</strong> — An "we can accommodate" that cannot be delivered is more dangerous than "we don’t, from the start." If training cannot keep up, promise, first, only "providing the allergen table."</li>
<li><strong>The plate of endurance</strong> — The standard of taste for a veg plate should be the same as for the ordinary ones. "It’s vegetables, so this will do" ends the relationship after one visit.</li>
<li><strong>The physics of separation</strong> — Complete separation is hard in a small kitchen. Set the "unit of separation" — one shelf, one cutting board — and align the scope of the promise to it.</li>
<li><strong>Religious accommodation is a separate expertise</strong> — Halal and kosher include requirements beyond cooking (see the halal-setup article). Draw the line, and guide guests honestly.</li>
<li><strong>Overusing "may contain"</strong> — A table that writes "may contain" on everything guarantees nothing. Be precise only where you know, and write the reason where you don’t.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is two sheets. Choose one dish from your present menu and mark it "V." And make one allergen table with the eight items as boxes to fill in. It can be done in a week — and precisely because no one is obligated to do it, it works.</p>

<div class="note">This article is intended for sharing ideas. Allergen accommodation is not a medical guarantee. For individual allergies, the safe design combines the premise "we regret we cannot make medical judgments" with the guest’s own judgment. For details of the labeling system, please check the Consumer Affairs Agency’s latest materials.</div>

<h2>1. 为什么现在要“饮食多样化”？</h2>
<p>国际客人来自对饮食限制有高度自我控制的文化。许多原产国都有严格的过敏原和成分标签制度，并且有根据食物成分进食的习惯。另一方面，国内客人也会出于多种动机寻求选择，包括健康导向、宗教原因，或者只是“今天以植物为主”。</p>
<p>重要的是<strong>“我们可以回应”本身就是差异化的</strong>它是要成为。还有许多住宿设施要么“无法提供”，要么“不知道”。“提供”的住宿设施在搜索时被选择的概率会更高。</p>
<h2>2.设计一：3层“选择”结构</h2>
<p>将“素食友好”纳入整个菜单，而不是将其视为单独的特殊待遇：</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>第一层：可自然容纳蔬菜的基本设计</strong> — 整个菜单的基础。以蔬菜、五谷、豆类、蛋类为主料，肉、鱼可分开作为“配料”。</li>
<li><strong>第 2 级：指定素食/素食餐盘</strong> — 明确定义每餐 2-3 个项目，并将其在菜单上显示为“V”。</li>
<li><strong>第三层：请求响应</strong> — 针对过敏/宗教限制的“可用”脚本。建立一条鼓励提前联系的沟通渠道。</li>
</ol>
<h2>3、设计二：交叉污染控制（食品安全）</h2>
<p>仅仅能够做出回应是不够的。证明“合规”的机制可以创造信任：</p>
<ul>
<li>烹饪区域和器具分开（专用切菜板、锅和存储标签）</li>
<li>基于原产国的过敏原显示（主要过敏原+牛奶/麸质）</li>
<li>对员工进行“我们为什么分开”的培训（反应的一致性）</li>
</ul>
<p>“我可以处理它，我可以证明这一点。”这两个步骤将使客人安心并在 SNS 上进行交流。</p>
<h2>4.设计三：控制成本</h2>
<p>多样性支持看起来可能会增加成本，但可以通过设计来减少：</p>
<ul>
<li>增加共同基础（蔬菜汤、谷物、豆类、鸡蛋）——低成本原料将成为焦点</li>
<li>“肉鱼配料”分离设计——一盘多种口味</li>
<li>示例：成本增加约 5%。可以转嫁给直接福利和季节性菜单价格</li>
</ul>
