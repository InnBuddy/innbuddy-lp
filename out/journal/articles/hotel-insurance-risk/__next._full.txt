— 自分で守るリスク</h2><p>江戸の商家は、火事から財産を守るために蔵を建てました。分厚い壁、小さな窓、火の粉を受けにくい構え——店が焼けても、蔵が残れば商いは再開できました。蔵造りは、<strong>リスクを自分の力で減らす設計</strong>です。現代の宿で言えば、火の元の点検や夜の巡回(<a href="#safety-patrol-night">安全パトロール — 誰も見ていない「見る」の基本</a>が担う部分)が、この仲間です。</p><p>しかし、蔵には限界があります。地震で地盤ごと傾けば、水害で町ごと沈めば、1軒の蔵では守りきれない。<strong>自分で守れるリスクと、守れないリスク</strong>——この線を引くことが、「起きる前に知る」の第一歩です。</p><h2>コーヒーハウスに生まれた「みんなの蔵」</h2><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>1688年頃のロンドン、テムズ川の船着場に近いタワー街に、エドワード・ロイドのコーヒーハウスがあった</strong> — 船主と貿易商、資産家が集まり、航海のリスクを「少しずつ引き受ける」取引が行われた。ここが世界の保険市場「ロイド」の始まりとされる(東京海上日動「海上保険の歴史」による)。</div><p>船が1隻沈めば、船主は破産します。でも、大勢でリスクを分け持てば、1人の被害は小さくなる。<strong>保険とは、みんなで持つ蔵</strong>です。リスクそのものは消せない。けれど「持ち方」は変えられる——保険の本質は、この1行に集まります。蔵を建てること(防ぐ)と、みんなの蔵に入ること(分ける)は、矛盾しません。両方を持つのが、宿のリスク設計です。</p><h2>知っておくべき3つのリスク — 建物・ゲスト・休業</h2><p>宿のリスクは、大きく3つのグループに分かれて見えます。起きる前に知るべきは、この3枚の地図です。</p><ol><li><strong>建物・設備</strong> — 火災、水害、地震、雪害。山の宿は雪と坂、海の宿は台風と高潮——地域によって、リスクの形が違います。修理費用だけでなく、営業できない期間の損失まで含めて考えるグループです。</li><li><strong>ゲスト</strong> — 客室内でのケガ、食中毒、アレルギー、盗難。宿の賠償責任に直結するグループで、起こる頻度は低くても、1回の重さが大きいのが特徴です。</li><li><strong>休業</strong> — 災害や事故で営業できない期間の損失。売上が止まっても、スタッフの給与と固定費は止まりません。「建物は直る。でも直る間のお金」まで考えておくグループです。</li></ol><h2>法律が定める「宿の責任」</h2><div class="stats"><span 1b:T23fb,<p>Insurance talk is too late after it happens. Reading the policy after a fire and learning "earthquake was excluded" — and reading the same line on a quiet weekday afternoon when nothing has happened — the knowledge is the same, but the meaning is completely different. This article is not about insurance products: it is about drawing <strong>the "map of risks" to know before anything happens</strong> (for product selection, please consult specialists such as insurance agents and accountants).</p>

<h2>Building the storehouse — risks you guard yourself</h2>

<p>Edo merchant houses built storehouses to protect their property from fire. Thick walls, small windows, a posture that catches few sparks — even if the shop burned, as long as the storehouse stood, business could restart. The kura storehouse is <strong>a design that reduces risk by one’s own strength</strong>. In a modern property, the checking of fire sources and the night patrol (the part handled by <a href="#safety-patrol-night">Safety Patrol: The Basics of Watching When No One Watches</a>) are of this family.</p>

<p>But the storehouse has limits. If an earthquake tilts it with the ground, if a flood sinks it with the town, a single storehouse cannot protect everything. <strong>The risks you can guard yourself, and the risks you cannot</strong> — drawing this line is the first step of "knowing before it happens."</p>

<h2>The "storehouse of everyone," born in a coffee house</h2>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>Around 1688, in Tower Street near the Thames wharves in London, stood Edward Lloyd’s coffee house</strong> — Shipowners, merchants, and men of means gathered, and trades were made in which each "took on a little" of a voyage’s risk. This is said to be the beginning of the world’s insurance market, "Lloyd’s" (according to Tokyo Marine & Nichido’s "History of Marine Insurance").</div>

<p>If one ship sank, the owner went bankrupt. But with many sharing the risk, each person’s loss grows small. <strong>Insurance is a storehouse everyone holds together.</strong> The risk itself cannot be erased. But "how it is held" can be changed — the essence of insurance gathers in that one line. Building a storehouse (preventing) and entering the storehouse of everyone (sharing) do not contradict. Holding both is the design of a property’s risks.</p>

<h2>3 risks to know — building, guests, closure</h2>

<p>A property’s risks sort, at the large scale, into three groups. What to know before anything happens is these three maps.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Building and equipment</strong> — Fire, flood, earthquake, snow. The mountain property faces snow and slopes; the seaside property, typhoons and storm surges — the shape of risk differs by region. A group to think about including not only repair costs but the loss of the period when business cannot run.</li>
<li><strong>Guests</strong> — Injuries in the room, food poisoning, allergies, theft. This group connects directly to the property’s liability: low frequency, but each single event is heavy.</li>
<li><strong>Closure</strong> — The loss of a period when business cannot run due to disaster or accident. Even with revenue stopped, staff salaries and fixed costs do not stop. The group where you think ahead to "the building will be repaired. But the money while it is."</li>
</ol>

<h2>The "innkeeper’s liability" defined by law</h2>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Japan’s Commercial Code defines the liability of "jouya operators" (innkeepers and similar hosting businesses), including hotels and ryokan</strong> — ① Luggage received from a guest: liable unless force majeure is proven ② Even without receipt, liable if the property’s willful act or negligence loses a guest’s belongings ③ Money, securities, and other valuables: not liable unless the type and value were declared at deposit (unless the property was grossly negligent) ④ A notice of "we assume no liability whatsoever" does not exempt (a general outline as of writing).</div>

<p>What works in practice are the next three. Luggage taken in: <strong>write the item names and counts and hand them over</strong>. Valuables: <strong>receive them on a form with a declaration field</strong> (some terms of stay set a liability cap for undeclared valuables — per Meijo University research on accommodation terms). And instead of a pasted notice, <strong>tell them in the words of check-in: "valuables, please, to the front desk."</strong> The law says "a notice does not exempt" — so the telling, too, should be in words.</p>

<h2>Earthquake and flood, where "being covered" is not enough</h2>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Earthquake insurance can only be joined as a set with fire insurance, and the insured amount must be within 30–50% of the fire-insurance amount</strong> — The caps are ¥50 million for buildings and ¥10 million for household goods (Order for Enforcement of the Earthquake Insurance Act; General Insurance Association of Japan, "Home Insurance Q&A"). Payout ratios: total loss = 100% of the insured amount; majority loss = 60%; partial-majority loss = 30%; partial loss = 5%.</div>

<p>This is the place where "after it happens" is latest of all. Covered by fire insurance, and damage from earthquake, eruption, or tsunami sits in a separate frame. If rebuilding costs exceed the cap, the excess can only come from your own pocket. Flood, too, is treated differently policy by policy. Confirming once, before anything happens, <strong>how far it returns you</strong> — that is the real reason to read the policy.</p>

<h2>60 minutes before it happens — the work of one sheet</h2>

<p>The work takes 60 minutes and finishes on one sheet of paper.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Put the policies on one sheet</strong> — Four lines: building; goods and equipment; liability; closure. For each, write "how far it pays." The whole shape of your insurance shows in these four lines.</li>
<li><strong>Highlight the "exclusions"</strong> — Earthquake, flood, snow… The excluded lines are precisely the information that decides the first move when it happens. Read them before the covered lines.</li>
<li><strong>Compare with a same-scale neighbor property</strong> — The shape of the region’s risks is known best by the property next door. Once a year, in the off-season, hold an "insurance study meeting" — a move that may fairly be counted as one of the collaborations with neighbors (<a href="#local-business-collaboration">Local Business Collaboration: Basics of Reservation-Driving Partnerships</a>).</li>
</ol>

<p>For the review day, take one day in the off-season of <a href="#12month-marketing-calendar">The 12 Months of a Property: A Ready-to-Use Marketing Calendar</a>. Mark the calendar, and it becomes habit.</p>

<h2>The other side — 5 traps insurance builds</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Joining, relaxing, easing the preparedness</strong> — Insurance is the tool of "after." "Before" is the patrol and the drills (<a href="#earthquake-emergency-30min">Earthquake Response: The First 30 Minutes of the Manual</a>).</li>
<li><strong>Over-insuring</strong> — Premiums are a monthly fixed cost. Making fixed costs visible is bookkeeping’s work (<a href="#small-hotel-bookkeeping">Small Hotel Bookkeeping: Three Numbers the Owner Watches Monthly</a>).</li>
<li><strong>Terms that change at renewal</strong> — There are cases where flood coverage shrinks. The renewal month is exactly the day for the highlighter.</li>
<li><strong>The blind spot of business interruption</strong> — The building’s insurance is "the money to repair"; closure coverage is "the money while stopped." Ask about both.</li>
<li><strong>Carrying everything on insurance</strong> — Take on yourself the risks you can take on. The storehouse, and the storehouse of everyone. The design is to hold both.</li>
</ul>

<p>Insurance is not "buying peace of mind" but <strong>a tool for dividing damage</strong>. What happens; how far you carry it yourself; from where you share it — draw that one sheet before anything happens. The first move, when it happens, changes with that sheet.</p>
