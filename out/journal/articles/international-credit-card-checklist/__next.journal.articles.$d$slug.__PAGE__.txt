11:T1b99,<p>For inbound guests, <strong>"online booking + international card payment" is a premise, not an option</strong>. Travelers who phone around to find a room are now rare. This article organizes what Japanese properties should prepare as a checklist of 13 items in three blocks — payment, booking, trust — and closes with the story of the "last three seconds," which appears on no checklist.</p>

<h2>Block 1 — payment (5 items)</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Accepting the major international cards</strong> — Visa, Mastercard, JCB, Amex. Putting the card marks on your sign and site becomes a display of reassurance.</li>
<li><strong>Contactless payment</strong> — The type you pay by just holding the card or phone over the terminal. It cuts the checkout line and the time.</li>
<li><strong>A system for multi-currency charging</strong> — Billing in Japanese yen is the base. Be able to give the exchange-rate explanation (the charge is made in Japanese yen and converted at the guest’s card company’s rate).</li>
<li><strong>Pre-authorization</strong> — A mechanism that temporarily secures a card’s credit line before arrival. Used for deposits and for guaranteeing expensive rooms and buyouts. The secured line has an expiry, so know too that a long stay may need re-authorization.</li>
<li><strong>Electronic receipts</strong> — Before paper, issuing receipts by email. They are needed for expense claims after the trip home.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Block 2 — booking (5 items)</h2>

<ol start="6">
<li><strong>An English booking page</strong> — With the same clarity as your listing copy (see the English listing article).</li>
<li><strong>24-hour online booking</strong> — "Phone only" is the biggest filter against inbound. The daytime of a guest in another time zone is night in Japan.</li>
<li><strong>Instant confirmation</strong> — When a booking arrives, an automatic reply first conveys receipt, and the human-confirmed details follow on top of it.</li>
<li><strong>Set free cancellation (until the day before, and the like)</strong> — A standard expectation among visitors to Japan. If you cannot set it, state the conditions up front.</li>
<li><strong>The "book now" path</strong> — Reaching the booking screen in one tap from the listing or site. Every moment of confusion at the entrance sends the booking to another property.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Block 3 — trust (3 items)</h2>

<ol start="11">
<li><strong>https and a booking engine you can trust</strong> — An unencrypted booking form is shown "Not secure" by the browser (see the direct-site article).</li>
<li><strong>Eliminating "cash only," or explaining it clearly</strong> — If yours is a property where cards cannot be used, that is information to convey "the very first."</li>
<li><strong>A privacy notice</strong> — How the card details and personal information entrusted to you are handled. It overlaps with the law’s display duties.</li>
</ol>

<h2>The last three seconds — the currency selection screen</h2>

<p>Here is the story missing from most checklists. When paying with an overseas-issued card, the payment terminal sometimes asks, "Would you like to pay in yen, or in your currency (dollars, say)?" This is a mechanism called <strong>DCC (dynamic currency conversion)</strong>. The conversion rate presented is set by the provider, often less favorable than the card company’s rate, with a fee commonly added on top. If the guest, unused to it, chooses their home currency, the same room charge becomes more expensive — in the last three seconds of payment, the impression is quietly shaved away.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>DCC (dynamic currency conversion) is a mechanism that proposes payment in the home currency when paying with an overseas card. Because the rate set by the provider (the merchant or payment company) is applied, it tends to be less favorable than usual; explanations describe a surcharge on the order of 3–5% being added. The card issuer’s foreign-transaction fee applies separately, and the user has the right to refuse</strong> — "Which currency is the better choice" depends on the guest’s country and card — basic knowledge a property should have.</div>

<p>What the property can do is simple. First, check once whether this selection screen appears on your payment terminal. Second, if it does — add one line: "If you choose Japanese yen, the conversion is usually made at your card company’s rate." Not a push, but a guide to the facts. A property with that one line is remembered, in the last seconds of checkout, as "a property that understands the details."</p>

<h2>The other side — when payment preparation exhausts the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>You need not do all of it</strong> — The 13 items are not to be done in order but chosen by cost-effectiveness. Start with Block 2 (booking). Lowering the booking barrier first brings more bookings than payment convenience (the spirit of the original design).</li>
<li><strong>The fees are the property’s cost</strong> — Card payment fees are borne by the property, and rates differ by brand and contract. Introduce every method without thinking about your revenue mix, and only thin-margin bookings increase.</li>
<li><strong>A missed re-authorization</strong> — Securing a credit line is not "done once taken." If the line expires while the stay continues, an unguaranteed room comes into being.</li>
<li><strong>Some guests live in a cash culture</strong> — In some countries cash culture remains. While advancing card support, being able to guide guests to the nearest exchange is a preparation kind to both kinds of guest.</li>
<li><strong>The lump "overseas guests"</strong> — Card cultures and currencies differ by market. Preparation matched to your property’s main markets is the cheapest and the most effective.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is, tonight, to assume a payment with an overseas card at the front-desk terminal and check whether the currency selection screen appears. Next, fill in the 13 items with circles, triangles, and crosses, and fix one triangle in Block 2. A guest’s bookings can turn on that single unglamorous fix.</p>

<div class="note">This article is intended for sharing ideas. Payment fees and DCC specifications differ by contract and provider. Please check the terms of your own payment company’s contract.</div>

<li><strong>电子收据</strong> — 通过电子邮件/二维码开具收据。</li>
</ol>

</ol>

</ol>

<h2>为什么现在值得关注？</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>客人期待的变化：</strong>“国际卡和在线预订 - 准备清单” 与五年前相比，如今宾客的期望发生了变化。过去“有了更好”的服务，如今已变成“没有就难以被选择”。适应这些变化，是住宿设施持续经营的前提。</li>
<li><strong>与地区关系：</strong>在网上吸引顾客的努力增加了整个地区的价值，而不仅仅是旅馆本身的价值。通过与当地生产者和周边商户合作，旅馆的行动会成为“当地故事”。社区共同参与，才更容易长期持续。</li>
<li><strong>不增加员工负担：</strong>新举措不应增加员工负担。通过建立机制，用一张表或每次15分钟的学习来运转，就能在不增加员工负担的情况下持续改进。</li>
</ul>

<h2>避免失败的3个要点</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>从小处开始——先做好一件事：</strong>一开始就想全部改变，往往难以持续。先从一件事开始。可以从一间客房、一个货架或一句话开始；即使效果不理想，影响也小，更容易调整。</li>
<li><strong>记录——用一张表看清楚：</strong>把行动记录在一张表里，效果就能被看见。你会知道哪些做法有效、哪些需要调整，记录也会成为下一轮改进的依据。</li>
<li><strong>持续——每周一次，每次15分钟：</strong>新行动需要持续。每周留出15分钟，逐渐形成习惯，最后才会成为团队文化。</li>
</ol>
