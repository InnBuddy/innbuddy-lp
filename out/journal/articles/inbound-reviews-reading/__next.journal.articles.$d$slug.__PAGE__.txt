11:T1b56,<p>At inns where English reviews have started to arrive, what happens first is mostly the same: press the translate button, understand the meaning, and still be at a loss — "I don't know what to respond to." The language barrier can be crossed by machine translation. But the barrier of reading — what is being praised, what stings, and for what purpose it was written — translation cannot cross. This article is about crossing that barrier of reading.</p>

<h2>First, the premises of the venue — a place where everyone "stayed," and one that is not</h2>

<p>Each place reviews appear on carries different premises of trust. On the representative booking site, Booking.com, reviews can be written only by the person who completed the booking on that site and actually stayed (an invitation email arrives after the stay, and the posting period is said to have a limit). Ratings are on a ten-point scale, with fields for "what you liked / what you didn't," and nationality and companions are displayed. Google Maps reviews, on the other hand, require no proof of stay — anyone can write them. In other words, before reading, divide the reading: booking-site English reviews are "testimonies, all by actual guests," while the map's reviews are "voices mixing actual guests and others."</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Booking.com reviews can be posted only by the person who completed the booking and stay on that site (via a post-stay email, with a limit on the posting period), and use a ten-point scale with fields for positives and negatives. Google Maps reviews require no proof of stay and can be posted by anyone</strong> — Before reading English reviews, confirm the premises of trust at that venue.</div>

<h2>Three differences — what gets praised, what stings, the purpose</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>What gets praised is "concrete action"</strong> — What English reviews praise is not the abstract "good service" but units of name and action ("Mr. So-and-so explained the ingredients of the miso soup at breakfast"). Not "the staff were wonderful" but "on the night we arrived late, they waited for us until 11 p.m." The concrete survives translation.</li>
<li><strong>What stings is "hidden costs, gaps with the photos, language"</strong> — Gaps with expectations sting. A surcharge never mentioned at check-in, a room smaller than the photos, English that did not get through. "Different from the photos" in particular is written more mercilessly than in domestic reviews.</li>
<li><strong>The purpose is "a reference for the next traveler"</strong> — While domestic reviews tend (for better and worse) to become essays addressed to the inn, English reviews are written as information for future travelers: the body runs long, and access and neighborhood details line up. What to read is not "the score" but the context of "what the next person wants to know."</li>
</ul>

<h2>Three points of reading</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Read the low ratings first</strong> — The low ratings are exactly what the next guest reads as decision material. The manners of replying to them are left to the review-replies article.</li>
<li><strong>Count the repeated keywords</strong> — When the same word (Wi-Fi, smell, noise, small) appears in three or more reviews, it is not an impression but a structural issue. It goes straight onto the inn's improvement list.</li>
<li><strong>Separate "pointed facts" from "taste"</strong> — What you can act on is the former. "The Wi-Fi is slow" is a fact (actable); "the room felt small to me" is taste (not fixable — prevented by adjusting expectations in the listing).</li>
</ol>

<h2>Putting them to work — reflecting them in the listing, and replying with quotes</h2>

<p>Repeated good words are reflected in the listing. An inn continually written about as "breakfast was wonderful" puts the breakfast photo and one line at the front of its listing. Add the evidence (measured Wi-Fi figures, the actual room size in numbers) and the words turn into trust. In replies, quote the reviewer's words and write "You mentioned X; we have done Y" (translation is fine). A reply to a quote tells not only the writer but every future traveler reading it that this is "an inn where voices arrive." The English of the reply: short sentences. Long explanations are the mouth of mistranslation and misunderstanding. Thanks, the point, one line of improvement — three lines are enough.</p>

<h2>The other side of English reviews</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Machine translation drops a few percent of meaning</strong> — Irony and idiom read backward when translated literally (as when "interesting" is not praise). For a low rating that looks serious, have the courage not to trust the translation as-is, and check with a person.</li>
<li><strong>Copying replies wholesale</strong> — A reply assembled from boilerplate on the internet is instantly visible to English-speaking readers. Writing your inn's own words in short English reaches far better.</li>
<li><strong>Arguing in English</strong> — A public rebuttal in English becomes a story read by future guests all over the world. The moment you want to argue is exactly the moment for three lines: thanks, and the facts.</li>
<li><strong>How to read culture</strong> — A review in a direct-speaking culture is "information," not "attack." Take offense at the stinging phrasing and the improvements become invisible.</li>
<li><strong>Quoting without permission</strong> — To use a line from a good English review in your listing, get the writer's permission. It is a courtesy that stays on record.</li>
<li><strong>Leaving them because they are English</strong> — An English review with no reply is read as the message "an inn that does not value overseas guests." However short, the presence of a reply carries great meaning.</li>
</ul>

<p>What to do this week is small. Take the three most recent English reviews, read them in translation, write one pointed fact onto the improvement list, and write a three-line reply to the newest one. On the far side of the language barrier is an ordinary traveler trying to choose your inn for the next weekend.</p>

<h2>1. 三个区别——赞美、感动和目的</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>赞扬的是“具体行动”——工作人员姓名/具体服务名称：</strong>入库评论中称赞的是“具体行动”。具体的行动会与名字一起得到赞扬，例如“工作人员___在办理入住时教我如何用山水煮咖啡”或“早餐时的当地蔬菜沙拉很好吃。”国内评论经常给出诸如“工作人员很好”之类的抽象赞扬，但入境评论则赞扬具体的行动。</li>
<li><strong>最突出的是“隐性成本”、“与照片的差异”和“语言”——它们与投诉文章中的四种模式重叠：</strong>入站评论中突出的三点（低评分）是“隐藏成本”、“与照片的差异”和“语言”。入境旅客特有的不满意，例如隐藏费用（例如办理入住时收取的额外费用）、与照片不符（例如实际房间与照片不同）以及语言（例如无法用英语交流），可能会导致评分较低。与投诉文章中的四种模式有很多重叠（请参阅文章）。</li>
<li><strong>目的是“给其他旅行者参考”——文字比日本的长：</strong>入境点评的目的是为其他旅客提供参考。国内点评的目的是为酒店提供反馈，入境点评的目的是为其他旅客提供参考。因此文字较长，包含很多具体信息（交通、客房、早餐、周边信息等）。</li>
</ul>

<h2>2. 如何阅读（3分）——从低评分、重复关键词开始阅读，区分事实和偏好</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>从最低评分开始阅读 — 这将帮助下一位客人做出决定（请参阅投诉文章）：</strong>从最低评分开始阅读入站评论。低评价将作为决定下一位客人的依据。通过阅读低评分的评论，您可以看到酒店需要改进的地方。对于评分较低的评论，我们将参考投诉文章并考虑回复。</li>
<li><strong>查找重复的关键字 - 如果相同的单词出现在三个或更多结果中，则这是一个结构问题：</strong>在入站评论中查找重复的关键词。如果相同的词语出现在三个或更多的回复中（例如“Wi-Fi 很慢”、“英语听不懂”、“与照片不同”等），则属于结构性问题。结构性问题需要立即解决。</li>
<li><strong>区分“指出事实”和“偏好”——前者可以这样处理：</strong>入站评论分为“指出事实”和“偏好”。我们可以对事实指示做出回应（例如，“Wi-Fi 速度很慢”、“与照片不同”等）。个人喜好（例如，“我不喜欢早餐”、“房间很小”等）很难回应。您可以通过解决事实问题来提高您的评论分数。</li>
</ol>

<h2>3. 如何使用——在Listing/Reply中引用对方的话进行反思</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>反映Listing中重复的关键词（作为证据）：</strong>反映列表中入站评论中重复的关键字。例如：通过在列表中反映“快速Wi-Fi”、“会说英语的员工”、“房间如照片”等重复关键词，下一位客人会觉得“这家旅馆值得信赖”。更新列表时，附上证据（例如 Wi-Fi 速度测量的照片）将进一步提高可靠性。</li>
<li><strong>引用对方的话来回复（“你说的，我做了……”）：</strong>在回复收到的评论时，引用对方的话。例如：“您提到 Wi-Fi 速度很慢，所以我为您更换了一个新的 Wi-Fi 路由器。”通过引用对方的话，你的客人会觉得他们的声音被听到了。</li>
<li><strong>入境评论解读为“参考其他旅行者”：</strong>入境评论被解读为“其他旅行者的参考”，而不是“对住宿的反馈”。使用入境评论作为其他旅客决定是否入住该旅馆的信息。</li>
</ul>
