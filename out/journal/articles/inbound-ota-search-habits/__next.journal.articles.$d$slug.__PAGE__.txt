11:T1f59,<p>Picture a domestic guest, and then imagine, for a moment, the scene of an overseas guest booking. What they hold is not Japanese but a search box in their own language. The journey begins from a state of knowing neither the property’s name nor the station’s. Inbound guests’ OTA search behavior differs from domestic guests’ <strong>in five points: keywords, decision period, how they read reviews, price perception, and booking timing</strong>. Understanding these differences is the first step of inbound acquisition.</p>

<h2>Difference 1 — keywords: "region + experience," not the property name — and the mother tongue</h2>

<p>Domestic guests search by "property name" and "station name," but inbound guests search by <strong>"region + experience."</strong> Place first, property after — which is why having the region’s appeal appear in the listing title and the first photo is the first condition for showing up in searches.</p>

<p>And one more easily overlooked fact. The language in which they gather travel information is not always English.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>In a Japan Tourism Agency survey (January–March 2024), "English" was the most common language in which visitors to Japan gathered travel information before departure, at 49.3% — followed by Korean at 28.6%, Traditional Chinese at 24.4%, Simplified Chinese at 14.3%, and Japanese at 12.3%, showing a thick layer of travelers who gather information in their mother tongue (multiple answers)</strong> — An English page is "the world’s common entrance," and yet English alone does not reach everyone.</div>

<p>The practical meaning of this fact is not pessimism but realism. Get the English listing in order as the majority’s entrance, while correctly including, in English, the words that ride the searches — region names, hot springs, the names of experiences. And for guests searching in their mother tongue, photographs and numbers work in their place — a design that delivers, through images and composition, what words cannot reach.</p>

<h2>Difference 2 — decision time: an average of 1.5 months ahead, but beware the "average trap"</h2>

<p>Domestic guests often start moving two to four weeks before check-in; inbound guests move a little earlier. Published data puts numbers on this.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">RESEARCH</span><strong>In an analysis published by the travel booking site Expedia for the first half of 2018, travelers to Japan booked regional accommodation an average of 47 days in advance. It differs by market: travelers from the United States about 40 days ahead; the United Kingdom, Hong Kong, and Singapore longer at 55–56 days; growth markets (Thailand, Italy, Brazil, and others) an average of 60 days. South Korea, the closest, was the shortest at under 30 days</strong> — The farther the market, the earlier the decision; the nearer, the more last-minute.</div>

<p>Two implications. Announcements aimed at distant markets work from several months before the season. And <strong>the last-minute discounting window is small in some markets</strong> — a nearby market like South Korea has thick last-minute demand, so a one-size-fits-all price-cut strategy does not fit.</p>

<h2>Difference 3 — reading reviews: the text, not the score; the low ratings, not the praise</h2>

<p>Domestic guests tend to compare by score, but many inbound guests <strong>read the review text — and read the low ratings first</strong>, as is often said. The reason is simple: staying in a land of different language and customs, they want above all to avoid failure. And what they read next is <strong>the property’s replies to the low ratings</strong>. A reply filled with excuses lowers the property’s impression more than the low rating itself. Review management is not something written — it is something read, replies included.</p>

<h2>Difference 4 — price perception: clarity of "what they are paying for," not "cheapness"</h2>

<p>For visitors to Japan, who spend much of their budget in advance on flights and lodging, a room rate whose total stays invisible to the end is the greatest anxiety. <strong>Is the total — tax, service charge, breakfast included or not — stated clearly?</strong> "Hidden costs" are a standard seed of inbound low ratings. Put the other way: a property that shows the total from the start has, in that alone, proof of honesty (the display duties of a direct site are organized in a separate article).</p>

<h2>Difference 5 — booking timing: the weekend peak, and the wall of time zones</h2>

<p>Inbound search and booking behavior is often observed to <strong>concentrate on Fridays and Saturdays</strong>, when travelers have time to plan the trip. What must not be forgotten here is the time difference. The guest’s Saturday noon can be evening in Japan, or the small hours of Sunday. In other words, <strong>the first response to a weekend inquiry occurs during the hours when Japan is asleep</strong>. An after-hours auto-reply, or a system for replying first thing the next morning, decides weekend bookings.</p>

<h2>What lies beyond the five differences</h2>

<p>Line up the five differences and one thing comes into view. Keywords, reviews, totals, responses — all of them are the work of <strong>"handing over the materials for judgment."</strong> Even without the mother tongue, line up the region’s photographs, numbers, and facts; reply honestly to low ratings; show the total; answer at midnight. A property with all of these in place becomes, beyond the wall of language, "a property with clear reasons to be chosen." The core of inbound acquisition is not making things cheap — it is building this clarity.</p>

<h2>The other side — the limits of generalities</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The average trap</strong> — "Inbound guests book early" is only half true. In data a booking site published on Kyoto (2017), bookings more than two months ahead and last-minute bookings within a week each accounted for about a quarter — a polarization was observed. Stocking water for both mountains, the early one and the near one, is the practical answer.</li>
<li><strong>Each market is a different thing</strong> — South Korea’s last-minute demand, the early bookings of Europe, America, and Australia, groups half a year ahead — round "inbound measures" into one and it misses every market. Identify your property’s main markets first.</li>
<li><strong>Check the numbers at your own property</strong> — Published data is a map of industry averages, not your property’s weather. The right answer is inside your reservation ledger.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is to count the past three months of bookings by "nationality × how many days before check-in the booking was made." Just add one column in Excel, and you can see whether your property’s inbound guests come from early markets or last-minute ones. The five differences are generalities. Only properties that replace those generalities with their own numbers turn difference into bookings.</p>

<div class="note">This article is intended for sharing ideas. The data cited is as published by each operator at the time (Expedia 2018, the booking site’s Kyoto data, and others), and markets shift. We recommend checking against your own reservation data.</div>
