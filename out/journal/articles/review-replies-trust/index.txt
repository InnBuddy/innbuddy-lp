1b:T1801,<p>At one property, this message of thanks arrived: "I read your reply, and it made me want to stay again." A review reply is not "customer service" — it is <strong>marketing addressed to future guests</strong>. This article organizes the design of stacking trust through replies, from both the numbers and the writing.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>In a survey by TripAdvisor and the research firm Ipsos (2019, about 23,000 users across 12 world markets): 89% answered that a "thoughtful reply" to a negative review improves their impression of the property. 77% that a personalized (non-boilerplate) reply makes them more likely to go ahead and book. 84% that replies should be polite and respectful — the survey itself concluded that "boilerplate should be avoided"</strong> — Replies are read, and they are being used as material for judgment.</div>

<h2>A reply has 2 readers</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Reader 1: the guest who posted it</strong> — The relationship deepens; it connects to repeat stays.</li>
<li><strong>Reader 2: the future guest who reads replies before booking</strong> — The reply is public content that directly sways the booking decision.</li>
</ul>

<p>Once written, a reply <strong>stays on the stage</strong>. Between a reply that ends at "thank you" and one that writes "here is what we did for you," the future guest’s judgment changes completely.</p>

<h2>The 4 elements of a good reply — the golden phrase is "what we did about it"</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Name + thank you</strong> — "Mr. and Ms. XX, thank you for staying with us."</li>
<li><strong>Reflecting the specific praise</strong> — Pick up and return the points the guest wrote ("So glad the window-side table pleased you"). This is an advertisement teaching future readers "this is what is good here."</li>
<li><strong>Answering the criticism, and the action taken</strong> — If there is criticism, write only the facts, and add on top "what we did about it."</li>
<li><strong>The invitation to return</strong> — "We look forward, truly, to welcoming you again."</li>
</ol>

<p>The golden phrase is <strong>"what we did about it."</strong> A reply with this written in tells the reader "this property is evolving." A property that can never write it, forever, is one that receives but does not fix.</p>

<h2>Replying to low ratings — a public record of improvement</h2>

<p>A reply to a low rating reaches future guests more than ten replies to high ratings. Do not defend. Keep the order: <strong>accept → explain (facts only) → action → invitation</strong>. And reply fast (a yardstick: within 72 hours). "Leaving it alone" is the greatest loss of points. The sorting and the operational numbers of low ratings are handled in the "Review Management" article, and the "one move" of response in "Service Recovery" — this article’s charge is <strong>the writing</strong>.</p>

<h2>Ripples into operations — the monthly 15-minute reread</h2>

<p>The work of replying itself becomes feedback into operations. Once a month, for 15 minutes, read that month’s reviews and your replies straight through — which topics dominate: cleaning, food, the front desk? The accumulated record of "what we did about it" becomes, as is, the property’s improvement history, and the evidence for the next guest. Where staff write the replies, deciding <strong>who may write and who checks</strong> in advance lets speed and quality coexist.</p>

<h2>The other side — when replying exhausts the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The temptation of the template</strong> — A "good template" with the four elements filled in starts showing through by the third use. As the survey above concluded that boilerplate should be avoided, readers notice repetition.</li>
<li><strong>The defensive reply</strong> — Surveys of the same kind have also shown that aggressive, defensive replies damage booking decisions. A reply where the excuse comes before the facts serves neither the writer nor the reader.</li>
<li><strong>Writing too much personal information</strong> — Do not write the dates of stay, room numbers, family makeup, or the purpose of the trip. A reply that identifies the individual makes future readers uneasy.</li>
<li><strong>Long text to everything</strong> — A property whose every reply is long becomes one where the reader no longer knows where to look. Replies to praise: short. Long only when there is a story of improvement to tell.</li>
<li><strong>Writing replies while the floor stays broken</strong> — A run of replies that cannot write "what we did about it" is a sign that operations have stopped. The quality of the replies is a mirror of the quality of the operations.</li>
<li><strong>Replying in English</strong> — The craft is different (see the inbound-reviews article). A reply settled with a translation app loses, by exactly as much as it got through, points with the reader.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is to reread three replies you yourself have written. What you count is the presence or absence of "what we did about it." If zero, start writing with this week’s first — trust is published in the form of replies, and stacks up little by little.</p>
