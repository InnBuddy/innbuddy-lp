1b:T1e23,<p>Heritage often sounds like something belonging to castles, temples, shrines, and museums. Yet what happens in preservation is much more down to earth: who will pass the memories of this building and these tools to the next person? Drawing on two innovations in preservation from Britain and Japan, this article considers how an inn can become a custodian of local memory.</p><h2>From three founders to five million members</h2><p>The National Trust was founded in Britain in 1895 by three people: Octavia Hill, who worked to improve housing conditions for the urban poor; lawyer Robert Hunter, who defended access to common land; and Canon Hardwicke Rawnsley, who worked to protect Lake District landscapes from railway development. The idea was simple and powerful. Beautiful and historic places should not merely be things people look at: they should be held for the nation. Buy them, then protect them forever. Its first property is said to have been five acres of coastal cliffs in Wales.</p><p>Over a century, this mechanism grew enormously. Membership was approximately 5.38 million in the 2023 reporting year. As Europe’s largest conservation charity, it cares for 250,000 hectares of farmland, 890 miles (around 1,400 kilometers) of coastline, and more than 500 historic buildings and gardens. Beatrix Potter, author of “The Tale of Peter Rabbit,” famously bequeathed her farms to the organization. The important point here is the membership model that supported preservation, rather than donations alone. Preservation is not just the work of leading experts: it is the design of relationships through which many people feel something belongs to them.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>Britain’s National Trust was founded in 1895 by a social reformer, a lawyer, and a clergyman, creating a way to protect landscapes and history through ownership for the nation</strong> — It is now Europe’s largest conservation charity, with approximately 5.38 million members. Its first property was five acres of cliffs. The essence of preservation is not simply protection, but building relationships of ownership and succession.</div><h2>A Japanese law born in the aftermath of a fire</h2><p>Japan approached the same problem from a different direction. On January 26, 1949, a fire in the Kondō, or Golden Hall, of Hōryūji Temple damaged its National Treasure wall paintings. Just over a year after that shock, the Act on Protection of Cultural Properties was enacted in 1950 through a legislative initiative by Diet members; it came into effect that August. Consolidating earlier separate laws, it is distinguished by the breadth of what it protects. Its scope extends beyond buildings and artworks to skills embedded in everyday life, including intangible folk cultural properties, and archaeological remains below ground, or buried cultural properties. In other words, Japan’s law treats cultural heritage as encompassing the whole life of a place.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>The fire at Hōryūji Temple’s Golden Hall on January 26, 1949, prompted the enactment of the Act on Protection of Cultural Properties in 1950</strong> — Its protection extends beyond buildings and artworks to folk and buried cultural properties. A broad concept of cultural heritage as the whole life of a place characterizes Japan’s law.</div><p>Both stories offer the same lesson for an inn. You do not need designation or registration to tell history. You need documentation, reflecting the spirit of Japan’s law, and relationships that enable something to be handed on as belonging to someone, reflecting the spirit of Britain’s Trust. The inn’s old doors and fittings, main structural pillar, account books, and well-used sickle are already potential cultural properties when viewed through the concept of folk cultural heritage.</p><h2>Five small tasks for passing on memory</h2><ul><li><strong>A one-page biography of the building</strong> — When was it built? Who lived here? What was repaired? Put what you know on one page and display it in the corridor. Write “unknown” where you do not know. An incomplete biography gives rise to questions.</li><li><strong>Label the tools</strong> — Add three-line labels to unused farm tools and everyday objects: their names, how they were used, and when they were acquired. A tool becomes an exhibit the moment its story is told.</li><li><strong>One more photograph from the same place</strong> — If you have even one old photograph, take a new one from the same angle and display them together. Comparing photographs is one of the least expensive and most powerful guides to history.</li><li><strong>Keep a voice in trust</strong> — Ask an older local resident for permission to record their stories around a chosen theme. A building’s memories often live in voices rather than on paper. Always obtain permission for publication.</li><li><strong>Do not erase repairs</strong> — Marks of mending, patching, and use. Preservation does not mean making something new again. Continued use is the surest form of preservation.</li></ul><h2>The responsibilities of handling memory</h2><ul><li><strong>Registered or designated properties</strong> — Changes to the existing condition of buildings registered or designated as cultural properties require notification. Check the requirements before renovation.</li><li><strong>Other people’s memories</strong> — Publishing diaries, letters, and photographs requires the agreement of the person concerned or their surviving family. Local memories can include things people do not want told.</li><li><strong>Do not exaggerate</strong> — Terms such as “samurai residence” or “hundreds of years old” need supporting evidence. A building whose history is unknown can be genuinely evocative without pretending otherwise.</li><li><strong>Difficult memories</strong> — Memories touching on local pain, such as disasters, abandoned villages, or interrupted festival traditions, are addressed in “Thinking About Dark Tourism - Ideas for Inn That Carefully Tells Memory of Region and Disaster”.
