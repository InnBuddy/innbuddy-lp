11:T1c80,<p>After checkout, back home, what the guest tells a friend is — not the summary "the service was wonderful," but <strong>a "small thing" someone did in thirty seconds</strong>. This article first confirms the psychology research that backs this phenomenon, then redesigns five touchpoints that generate word of mouth at almost zero cost.</p>

<h2>The mechanism — why "small things" get told</h2>

<p>The <strong>peak-end rule</strong>, shown by the behavioral economist Daniel Kahneman and his colleagues. People look back on an experience not by its totality or its average, but by <strong>the moment emotion moved most (the peak) and the way it ended (the end)</strong>.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">RESEARCH</span><strong>In the 1993 experiment (Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber, and Redelmeier / the journal Psychological Science): comparing the experience of holding a hand in 14°C water for 60 seconds with the experience of 60 seconds followed by 30 more seconds at a warmed 15°C, most subjects chose to repeat the "longer" one, 90 seconds in total. In the 1996 study (Redelmeier and Kahneman): recording pain in real time for 154 colonoscopy patients and 133 lithotripsy patients, what predicted the discomfort remembered afterward was "the intensity of peak pain" and "the final pain" — the length of the procedure could not predict it (duration neglect)</strong> — The memory of an experience is written not by its length but by "its high point and its ending."</div>

<p>Your property’s rating is decided not by the sum of two nights and three days but by "the finest moment" and "the moment of checkout." And what becomes the material of the "peak" is, surprisingly, the small and the unexpected. This is why "small experiences" carry leverage.</p>

<h2>The essence — the end can be remade, one more time</h2>

<p>What this research illuminates for a property’s work — the most important reading is this. <strong>The peak, in most cases, is something that "happens"; the end is something that can be "designed."</strong> In the 1996 study, making the final few minutes just slightly easier changed the memory of the whole experience. What properties with working complaint recovery are doing is exactly this — remaking the ending of the pain, one more time. Adjusting not the amount of compensation but "the feeling of completion" is the last chance to rewrite the memory of a guest who went home dissatisfied (the basics of service recovery are in that article).</p>

<h2>The 5 touchpoints</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>A handwritten welcome note</strong> — The guest’s name plus one line. Cost: one minute. If you know the purpose of the stay (an anniversary, a business trip), one more line. Being handwritten is the point. The air of "written for you" does not come through in print.</li>
<li><strong>A small regional souvenir (cost ¥300–500)</strong> — A local producer’s confection and the like — "something you cannot buy in the department store of home" is what gets talked about. Small and light (easy to carry home), with one line of the producer’s story attached — those are the conditions.</li>
<li><strong>The 30-second farewell at checkout</strong> — One line from the front desk to each guest. "It turned rainy — please come again on a clear day." "Next year’s autumn-leaf season." <strong>The last words of the stay are what get carried home.</strong></li>
<li><strong>One or two photos from the stay</strong> — Printed and handed over, or sent. Cost is nearly zero, and they become "photos the guest wants to share" (with consent; how to handle photos of individuals and children — confirm each time).</li>
<li><strong>The 30-day care email</strong> — A short line: "Thank you for staying with us. This season, we have △△ ready." It is at once a recall invitation for direct bookings and the timing for a review request (see the direct-booking article).</li>
</ol>

<p>The discipline of the design is <strong>"an upper limit of ¥300–800 per guest."</strong> A small experience is "a cost that gets remembered" — money spent on the moment within this frame comes back as reviews, word of mouth, and repeat stays. Compared with a renovation, few other investments make this calculation work. And a property that does all five finds that none of them get told. <strong>Choose two or three</strong> — what you do not choose is "the design of what not to do."</p>

<h2>What not to do — do not "flatten" the peak</h2>

<p>One more "don’t" design to add. At the moment of the peak — breakfast in the middle of a great view, the veranda after the bath, the view in the instant of entering the room — <strong>service pulls back</strong>. A "Would you like more?" cutting into the finest moment flattens the peak. For a property whose peak is silence itself, guard the silent service at peak hours at least. Before "adding" small experiences, confirm first that you are "not flattening" the peak.</p>

<h2>The other side — when small experiences exhaust the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Hollowing out through routinization</strong> — The moment it becomes "the chore of being made to write notes," the handwriting goes sloppy, and the reader can tell. Decide in advance who may make the call "today we don’t write."</li>
<li><strong>Fixing the souvenir</strong> — The same souvenir every time becomes, by the third visit, not "a surprise" but "a given." Rotate a few patterns, changing with the seasons.</li>
<li><strong>Consent for photos, and posting them</strong> — Putting the photos on the property’s social is a separate consent. Draw the line between waiting for the guest to post and the property posting.</li>
<li><strong>Email frequency</strong> — Stop at one email, 30 days out. From the second on, a monthly cap and a seasonal reason are needed (a discipline shared with the direct-booking article).</li>
<li><strong>Pushing the peak</strong> — The "we have prepared a moving experience" type is, for people who want a quiet trip, a dissatisfaction. The shape of the peak differs by guest segment — check it by reading your reviews.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is to choose one of the five. Considering your current staff and the busyness of each day, the one you can start tomorrow — at many properties, the 30-second farewell at checkout is the smallest to begin. At the moment the guest leaves, call their name and add one line of the season. The cost of rewriting memory: zero yen.</p>

<p>行为经济学家丹尼尔·卡尼曼<strong>峰终定律</strong>：对体验的评价是由“峰值（最高点）”和“结束（结束）”决定的，而不是平均值。</p>
<p>换句话说，你的住宿评级不是由7天的总和决定的，而是由“最好的时刻”和“退房的时刻”决定的。而“峰值”的材料是令人惊讶的小、意想不到的东西。这就是为什么“小经验”具有影响力。</p>
<h2>2.接触点1：手写欢迎词</h2>
<p>客人姓名 + 1 条留言。费用为1分钟。</p>
<ul>
<li>基本信息是“○○先生，我们欢迎您。请慢慢来。”</li>
<li>如果您知道您的住宿目的（周年纪念日、商务旅行等），请再添加一行。</li>
</ul>
<p>重要的是它是手写的。 “我为你写的”的氛围是印刷所无法传达的。</p>
<h2>3.接触点2：当地小纪念品（500日元以下）</h2>
<p>来自供应商生产商的当地小特产或糖果。价格为300～500日元，被称为“在家乡的百货商店买不到的伴手礼”。其要点是“小而轻”（容易带回家），并且附有制作者的故事。</p>
<h2>4.接触点3：结账时30秒告别</h2>
<p>前台在退房时对每位客人说的话。 “正在下雨，但请在晴天再来。”“明年树叶变红时见。” 30 秒规则。<strong>您住宿期间的最后一句话就是您带回家的东西</strong>。</p>
<h2>5.接触点四：入住期间的照片</h2>
<p>打印 1-2 张您入住期间拍摄的照片（或通过二维码发送）。成本几乎为零，嘉宾变成了“在SNS上发布的照片​​”——一种无偿的公关效果。 （经客人同意。）</p>
<h2>6. 接触点 5：来自您住处的关怀电子邮件（30 天后）</h2>
<p>结帐后 30 天，一封简短的电子邮件。 “感谢您使用我们的服务，我们为这个季节准备了△△，期待您的再次光临。”这同时也是一种直接预订机制（参见文章“直接预订 42%”）和请求评论的机制。</p>
