11:T1a01,<p>Modern inns have been designed to eliminate darkness through security lighting, exit signs, and night lights. These are sensible safety choices. Yet in the process, we have lost places to encounter the dark. This article considers how to design darkness as an experience rather than an accident. Switching off light and creating darkness are different things. Darkness can be designed.</p><h2>One story, one light extinguished</h2><p>On summer nights in the Edo period, people gathered indoors for hyakumonogatari, or one hundred ghost stories. One hundred andon lamps covered with blue paper were prepared, and participants took turns telling a tale. After each story, one light was extinguished. When the hundredth story ended and the last light went out, a supernatural presence was believed to appear. Gatherings are said rarely to have completed the full sequence. Some accounts trace the origins to samurai tests of courage or storytelling among otogishū, attendants who entertained a lord with tales, as discussed by Edo-period scholars of Japanese traditions. What matters here is that the practice understood how to create darkness. It arrived not suddenly, but one step at a time with each story. Extinguishing a light became part of the narrative itself: an early model for staging darkness.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Edo-period hyakumonogatari gatherings prepared one hundred blue-paper andon lamps and extinguished one after each ghost story. Few gatherings are said to have completed the full sequence</strong> — Darkness was created gradually rather than all at once. Reducing light became part of the story itself.</div><h2>In darkness, blind people become guides</h2><p>A second reference comes from modern Germany. Dialogue in the Dark emerged in Frankfurt in 1988. Former journalist Andreas Heinecke developed the idea through work supporting a colleague who had lost his sight in returning to work. The exhibition takes place in complete darkness. Participants walk, touch, and listen, guided by blind people. Those often offered assistance in a sighted environment become the guides in the dark. This reversal of roles is central to the experience. The exhibition has appeared in cities worldwide, and its organizers report millions of participants. The quality of a darkness experience depends not merely on the darkness, but on the people guiding it. The same applies to an inn’s night.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>Dialogue in the Dark, developed in Frankfurt in 1988, is an exhibition where blind guides lead participants through complete darkness; millions have experienced it worldwide, according to its organizers</strong> — In the dark, blind people guide the way. The presence of a guide, rather than darkness alone, shapes the quality of the experience.</div><h2>Creating darkness in stages at an inn</h2><ul><li><strong>An evening of ten stories</strong> — Adapt one hundred stories into ten, with ten lights, for a summer evening. Extinguish one candle after each tale. The action of extinguishing light is as central as the ghost story itself.</li><li><strong>Make dimming an event</strong> — Lights-out can mark a transition rather than an ending. “At 9:30 p.m., we change the shared-space lighting to candles.” One line can give the night another layer.</li><li><strong>A walk on a moonless night</strong> — Choose a night without moonlight and walk a dark path on the property or through the village with small lights for safe footing. Even when stars are hidden, the depth of darkness can be experienced.</li><li><strong>A story heard in the dark</strong> — Tell a story after the lights are lowered. Eyes take time to adapt; adaptation to moonlight is discussed in “Inn Enjoying Moon - Ideas for Moon Viewing, Moonrise, Moonlight Night”. Fill the first few minutes with a voice.</li><li><strong>A guide’s voice</strong> — During dark time, a staff member’s voice can act as a reassuring reference point. This is a lesson from Dialogue in the Dark. Reassurance lies within reach of the voice.</li></ul><h2>The responsibilities of handling darkness</h2><ul><li><strong>Never switch off emergency lighting</strong> — Exit signs and emergency lights are legally required life-safety equipment. Create the experience around them, not by disabling them. Clearly identify the lights exempt from any dimming event.</li><li><strong>Security and routes</strong> — Mark boundaries and provide minimum lighting underfoot on dark paths. Experiential darkness and safety lighting can serve different roles without contradiction.</li><li><strong>Fear is normal</strong> — Fear of darkness is normal for adults as well as children. The experience is an option, not a way to force people to acclimatize. Always keep a route back to a lit area available.</li><li><strong>Guests with visual impairments</strong> — Describing a space through sound and tactile clues can improve guidance for all guests. Darkness design overlaps with accessible design.</li><li><strong>Safe use of flames</strong> — Establish extinguishing tools and procedures before candle-based activities. Follow the practical fire guidance in “Night Surrounding Fire - Ideas for Gathering Night with Bonfire, Irori, Wood Stove”.</li></ul><h2>InnBuddy VIEW</h2><p>Hyakumonogatari created darkness in stages; Dialogue in the Dark placed guides within it. An inn can combine these two approaches. Start small by preparing ten lights and one ghost story for a summer night. Darkness need not be a failure or accident: with safety protected, it can be designed as deliberately as light.</p><div class="note">This article is intended for sharing ideas.
