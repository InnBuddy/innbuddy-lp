11:T1d87,<p>Build a direct site as "the inn's brochure," and what takes shape is a page people only look at, never book through. This article's starting point is more concrete: a direct site is <strong>a page where a booking completes</strong> — and in the eyes of the law, it is a store.</p>

<h2>A store called mail-order sales — two lines</h2>

<p>First line. An inn taking room reservations on its own site falls, in law, under "mail-order sales." Mail-order is not only about goods; services — lodging included — booked over the internet are covered too. And mail-order sales carry display duties: the business's name, address, and phone number; the consideration (the charges); the timing and method of payment; the timing of provision; the conditions for withdrawing or canceling the application. These are not "nice-to-have trust elements" — <strong>they are items whose display is required</strong>.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>The Act on Specified Commercial Transactions regulates the provision of services (lodging included) booked over the internet as "mail-order sales." Mail-order advertisements must display the selling price (consideration), the timing and method of payment, the timing of service provision, matters on withdrawal and cancellation of the application, and the business's name, address, and phone number (Article 11); the June 2022 amendment also established required information display on the final order-confirmation screen (Article 12-6) and rules against displays that mislead about cancellation of service contracts</strong> — That "notice under the Act on Specified Commercial Transactions" link at the bottom of e-commerce sites exists for exactly this.</div>

<p>What does this fact change about a direct site? The way you see it. <strong>Those displays are not "trust elements" but "the front desk, reproduced."</strong> At a physical property, you hang the house name at the entrance, show the rate card in the lobby, and explain the cancellation terms in person. The direct site's display duties can be read as doing that front-desk work over again, on a screen. Legal compliance becomes, as it is, the design of the guest's ease — that is the structure.</p>

<p>The second line is safety. In July 2018, the browser used by much of the world began marking every unencrypted site "Not secure." The scope had already widened in stages through 2017 — pages with input fields, and so on — and from July 2018 the marking shows on all pages in ordinary browsing. If an inn's site with a booking form carries that marking, guests leave before typing a card number.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Since July 2018 (version 68), Google Chrome has shown "Not secure" in the address bar on every page without https encryption. Before that as well, the scope had widened in stages — pages handling passwords and card information (from January 2017), pages with input fields (from October 2017)</strong> — https is not "recommended"; it is the standard whose absence brings a warning.</div>

<h2>The five items of the minimum setup</h2>

<p>With the two lines laid down, here is the minimum setup of a page where a booking completes.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Plan list — write the total</strong> — Charges, inclusions, and cancellation terms in the full total, tax and service charge included. A design where the total grows at the end only sows the seeds of low ratings.</li>
<li><strong>Taking bookings — manual is fine</strong> — A booking engine is not required. Receiving by form or phone and <strong>replying with a confirmation email within an hour</strong> fully establishes "a page where a booking completes." Engine-ize only when bookings grow past what manual handling can carry.</li>
<li><strong>Payment — even on-site payment, stated clearly</strong> — Without card payment, write "payment is made on-site." Ambiguity about the method and timing of payment is both a line under the Specified Commercial Transactions Act and a guest's anxiety.</li>
<li><strong>Business displays — the front desk, reproduced</strong> — Name, address, phone number, cancellation policy. A legal requirement, and at once a window saying this is not an inn where no one ever answers the phone.</li>
<li><strong>CTA and mobile — one entrance to booking</strong> — The "Book" and "See rooms" buttons must sit where a phone screen always shows them. A considerable share of bookings happens on smartphones.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Three common pitfalls</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The brochure that cannot be booked</strong> — Beautiful photos, yet the only booking method is "please call us." A state where the showing page and the booking page are mixed into one.</li>
<li><strong>The total saved for last</strong> — Extra charges first appear on the final booking screen. The more you surprise, the harsher the review that gets written.</li>
<li><strong>The page that opens slowly</strong> — A top page lined with heavy images loses people the moment it makes a phone wait a few seconds. Speed is part of trust.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The other side — when the direct site exhausts the inn</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Impatience over the "direct ratio"</strong> — The direct site's purpose is not to replace the OTAs but <strong>to make the second booking easy</strong>. First acquisition through OTAs; the second and onward through direct. Designed with this division, you are not dragged into desperate price wars.</li>
<li><strong>The package trap</strong> — Build transfers or arrangements for other facilities into a page selling only lodging, and the world of the Travel Agency Act peeks in. Before building them in, confirm whether registration or notification is required.</li>
<li><strong>An unupdated site is a peeling signboard</strong> — Old rates and finished plans on an unattended site carry the same effect as an old paper notice on a physical storefront. Once posted, decide the day it ends.</li>
<li><strong>Estimating the management load</strong> — A booking engine, for all its convenience, adds the work of managing inventory and prices to the inn. Introducing one while manual handling is still enough is a source of incidents and burdens.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is neither a contract nor a tool. Open the site you have (if you have one) and check the five items — yes, partly, or no. If you have none, what you need is one booking form with the phone number, the charges, and the cancellation policy written on it. A page threaded with the two lines — the law's and safety's — however small, starts working as a store.</p>

<li><strong>持续——每周一次，每次15分钟：</strong>新行动需要持续。每周留出15分钟，逐渐形成习惯，最后才会成为团队文化。</li>
</ol>
