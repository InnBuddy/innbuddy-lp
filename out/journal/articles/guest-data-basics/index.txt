1b:T2189,<p>When you stay at an inn, you are asked to write your name and address. This is not a recent system; it is part of the inn's work that has continued for 400 years. But "recording" and "using" are separate skills. This article treats guest data not as "records" but as "material for the next act of hospitality," by passing through three layers in order: history, law, and practice.</p>

<h2>The Edo inn took custody of the traveler's identity</h2>

<p>In the Edo period, commoners needed a travel permit — a proof of identity — to go on a journey. It recorded the traveler's name, address, religious sect, family temple, and purpose of travel (a hot-spring cure or a pilgrimage), and was issued by the village headman or the family temple. Some surviving permits even say: "If I die on the road, there is no need to inform my hometown — please simply perform a funeral, whatever the sect." At checkpoints, inspectors watched for "incoming guns and outgoing women," and at the honjin inns of post stations, "guest registers" recorded the stays of feudal lords. At the old family home of a honjin in Fukuroi-juku, Shizuoka, registers from 1618 to 1634 survive, showing that lords stayed 310 times over those 17 years. The shogunate further issued a directive in 1733 ordering inns and villages to have sick or injured travelers seen by a doctor, let them recuperate, and escort them home after recovery. In other words, the inn's records were a device for guaranteeing the traveler's safety. Without records, there were things that could not be protected.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Edo-period travelers carried travel permits (recording name, address, sect, and purpose of travel, issued by village headmen or family temples), and honjin inns kept "guest registers" recording the stays of feudal lords. The registers of a honjin family in Fukuroi-juku record 310 lordly stays over the 17 years from 1618 to 1634</strong> — The origin of the inn's records was not marketing but the safekeeping of the traveler's identity and safety.</div>

<h2>The modern guest register — records defined by law</h2>

<p>A direct descendant of the Edo guest register continues in modern inns: the guest register required under the regulations of the Ryokan Business Act. It must record guests' names, addresses, and contact details, and be preserved for three years (a December 2023 revision replaced the former "occupation" field with "contact details"). For foreign guests without an address in Japan, nationality and passport number must be recorded, and a copy of the passport kept for three years. Storage may be electronic, and the register must be kept at the property or its office. The role of these records is the investigation of infection routes during outbreaks of disease and the prevention of unlawful acts such as terrorism. That guest registers became the foundation of contact tracing during the recent pandemic is fresh in memory. Those few lines guests write are recorded as a requirement of society.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>The guest register under the Ryokan Business Act regulations requires recording name, address, and contact details (plus nationality, passport number, and a passport copy for foreign guests without an address in Japan) and preserving them for three years</strong> — The purpose is infection-route investigation and prevention of terrorism. Part of an inn's records is something held on behalf of society.</div>

<h2>The Personal Information Protection Act — rules for the using side</h2>

<p>Separately from legally mandated records, a different law governs the "preference notes" and "conversation records" an inn voluntarily collects: the Act on the Protection of Personal Information. The revised act, passed in June 2020, took full effect on April 1, 2022. Its main changes are restrictions on purposes of use (not using data beyond the stated purpose), duties to report incidents and to notify the individuals concerned, and stronger penalties. Particular care is needed with information related to health and medical history. Allergy records are convenient data directly useful for hospitality, but information touching medical history is treated as "special care-required personal information," and in principle cannot be obtained without the individual's consent. Before building "material for the next hospitality," get this line right. Define the purpose of use concretely, and communicate it visibly at booking or check-in. That is the basic posture under the revised act.</p>

"Special care-required personal information" concerning health and medical history cannot, in principle, be obtained without the individual's consent</strong> — The convenience of preference notes and the weight of information are different things. An inn's records are something held in trust.</div>

<h2>Practice — one sheet and three rules</h2>

<p>So what should be kept, and how? The practical basic is to consolidate everything onto a single sheet. Keep the recorded items to three: (1) basic information (name, contact, stay history), (2) preferences and requests (pillow, meals, allergies, anniversaries), (3) conversation notes (what was discussed during the last stay, staff observations). The reason for one sheet is to make searching effortless. Records split across many files do not get used.</p>

<p>Three rules govern the workflow. Record it — one line, the same day, is enough. Share it — read it aloud at the morning meeting, or keep it where everyone can see it. Use it — turn it into a single remark at the next stay. What matters most is the form of "using." The fruit of records appears as one line at the next stay: "You were here with your little one last time." "You mentioned the pillow didn't suit you, so we have prepared a replacement." Records exist for that one line. Don't let paperwork grow. All that grows is records you can never use up.</p>

<h2>The other side of records — the inn that remembers too much</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The line of discomfort</strong> — Some guests genuinely find it unsettling when small details from years ago are remembered. Use only records of topics the guest enjoyed talking about. Not keeping records of matters guests would rather not revisit is part of the craft.</li>
<li><strong>The scope of sharing</strong> — Share the sheet among all staff, but keep it hidden from outside eyes. "Visible" is a story that stays inside the inn.</li>
<li><strong>Designing deletion</strong> — Records to keep for years, or records to erase after a few months? Deciding when to delete aligns with the spirit of the law.</li>
<li><strong>Consent for email</strong> — Post-checkout direct email stands only when consent and an opt-out method are clearly stated. The details are left to the Email and CRM article.</li>
<li><strong>Data via OTAs</strong> — Reusing information obtained through booking sites is bound by each site's terms. Check the terms before mixing it with the inn's own records.</li>
</ul>

<p>The Edo guest register was written to protect the traveler. Your single sheet today originally stands in the same place: not for knowing the guest, but for the joy of the day you meet again. Start small: leave one line beside tonight's register. The moment a record turns into the next hospitality always begins with a single line.</p>
