11:T1fa1,<p>The same inn can look like a completely different inn depending on how the numbers are shown — that is what makes reviews interesting. An inn with a 4.8 score and 8 reviews, and an inn with 4.3 and 480. Which does the traveler choose? The answer changes with one more number: recency. This article is the basics of reading and running reviews through the three numbers — score, count, and recency. And at the end, a proposal for a fourth number that matters more than the third.</p>

<h2>What the research shows — reviews are material for pricing</h2>

<p>First, one study to back the weight of the numbers. In 2012, Chris Anderson of Cornell University's School of Hotel Administration published the first comprehensive quantitative analysis of the relationship between reviews and hotel revenue. Within it, from transaction data of U.S. booking sites, a one-point rise in review score on a five-point scale (say, 3.3 to 4.3) was estimated to allow a price increase of about 11.2% while maintaining the same share (the probability of being chosen). Further, matching reputation metrics with hotel revenue data, a 1% rise in reputation score was reported to relate to rises of up to 0.89% in average rate, 0.54% in occupancy, and 1.42% in RevPAR.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">RESEARCH</span><strong>The Cornell School of Hotel Administration study (Anderson, 2012) estimated that a one-point rise in review score on a five-point scale allows a price increase of about 11.2% while maintaining the same share, and reported that a 1% rise in reputation score relates to increases of up to 0.89% in average rate, 0.54% in occupancy, and 1.42% in RevPAR</strong> — A review is not merely an inn's reputation; it is material for pricing.</div>

<p>The meaning of these numbers is not "collect good reviews and gain." It is that the review score becomes part of the basis on which the inn sets its prices — the backing for why the lines of 4.5 and 4.7 are spoken of as "signals for raising rates." Note, though: this research is a correlation analysis on U.S. data, with no guarantee it applies as-is to a small Japanese inn. Read it as "reliability of direction" and "a sense of magnitude."</p>

<h2>The three numbers, one by one</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Score — the line of quality</strong> — As a working guideline, 4.5 reads as the standard line and 4.7 and above as "the line of recognized value" (a practical rule of thumb only). But what the next guest sees more than the score itself is "the replies to low ratings." The craft of those words is left to the review-replies article.</li>
<li>Count — <strong>the thickness of track record</strong> — Count is the thickness of trust. Roughly 100 reads as "proven," 500 as "thick" (again, working guidelines). Count grows only by accumulation. Push the requests, and thin reviews still make no thickness.</li>
<li><strong>Recency — proof it is still so</strong> — Travelers read the most recent reviews. An ordinary voice from last month over a rave from three years ago. Here is an interesting study. In 2011, Australian researchers (Sparks and Browning) experimentally examined the relationship between hotel reviews and booking intention, and reported that the presence of recent positive reviews can mitigate the influence of a cluster of negative ones. In other words, "recency" is one of the few weapons that can overwrite past wounds with today's voices.</li>
</ol>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">RESEARCH</span><strong>The Sparks and Browning study (2011, Tourism Management) used experiments to show that the presence of recent positive reviews can mitigate the impact of a cluster of negative reviews on booking intention</strong> — "Is there a new, good voice in the last thirty days?" has a basis in method, not just impression.</div>

<h2>The operating cycle — request, reply, prevent</h2>

<p>What turns the three numbers is the following cycle. (1) Request — right after checkout, ask for impressions (a single QR code is enough; while the satisfaction still lingers). (2) Reply to all — within 72 hours, the more carefully the lower the rating. (3) Prevent low ratings — most of them gather into three points: the gap between photos and reality, how the total price reads, and waiting times. Prevention for each has its own article.</p>

<h2>The three classes of low ratings</h2>

<p>When a low rating arrives, first sort it into three. <strong>Pointed facts</strong> (a fixture out of order, a missed spot in the cleaning): fix it, and write "we have done so" in the reply — the reply is a report to future guests, not to the past one. <strong>Differences of taste</strong> (too quiet, too rural): accept them, and supplement with facts only — do not try to fix what cannot be fixed. <strong>Safety and hygiene points</strong> call for a formal apology and a report of the response; that is the territory of the service recovery article.</p>

<h2>The fourth number — the reply rate</h2>

<p>Finally, a proposal. Score, count, recency — all three are numbers the guests make. The inn cannot move them directly. But there is one more number that only the inn can make: the reply rate. Of all reviews, what share has the inn replied to? This number alone can be brought to 100% starting today. And a reply is the inn's statement to every traveler who reads next. An inn at 4.3 with a 100% reply rate is trusted more than an inn at 4.8 left unattended — not an exaggeration, but a natural consequence of replies being "content that gets read."</p>

<h2>The other side of review management</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Pushing the request</strong> — Do not turn the satisfaction of checkout into a collection point for numbers. Ask once, with a smile. "We would be glad if you wrote one" is the limit.</li>
<li><strong>Fake reviews and rewards</strong> — Rewarding reviews or writing your own violates the terms of the booking sites and the map alike. The way trust is lost when it comes to light compares to nothing a single low rating can do.</li>
<li><strong>Long excuses to low ratings</strong> — An excuse is read not as an explanation to future guests but as the record of a quarrel with a past one. Three lines are enough for a reply.</li>
<li><strong>Competing on count</strong> — Count is a result, not a target. Is the inversion — shaving the comfort of the stay to grow the count — happening anywhere?</li>
<li><strong>Management that watches only the score</strong> — Even a 4.8 inn collapses on recency if the good reviews stopped a year ago. Always read the three numbers together, all three.</li>
<li><strong>Dependence on the numbers</strong> — The studies introduced are correlation analyses, not guarantees of effect. Numbers are tools; the substance of an inn is the experience of staying.</li>
</ul>

<p>What to do this month: write out the three numbers. Score, count, the voices of the last thirty days. No calculator needed — ten minutes with the review page open. And if the replies are not yet written, write three lines to the newest review. The fourth number becomes 100% today, starting there.</p>

<div class="note">This article is intended for sharing ideas. The studies cited are analyses based on U.S. and Australian data and do not guarantee effects for Japanese inns. The numeric guidelines (4.5, 100 reviews, 72 hours, and so on) are practical reference values.</div>
