11:T224d,<p>Among the elements of Japanese culture, the onsen (hot spring) is at the top of the list of “things hard to explain in words.” Washing your body at the washing area, the bath being for everyone, not putting your towel in the water — to people who grew up in Japan these are all part of the air, needing no explanation, but for first-timers they are hard even to imagine. That is exactly why onsen guidance is the place where the true power of “pictures” is tested most.</p><h2>2,839 hot-spring areas: simply “being there” is not enough to be chosen</h2><p>According to the Ministry of the Environment’s “Hot Spring Use Status,” as of the end of March 2025, there were 2,839 hot-spring areas nationwide and 27,899 hot-spring sources in total. The number of hot-spring areas has fallen for six consecutive years since fiscal 2019, a decrease of 132 over those six years. Oita Prefecture has the most sources, at 5,094, and is also first in the nation in flow volume. The fact that the numbers are slowly falling can be reread this way for an inn. An onsen is not chosen simply because it is “there.” The quality of each hot-spring area’s explanation and experience will make the difference from now on. Picture guidance is the most reliable investment in that.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>2,839 hot-spring areas and 27,899 hot-spring sources in total (as of the end of March 2025, Ministry of the Environment, “Hot Spring Use Status”)</strong> — The number of hot-spring areas has fallen for six consecutive years since fiscal 2019. Oita Prefecture’s 5,094 sources are the most in the nation.</div><h2>Baths with the sequence carved into the architecture — the Roman thermae</h2><p>Bathing in the public baths (thermae) of Rome 2,000 years ago was a series of steps. Undress in the apodyterium (changing room), exercise in the palaestra (exercise ground), get used to the heat in the tepidarium (warm room), warm up properly in the caldarium (hot room), and finish in the frigidarium (cold room). This order is carved, as it is, into the layout of the rooms in the bath buildings. They were built so that bathers passed through the correct sequence simply by walking along the corridors.</p><p>Why is this story interesting? Because even without a single sign in a huge bathhouse, the building itself was the sign. The essence is that “explanation is the design of circulation.” The same can be said of Japanese onsen. Where you take off your shoes → the changing room → the washing area → the bath. Place a picture at each point along this route, and the building will carry the explanation for you. Merely hanging one “picture guide board” on a wall does not convey the order. Place them so that guests encounter them in sequence as they move forward. This is the heart of the design.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>In the Roman thermae, the bathing sequence (undressing → preparation → warm bath → hot bath → cold bath) was carved into the architecture as the layout of the rooms</strong> — Even without signs, the route itself was the guidance. Explanation is the design of circulation.</div><h2>The craft of conveying with pictures was born in Tokyo</h2><p>The international lineage of “conveying with pictures without relying on words” lies in an unexpected place: the 1964 Tokyo Olympics. At these Games, signs made of picture symbols, “pictograms,” were introduced in earnest for the first time at an Olympics. Under the direction of Katsumi Masaru and others, young creators made 39 types of picture symbols for facility guidance, and it is said they even gave up their copyright to prioritize their spread. From then on, pictograms spread to the Olympics from the Mexico City Games onward, to world expos, and to airports around the world, and the world standard for the men’s and women’s restroom symbols was also born from this current.</p><p>In other words, “conveying with pictures” is an invention Japan brought to the international community. Onsen picture guides lie on the extension of that invention. Pictures are not translation but a language that has already become a world standard.</p><h2>The core of the manners — the bath is “everyone’s water”</h2><p>Laid out concretely, the sequence to put into pictures is this. ① Take off your shoes ② Undress in the changing room (take one towel with you) ③ Wash your body at the washing area ④ Pour hot water over yourself (kakeyu) to get your body used to the water ⑤ Get into the bath (put the towel on your head or outside the bath) ⑥ After getting out, dry your body before going into the changing room. Of these six pictures, the essence most worth conveying is the relationship between ③ and ⑤. The bath of a Japanese onsen is “everyone’s water,” entered with a washed body. The washing area and the bath are not separate rooms but are connected by “order” — if this gets across through pictures, the manners are understood not as rules but as logic.</p><p>Also decide what to convey with numbers. The temperature of the source (e.g., 42°C), the name of the spring type, the pH, and whether it is “gensen kakenagashi” (free-flowing from the source) or “recirculated.” The smell of sulfur is part of “the water’s instruction manual.” Water that can be drunk is limited — add a line saying that if drinking is possible, it is only at places displaying “safe to drink.”</p><h2>Explain tattoos with a single card</h2><p>The topic of tattoos is unavoidable in bathing by overseas guests. In March 2016, the Japan Tourism Agency issued “Points to Note and Examples of Responses Regarding Bathing by Foreign Travelers with Tattoos,” giving examples such as covering them with stickers and responding with hygienic bathing wear. Responses differ by facility: there are inns that state their rules clearly, inns where guests can bathe with cover stickers, and inns that guide guests to private baths. What matters, whatever the choice, is “explaining.” Guests who do not understand the rules leave silently before causing trouble.
