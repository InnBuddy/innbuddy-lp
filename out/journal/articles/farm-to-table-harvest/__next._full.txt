We look at the reason in the research of psychology and the nature of vegetables.</p>

<h2>What you make yourself is five times dearer</h2>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">RESEARCH</span><strong>The "IKEA effect" — experiments have shown the tendency to overvalue what one has poured labor into</strong> — In the Harvard Business School experiments of Norton and colleagues, the folds of amateur paper cranes were valued by their own folders at roughly five times the amount they would pay for identical cranes folded by others. Experiments showing people attaching high value to furniture they assembled themselves are also known (per Norton, Mochon, and Ariely, 2012).</div>

<p>Why it is interesting: <strong>the vegetables a guest picks themselves rise in value over bought vegetables</strong> — that is the ground of the harvest experience, legible in the flow of the experiments. But this research carries one more important condition: for participants who could not finish the task, the effect vanished. <strong>Labor becomes love only when it is "completed."</strong> So the harvest experience must be a design that absolutely completes — one crop, fifteen minutes, chosen so there is no failing.</p>

<h2>After the harvest, the clock is already running</h2>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Sweet corn, at room temperature, begins losing sugar 5–6 hours after harvest</strong> — After harvest, respiration continues consuming the sugar, converting it to starch (per seed-company growing manuals and similar). "Fresh-picked is sweetest" is not a feeling but a matter of time.</div>

<p>The other ground of "eating by the field" is here. The difference in taste is a matter not of distance but <strong>of time</strong>. That morning-picked taste beside the field differs from store-bought is a difference that follows, naturally, as a consequence. A property that can explain this one line needs not even speak the meaning of the experience.</p>

<h2>The story of a plate — give the field a name</h2>

<p>The close of the experience is the one line of serving. <strong>"These ○○ were picked this morning in the field."</strong> And instead of the producer, try using <strong>the field’s name</strong> — "tomatoes from the south field," "potatoes from the mountainside field." Give the field a name, and the story gains a subject. The day a guest says "I want to go back to the south field," the story is complete.</p>

<h2>Pick, wash, cut — 3 hands</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Pick (15 minutes)</strong> — To the field together with the guest. Teach one thing only: how to use the shears, or one sign of ripeness. Not "any is fine" but "the ones that have turned red" — hand over one criterion, and the act of picking gains weight in the hands.</li>
<li><strong>Wash</strong> — The time of rinsing off the soil is, in fact, the climax of the experience: the first moment the field’s things turn into ingredients. Prepare one washing place, and let this too be done with their own hands.</li>
<li><strong>Cut and lay out</strong> — In the kitchen (or the experience room), the harvest goes straight into a salad. No elaborate cooking — the manner is to deliver the taste of what was picked by the shortest distance possible.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Operation — as a one-couple-a-day experience</h2>

<p>Serve "harvest experience + breakfast" to one couple a day. Start at the first hour of morning, when the crop is at its best (the usual reason for morning picking is handled by <a href="#idea-farm-one-day">Experience One Day in Field - Ideas for Enjoying Morning Harvest, Daytime Cooking, Evening Table in One Day</a>). Keep the photography to one frame — "the field, the hands, the fruit" — the hand at the moment of picking carries more story than any plated photograph (the craft of presenting a plate can borrow from <a href="#transparent-tomato-presentation">Seasonal: The Presentation Power of a Single Transparent Tomato</a>).</p>

<h2>The other side — 5 lines of the harvest experience</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Over-picking</strong> — Set a limit: "three per person." A limit does not cheapen the experience; it raises it (things capped in number become scarce).</li>
<li><strong>Pesticides and raw eating</strong> — Crops for the harvest experience must be managed on the assumption they are eaten raw. Whatever enters the mouth is designed through to the washing stage.</li>
<li><strong>Confirming a borrowed field</strong> — Borrowing a farmer’s field: clarify crop damage, insurance, and the bounds of entry in advance. This confirmation works longer than the experience’s hour.</li>
<li><strong>Weather</strong> — A rainy field is hard to enter. Decide in one line the previous-day criterion and the alternative way to spend the time.</li>
<li><strong>Allergies and clothing</strong> — Reactions to pollen or particular crops; the shoes and clothes for entering a field. Add one line of guidance at booking confirmation.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is choosing the one crop that can be harvested. Tomatoes, potatoes, herbs — one protagonist of the season. When that one crop bears the name "the south field," and the fifteen minutes and the one line of serving are decided, the experience is already moving. Guests go home remembering the taste of the plate they picked themselves a little richer than it actually was — a property of things the designer of the experience should know.</p>

Please design crop selection, pesticide management, and land-use terms for the harvest experience after confirming with the authorities and the farmers.</div>
