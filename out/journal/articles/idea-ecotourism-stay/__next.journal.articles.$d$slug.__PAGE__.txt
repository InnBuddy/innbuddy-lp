11:T1e17,<p>The root of “eco” is the Greek oikos — “house.” Ecology and economy both start from the same word, “house.” In other words, ecotourism means that enjoying nature without destroying it and making a living from nature live in a single house. This article is about bringing that balance down to small experiences at an inn.</p><h2>The word was born in Mexico in 1983</h2><p>The word “ecotourism” is said to have been coined in July 1983 by the Mexican environmental architect Héctor Ceballos-Lascuráin. He was working to protect wetlands on the Yucatán Peninsula, and used the word for a form of travel to natural areas in order to “study, admire, and enjoy” them. In 1990, The International Ecotourism Society (TIES) was founded, and the definition often cited today was set out: “responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment and improves the well-being of local people.” What deserves attention is that two-thirds of this definition is not about “the environment” but about “conservation and the local community.” The core of ecotourism is not nature but the system that protects it.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>The word “ecotourism” was coined in 1983 by Mexico’s Héctor Ceballos-Lascuráin, and The International Ecotourism Society (TIES), founded in 1990, defined it as “responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment and improves the well-being of local people”</strong> — The heart of the definition is not “nature to enjoy” but “a system that protects it and reaches the local community.”</div><h2>In 2007, Japan made a law that “limits” tourism</h2><p>Japan has its own path as well. In June 2007, the Ecotourism Promotion Act (Act No. 105 of 2007) was enacted, and it came into force in April 2008. What the law sets out is the sustainable protection of natural tourism resources and cooperation among the region’s diverse actors. And the host side is asked for measures that limit the number of visitors and the areas they enter, and even for training specialist guides. It is a law for promoting tourism, yet its content is “measures for not increasing.” This paradox is the very essence of ecotourism, and the same question arises when an inn designs an experience: is this experience an amount this land can afford to increase?</p><h2>An island protected by the world’s strictest “rules for visiting”</h2><p>The extreme of such a system is the Galápagos Islands. About 97% of the islands’ land area is national park. For tourists to walk within the national park area, they are required to be accompanied by a certified naturalist guide trained and licensed by the national park service, and one guide is said to be able to lead at most 16 people. The only things you can take away are photos; you cannot take home so much as a single stone. Distance from animals, not touching, strictly keeping to routes. Chosen in 1978 as one of the first 12 World Heritage Sites, these islands have protected their nature by “not letting people walk wherever they like,” and have sustained the local community through the occupation of guide and the revenue of entrance fees. It is a place where the definition of ecotourism — protect, learn, reach the local community — became a system as it is.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>About 97% of the Galápagos Islands’ land area is national park; being accompanied by a certified naturalist guide is mandatory (up to 16 people per guide), and the only thing you can take away is photos</strong> — The extreme of a system in which “nature remains because there are guides.” The three conditions — protect, learn, reach the local community — are realized as a system.</div><p>What a small inn can learn from the Galápagos is the weight of the presence of a guide. A place where people are guided preserves both nature and the quality of the experience better than a place where people walk freely. If the innkeeper can name ten of the land’s birds and wildflowers, that is already a kind of qualification.</p><h2>Small ecotourism within the inn</h2><ul><li><strong>A “this week you can see” board</strong> — Update three lines every week: flowers in bloom now, birds you can hear now, stars you can see now. Freshness is the life of nature guidance.</li><li><strong>An observation list</strong> — Place a single checklist of birds, insects, flowers, stars, and sounds. For children it becomes a treasure hunt; adults become observers.</li><li><strong>Lending binoculars and field guides</strong> — When the tools change, the world you can see changes. Choose regional field guides. Rather than a guide to the world, “one book for this land.”</li><li><strong>The inn’s observation notebook</strong> — A notebook in which guests write down what they found. The previous guest’s record becomes the next guest’s guide.</li><li><strong>A 15-minute observation session a day</strong> — In the morning, count birds in front of the inn for just 15 minutes. Rather than a long tour, a smallness you can keep up is ecotourism.</li></ul><h2>The responsibility of those who trade on nature</h2><ul><li><strong>Laws on collecting</strong> — In national parks, quasi-national parks, and prefectural nature conservation areas, collecting plants and animals is prohibited by law. “The only thing you may take is photos” is a manner to keep not only in the Galápagos.</li><li><strong>Don’t feed</strong> — Feeding birds or fish shortens their lives. Do not call the subjects of observation with food.</li><li><strong>The rarer it is, the less you write its location</strong> — Writing the location of precious flowers or creatures in detail on social media can invite poaching and overharvesting. Keep the record, but blur the location.</li><li><strong>The boundary of paid guiding</strong> — If you offer the inn’s experience as “guiding,” check travel-agency registration and the division of roles with local guides.</li><li><strong>Don’t let it be all signboard</strong> — Once you call yourself “eco,” the reality of your detergents, wastewater, and garbage is called into question. If words run ahead, trust collapses later.</li></ul><h2>InnBuddy VIEW</h2><p>The word “ecotourism” was born in 1983, Japan set out “tourism that limits” by law in 2007, and the Galápagos protects its islands with certified guides alone.
