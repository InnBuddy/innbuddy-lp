1b:T1c99,<p>The word “wellness” has taken on a life of its own, and many inns have convinced themselves that “we are not a wellness place.” But go back to the definition, and wellness tourism means “travel that pursues the maintenance or enhancement of personal health.” No new facilities or yoga instructors are needed. Redesign the morning, walking, and bathing times the inn already has as “times to restore balance,” and it has begun.</p><h2>Most guests are “people who want to look after their health while traveling”</h2><p>The Global Wellness Institute (GWI), which measures the market, divides wellness travelers into two types: “primary” travelers, whose main purpose is wellness itself, and “secondary” travelers, who want to maintain their health or take part in experiences while traveling. The market nearly doubled in ten years and is estimated at about 890 billion dollars in 2024 (as of writing). What deserves attention is that most of it is supported by secondary travelers. In other words, guests are not “traveling for wellness” but thinking, “I want to restore my balance along the way.” If so, the inn’s job is not selling programs but preparing “times to restore balance” within the stay.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>The Global Wellness Institute’s definition is “travel associated with the pursuit of maintaining or enhancing one’s personal well-being.” Travelers divide into a main-purpose type (primary) and a maintenance/participation type (secondary)</strong> — The market nearly doubled in ten years, to about 890 billion dollars in 2024 (GWI estimate, as of writing). Most are “secondary” travelers who want to restore their balance while traveling.</div><h2>The country where the sauna became cultural heritage</h2><p>On December 17, 2020, Finland’s sauna culture was inscribed on UNESCO’s Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage. It was Finland’s first intangible cultural heritage. A country of about 5.5 million people has about 3 million saunas, and nearly 90% of the population is said to take a sauna at least once a week. The reason for the inscription is that the sauna is not bathing equipment but a “living culture” of family time, reflection, and conversation. The word “sauna” itself is a Finnish word exported to the world.</p><p>This story overlaps directly with Japan’s culture of bathing. Onsen hot springs, sentō public baths, and tōji therapeutic bathing stays were, in Japan, not “facilities” but “culture.” For overseas guests, the inn’s bath is one of the most cross-cultural experiences of all. Yet the guidance tends to read “source 44°C / indoor bath / open-air bath.” Rewrite the bath guidance in the words of culture — “Kakeyu, pouring hot water over yourself, is a manner that protects both body and bath”; “After bathing, please allow 30 minutes for this time of cooling down” — and that alone puts the inn’s bathing at the center of wellness.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>Finland’s sauna culture was inscribed on UNESCO’s Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage on December 17, 2020 (Finland’s first)</strong> — About 3 million saunas for a population of about 5.5 million, with nearly 90% of people taking one at least weekly. An example of the world recognizing that bathing is not a “facility” but “culture.”</div><h2>The morning set of three — breathing, walking, bathing</h2><ul><li><strong>Five minutes of breathing</strong> — Just specify the time and place: “6:30 a.m., five minutes of deep breathing on the paddy ridge.” Don’t make it a program. All you write on the card is the time, the place, and a word.</li><li><strong>Fifteen minutes of walking</strong> — Walk within the 15-minute zone around the inn (the method of the article “Convey Region with Walk - Ideas for 15, 30, 60 Minute Walks Starting from Inn”). The morning light and dew are the protagonists of the time to restore balance.</li><li><strong>The order of bathing</strong> — A recommendation for a morning bath. Wake up with hot water, or ease the body awake with lukewarm water? Simply writing the options for the order of baths turns bathing into “a culture with procedures.”</li></ul><p>What ties these together is a three-line “how to spend this morning” card. Time, place, and a word. The quality of wellness lies not in additional facilities but in the precision of the suggestion.</p><h2>The lines to draw when selling restoration</h2><ul><li><strong>Don’t use medical language</strong> — “Detox,” “improves circulation,” and “effective for fatigue recovery” are claims of medical efficacy. Statements of hot-spring efficacy belong to the domain governed by the Hot Springs Act. For everything else, the etiquette is to keep a distance: “Please enjoy it as your own experience.”</li><li><strong>The variety of health conditions</strong> — Chronic illness, pregnancy, high blood pressure, after drinking alcohol. There are always people who cannot use hot baths or saunas. Give guidance on lukewarm-bath times and rest areas.</li><li><strong>Preserving freedom</strong> — Do not force “restoration.” For some people, being woken at 6:30 a.m. is itself stressful. Greet those who take part and those who don’t with the same face. That is the etiquette of wellness.</li><li><strong>Scent and temperature</strong> — Strong-smelling bath additives and high temperatures are a burden for those who dislike them. Offer fragrance-free options and display temperatures.</li><li><strong>Don’t extend it</strong> — Times to restore balance can be short. Five minutes of breathing, fifteen minutes of walking. Long programs disappear first at the inns that cannot keep them up.</li></ul><h2>InnBuddy VIEW</h2><p>Most wellness travelers are not on a special trip but are “people who want to restore their balance while traveling.” The essence of the country that made the sauna cultural heritage was treating bathing as culture rather than a facility. Start small, by writing a three-line “how to spend this morning” card. Breathing, walking, bathing — the three times the inn already has are the starting point as they are.</p><div class="note">This article is intended for sharing ideas.
