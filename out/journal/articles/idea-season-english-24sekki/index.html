二十四節気は、ユネスコに登録された「時間の知識」です

二十四節気の起源は古代中国にあります。戦国時代から漢代（紀元前2世紀頃）にかけて、黄河流域の気候を基準に完成しました。仕組みは明快で、太陽の道筋（黄道）を15度ずつ24に分け、太陽の位置で季節を刻むものです。月の満ち欠けで作る暦では季節がずれてしまうため、そのずれを補う「太陽側の目盛り」として生まれました。月ではなく太陽基準――つまり二十四節気は、実質的には精密な太陽暦の骨格です。2016年11月30日、「二十四節気」は中国の申請によってユネスコ無形文化遺産（人類の無形文化遺産の代表一覧）に登録されました。世界の気象関係者の間で「中国の第五の大発明」とも評される知識体系です。2022年の北京五輪では、開会式のカウントダウンに二十四節気が使われました。

七十二候は、日本で書き換えられました

二十四節気をさらに約5日ずつ三分割したのが七十二候です。初候・次候・末候。たとえば立春の初候は「東風解凍（春風が氷を解かす）」。ここまでは中国から来た暦ですが、日本に伝わった暦には、当然、中国の黄河の気候が書き込まれていました。日本の季節とは合いません。そこで江戸時代、日本初の国産暦「貞享暦」（1685年施行）を作った天文暦学者の渋川春海が、七十二候を日本の気候と生き物に合わせて改訂したのです。春分の一候は、中国式の「玄鳥至（つばめ来る）」に対して、日本式は「雀始巣（すずめが巣を作る）」「桜始開（桜が咲き始める）」。今の形は明治7年（1874年）の略本暦でさらに整えられました。

Risshun, Keichitsu, Bōshu, Hakuro, Tōji. When you hear “convey the names of the 24 solar terms (nijūshi sekki) in English,” the first urge is to make “a glossary.” But a glossary is the way to make seasonal greetings as dull as possible. This article starts with two facts worth knowing before any translation: that this calendar is knowledge of world-heritage caliber, and that it is a calendar rewritten after it came to Japan.

The 24 solar terms are “knowledge of time” inscribed by UNESCO

The 24 solar terms originated in ancient China. They were completed between the Warring States period and the Han dynasty (around the 2nd century BC), based on the climate of the Yellow River basin. The mechanism is clear: the sun’s path (the ecliptic) is divided into 24 segments of 15 degrees each, and the seasons are marked by the sun’s position. Because a calendar based on the waxing and waning of the moon drifts out of step with the seasons, the terms were born as “a scale on the sun’s side” to correct that drift. Based not on the moon but on the sun — in other words, the 24 solar terms are in effect the skeleton of a precise solar calendar. On November 30, 2016, “The Twenty-Four Solar Terms” was inscribed on UNESCO’s Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity on China’s nomination. It is a body of knowledge that some in meteorological circles around the world have even called “China’s fifth great invention.” At the 2022 Beijing Olympics, the 24 solar terms were used in the opening ceremony countdown.

FACTThe 24 solar terms are a sun-based calendar dividing the ecliptic into 24 segments of 15 degrees each, and were inscribed as UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2016 — Completed in the Yellow River basin of ancient China, they are recognized worldwide as “knowledge of time” that corrects the seasonal drift of a lunar calendar.

The 72 microseasons were rewritten in Japan

Dividing each of the 24 solar terms further into three parts of about five days each gives the 72 microseasons (shichijūni kō): the first, second, and last kō. For example, the first kō of Risshun is “Tōfū kōri wo toku” (the east wind melts the ice). Up to this point it is a calendar that came from China, and the calendar that reached Japan naturally had the climate of China’s Yellow River written into it. It did not fit Japan’s seasons. So in the Edo period, the astronomer and calendar scholar Shibukawa Harumi, who made Japan’s first domestically produced calendar, the “Jōkyō calendar” (in force from 1685), revised the 72 microseasons to fit Japan’s climate and living things. For the first kō of the spring equinox, against the Chinese “Genchō itaru” (the swallows arrive), the Japanese version has “Suzume hajimete sukuu” (sparrows begin to nest) and “Sakura hajimete saku” (cherry blossoms begin to bloom). The present form was further refined in the abridged calendar of Meiji 7 (1874).

CASEWith the Jōkyō calendar, Shibukawa Harumi rewrote the 72 microseasons into Japan’s living things (“the swallows arrive” → “cherry blossoms begin to bloom”) — A pioneer of calendar localization, rewriting an imported calendar to fit his own country’s climate. For more than 300 years it has taught us that seasonal words should be not a “literal translation” but “a translation into the land.”

This fact changes the design of an inn’s seasonal cards. What to hand over is not a glossary of the 24 solar terms but “today in our own land.” Risshun (around Feb 4) — the calendar says spring begins today. Add a photo of the plum buds in our garden to this one line. What Shibukawa Harumi did — the work of rewriting the Chinese calendar into Japan’s cherry blossoms — the inn now does in the words of its own land.

The word “climate” itself is a fusion of this calendar

In the world of the calendar, an etymological explanation has been handed down that reads the “ki” of the 24 sekki and the “kō” of the 72 kō together as “kikō” (climate). Twenty-four ki and seventy-two kō. The scale of the heavens, and the movements of living things that fill the gaps between. Tell guests that these two calendars sleep inside the word “kikō” that we use in weather forecasts today, and each English card becomes a piece of linguistic archaeology.

How to make the calendar — sell not the translation but the calendar’s gap

  • Today’s kō on one card — “Today is the first kō of Risshun, Tōfū kōri wo toku: the season when the spring wind begins to melt the ice.” Three lines — Japanese, reading, and English — are enough.
  • Explain the gap between calendar and feeling — Risshun falls in midwinter. This gap is precisely the seed of the story. The pleasure of filling the space between “the calendar’s spring” and “the spring you feel” with observations of the land is universal.
  • Make our land’s own 72 microseasons — Following the example of Shibukawa Harumi, write out the kō of our garden, fields, and mountains. What is our “sparrows begin to nest”? That becomes a calendar only this inn has.
  • A seasonal walking map — Pin the flowers blooming and the birds singing in the current kō onto the walking course. The card turns into a walk.
  • What not to do — Do not make a finished set of all 24 solar terms × 72 microseasons. Start with today’s single card. Seasonal cards have meaning in “continuing.”

Points of caution

  • Dates move — The dates of the solar terms shift slightly from year to year. It is safe to write them as an approximation, as in “around Feb 4.”
  • Don’t leave machine translation as is — Have the terms and English translations checked once by someone who knows English. For an investment of only printing costs, it becomes a permanent asset.
  • Get the origin right — The 24 solar terms are a calendar born in China. Do not introduce them as Japan’s own unique culture. On that basis, the story of their being “rewritten in Japan” is, if anything, interesting as a history of exchange.
  • For children — Even if the kanji of the kō are hard to read, their meanings become pictures. “The season when sparrows begin to nest” is a seasonal word you can draw with children.

Conveying seasonal greetings in English is not making a glossary but putting the observations of our own land into a world language. Beyond these two facts lies “the third version of the 72 microseasons” that an inn writes. Start by putting today’s kō on a single card.
