11:T1e9e,<p>Contents tourism can sound like something belonging elsewhere, such as pilgrimages to places associated with anime. Yet in Japan, creating tourism around local stories has existed for more than a century. One of its earliest successes is “The Legends of Tōno,” from Tōno in Iwate Prefecture. Through two examples from different eras — Tōno and anime pilgrimage sites — this article considers what it means for an inn to record the stories of its own place.</p><h2>A century ago, oral accounts created a town’s heritage</h2><p>In June 1910, Kunio Yanagita published “The Legends of Tōno,” or Tōno Monogatari. It brought together strange stories passed down in the Tōno area, told to Yanagita by Tōno native Kizen Sasaki, whose pen name was Kyōseki: kappa, zashiki-warashi household spirits, mountain deities, and Oshira-sama. The book collected 119 accounts in a restrained style. In his preface, Yanagita expressed the wish that these tales be told to make the people of the plains shudder — not merely as frightening rural stories, but as a record of Japanese folklore.</p><p>This one book shaped a century for Tōno. Kappa-buchi, the pool behind Jōkenji Temple, remains a local attraction. Tourist accounts describe Tōno’s kappa as red-faced, unlike those elsewhere, while Kizen Sasaki is called “Japan’s Grimm.” People continue to visit places associated with the book. What matters is that Tōno made stories a resource rather than relying on a grand castle or famous hot spring. The act of putting oral accounts on paper became part of a town’s industry over a century.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>“The Legends of Tōno” (1910) is a collection of 119 folk accounts recorded by Kunio Yanagita from Tōno native Kizen Sasaki, and has remained central to the town’s tourism resources for more than a century</strong> — Stories such as those of Kappa-buchi and zashiki-warashi became the town’s signature, rather than a castle or hot spring. Recording stories can create an industry.</div><h2>Modern pilgrimage sites are selected with fan votes</h2><p>Anime pilgrimages are a modern version of the same structure. In August 2017, the Anime Tourism Association made its first selection of “88 Japanese Anime Spots to Visit,” the 2018 edition, drawing on votes from anime fans around the world. The list included Hida City in Gifu Prefecture and Shinjuku in Tokyo, associated with the film “Your Name.” Its first president was anime director Yoshiyuki Tomino. Publishers, travel companies, and airlines are represented on its board, making it a mechanism for translating stories into economic activity. Tomino has said that he wants people to use anime as a starting point for learning broadly about a place, and that congestion at pilgrimage sites also needs consideration. Stories bring people; hosts then think about guidance to the wider area and managing crowds. The structure is the same as in Tōno.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>In August 2017, the Anime Tourism Association first selected its “88 Japanese Anime Spots to Visit,” drawing on votes from fans worldwide</strong> — Hida City and Shinjuku, associated with “Your Name,” were among those selected. It operates as a modern structure linking story → place → pilgrimage → local economy.</div><h2>Tōno and anime sites teach the same lesson</h2><p>Placed side by side, the two examples reveal a shared principle. When a story is situated in a place, it can create a pilgrimage. People visit Tōno’s Kappa-buchi and an anime-associated stairway because they are settings for stories. Sustaining that interest requires two efforts from hosts: preserve the story accurately, and guide pilgrims to the wider region. That is where an inn has work to do.</p><h2>An inn becomes the first reader of its local stories</h2><ul><li><strong>Collect local folktales in one booklet</strong> — The origins of a small shrine, a legend about a river pool, the name of a stone in a field. Ask older local residents and make a small booklet. In essence, this was how “The Legends of Tōno” began.</li><li><strong>Read one story each evening</strong> — Share one local story after dinner or before bed: a bedtime tale for children, something to accompany a drink for adults. Providing a setting for storytelling belongs naturally to hospitality.</li><li><strong>Draw a story map</strong> — On a picture map, using the approach in “Draw Local Map with Pictures - Ideas for Conveying Region with Illustrated Map”, mark where local stories took place. A map with a line saying “Here, this happened” can turn a walk into a pilgrimage.</li><li><strong>If the area is an anime or film setting</strong> — Include etiquette for visiting the sites: do not enter private property, do not photograph residents’ private lives, and take litter home. An inn’s quality in such a place is reflected in its guidance on respect.</li><li><strong>A notebook of stories from our places</strong> — Leave a notebook where guests can record similar tales they heard as children. When an inn becomes a place for collecting oral accounts, the stories keep growing.</li></ul><h2>The responsibilities of handling stories</h2><ul><li><strong>Local traditions belong to the community</strong> — Obtain the storyteller’s agreement before publishing recorded accounts. Agree with the community on how to handle stories connected to festivals or beliefs.</li><li><strong>Separate invention from tradition</strong> — If the inn embellishes a folktale for entertainment, clearly label it as a new creation. Stories close to entertainment can require especially careful distance.</li><li><strong>The burden on pilgrimage sites</strong> — More pilgrims can erode residents’ peace and quiet. Tomino’s concern about congestion is also an issue for inns.</li><li><strong>Copyright and likeness rights</strong> — Commercial use of anime images or titles by an inn requires permission from the rights holders.</li></ul><h2>InnBuddy VIEW</h2><p>Tōno built a century of tourism around one book of oral accounts, while anime sites are selected with fan votes and become economic resources.
