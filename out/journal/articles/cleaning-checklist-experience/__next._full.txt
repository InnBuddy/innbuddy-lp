清掃はもともと歓迎の行為だった</h2><p>数字の前に、文化の記憶をひとつ。茶の湯には「掃き払い」という作法があります。客を迎える前に、露地(茶室へ続く小路)を亭主が掃き清め、打ち水を打つ。<strong>掃除は準備作業ではなく、歓迎の最初の行為</strong>として行われてきました。清掃をコストと見る発想は、実は歴史の中では新しいのです。日本の宿の原点では、掃き払いこそおもてなしの第一動作でした。チェックリストを設計するとき、この視点を持つと項目の選び方が変わります——「何を確認するか」でなく「何で迎えるか」。</p><h2>チェックリストの3層構造 宿のひとこと。手書きのメモ、季節の一輪、その宿にしかない置き物の演出。層3は「毎部屋必ず」にしない——小さな体験の選択の規律は、チェックアウト後に語られる体験の記事が扱っています。</li></ol><p>層1が清潔さを守り、層2と層3が体験を作ります。<strong>合計25項目。3層に分ける理由は、配分を間違えないため</strong>です。多くの宿のチェックリストは層1に偏り、層3だけの宿は清潔さを欠く。数字の「15・7・3」は目安ですが、層の順番は動かさないでください。</p><h2>運用の3つ And cleanliness is the item where <strong>being unnoticeable is the right answer, and being noticeable is the loss of points</strong>. This article organizes the design of a checklist that turns housekeeping from "cost" into "experience," from both the numbers and the culture.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>In the same accommodation-attitudes survey introduced in the room-photos article (314 people, conducted March 2026), the leading elements felt as letdowns at higher-priced properties were "poor quality of service and staff" at 30.4%, "places the cleaning had not reached" at 24.7%, and "poor quality of the food" at 21.6%. Cleaning, above the food, has become an item that betrays expectations (as of writing; an attitudes survey)</strong> — The moment the "taken for granted" of cleanliness breaks, it is the second-heaviest thing.</div>

<h2>Hakibarai — cleaning was originally an act of welcome</h2>

<p>Before the numbers, one memory of culture. In the tea ceremony there is a practice called "hakibarai" — sweeping. Before receiving a guest, the host sweeps clean the roji (the path leading to the tea room) and sprinkles it with water. <strong>Cleaning was performed not as preparation but as the first act of welcome.</strong> The view of cleaning as a cost is, in fact, new in history. At the origin of the Japanese inn, the sweeping was the first motion of omotenashi. Hold this viewpoint when designing a checklist, and the way you choose items changes — not "what to check" but "with what to welcome."</p>

<h2>The checklist’s 3-layer structure — 15 standard, 7 experience, 3 signature</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Layer 1: standard (15 items)</strong> — The daily baseline. Bedding, soap scum and hair in the bath, odors, dust, the remote controls and kettle handles, the backs of the curtains, the light shades. The layer that defends "cleanliness" — build a system that checks it twice.</li>
<li><strong>Layer 2: experience (7 items)</strong> — The details guests "feel." The first five minutes of arrival (the air and temperature at the moment the door opens), the room’s scent, the lighting scene (is everything on?), the fold of the towels, the direction of the slippers at the entrance.</li>
<li><strong>Layer 3: signature (3 items)</strong> — The property’s one word. A handwritten note, a single seasonal flower, the staging of an object that exists nowhere else. Do not make Layer 3 "every room, without fail" — the discipline of choosing small experiences is handled in the small-experiences article.</li>
</ol>

<p>Layer 1 defends cleanliness; Layers 2 and 3 make the experience. <strong>25 items in all. The reason for the three layers is not to get the allocation wrong.</strong> Most properties’ checklists lean on Layer 1; a property with only Layer 3 lacks cleanliness. The numbers "15 / 7 / 3" are yardsticks, but do not move the order of the layers.</p>

<h2>Three practices — not relying on memory</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Leave a record</strong> — On paper or in an app, in a form where "who, when, ✔" remains for each room. Cleaning that runs on memory and goodwill always breaks in the peak season.</li>
<li><strong>Double-check Layer 1</strong> — Separate the person who cleans from the person who verifies. Layer 1 is the layer where "an oversight is a lost point," so it is worth the second pair of eyes most.</li>
<li><strong>Teaching the "why"</strong> — Why look behind the curtains (because it is the nearest place to the sleeping guest). Telling why stays on the floor longer than telling how.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The manner of showing the cleaning</h2>

<p>If you meet a guest while cleaning — do not look away; bow, and add one line: "We are preparing your room — may we ask for a little time?" The experience of being bowed to mid-cleaning stays, in fact, in the guest’s memory. In the culture of hakibarai, the act of sweeping was visible to the guest as the host’s own gesture — <strong>cleaning is not something to hide; only when it is visible who is doing it does it become "an act."</strong> Properties that put their housekeepers’ names and faces in their introduction are, on this same line, helping their hiring too (see the hiring-PR article).</p>

<h2>The other side — when the checklist exhausts the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Hollowing out into ✔s</strong> — A list where everything gets a ✔ is, in fact, a list where nothing is being seen. A rough remedy — one day a week on which ✔s are forbidden — exists, but start by testing whether staff can talk about the Layer 2 and 3 items.</li>
<li><strong>The line with outsourced cleaning</strong> — Layer 1 is a layer that can be outsourced; Layer 3 is a layer to keep in the property’s own hands. Decide the line first, or only the signature experience gets outsourced.</li>
<li><strong>Pushing a scent</strong> — Layer 2’s "scent" includes the choice of no scent at all. For sensitive guests, the air freshener itself is the complaint (see the scent-design article).</li>
<li><strong>All 25 items at once</strong> — A property introducing all three layers this week returns to last week by next week. This week, start by rewriting Layer 1’s 15 items through the lens of "cleanliness the guest notices."</li>
<li><strong>Checks do not make quality</strong> — The list is a tool for holding the minimum. What raises cleanliness to a higher level is the eye of the checker growing. Once a month, swap the rooms the veteran and the newcomer each look at.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is to reread your current checklist once. What you count while reading is the number of "items the guest notices." If few, add them through the Layer 2 lens — the moment the door opens, the first five minutes, the things taken in hand. The goal of cleaning is not to pass an inspection; it is <strong>to make a stay in which nobody talks about cleaning</strong>. Not being talked about is this department’s medal.</p>
