11:T1d59,<p>The guest who leaves after one night, ¥10,000, room only — and the guest who, on the same ¥10,000 stay, spends another ¥5,000 on an experience, ¥3,000 on food and drink, ¥2,000 on souvenirs in the region, and tells the story of that experience three times after returning home. Which is worth more to the region hardly needs asking. To measure an inbound guest's value <strong>not by "the rate of one night" but by "the time and money spent in the region,"</strong> this article organizes the three elements of an experience program and the design for building it into the inn.</p>

<h2>The satisfaction exists. It is not being bought.</h2>

<p>Ask foreign visitors about their satisfaction with "experiences of everyday life in Japan," and 96.9% answer "satisfied" (a 2022 Japan Tourism Agency survey). History and traditional culture: 96.4%. But look at the payment side and the story changes. Breaking down 2025 visitor spending by category: lodging 36.6%, shopping 27.0%, food and drink 21.9%, transport 10.0% — and <strong>entertainment and services (experiences) stops at 4.5%</strong>.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>In Japan Tourism Agency surveys, foreign visitors' satisfaction with "experiences of everyday life in Japan" is 96.9%, and with history and traditional culture 96.4% (fiscal 2022 report). Meanwhile, in spending by category, entertainment and services is 4.5% — small beside lodging 36.6%, shopping 27.0%, food and drink 21.9%, and transport 10.0% (Survey of Foreign-Visitor Consumption Trends, 2025 final figures, as of writing)</strong> — The experience exists as "satisfaction," but is still small as "a product."</div>

<p>There is only one way to read this gap. <strong>Demand for experiences already exists; a buyable form has not been prepared.</strong> The miso warehouse tour, the morning market at the fishing port — most remain unconverted into "a product you can reserve and buy." The work of filling this gap goes undone unless someone in the region does it. And the business best placed to stand at its entrance is the inn.</p>

<p>Note, too, that per-visitor entertainment spending differs greatly by country: the highest, Australia, at ¥35,000 (same final report). The money that falls on experiences moves with the guest layer — meaning the choice of whom the inn invites is itself part of designing experience revenue.</p>

<h2>Three things that grow — length of stay, spending, word of mouth</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Length of stay</strong> — If it is only "a place to sleep," one night is enough. "Tomorrow the miso warehouse, the day after the fishing port's morning market" — and a reason for consecutive nights exists. Experiences work as a reason to extend.</li>
<li><strong>Spending in the region</strong> — Experience fees, food and shopping at the venue, transfers. Money dropped outside the room charge stays in the region. The inn can become the entrance to regional spending.</li>
<li><strong>Depth of word of mouth</strong> — "The room was clean" is forgotten by next year. "I pickled umeboshi with a grandmother" is still being told after the flight home. Experiences are, themselves, the material the inn gets talked about with.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The three elements of an experience program</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>A reason to stay — connection with producers and the region</strong> — Sightseeing rounds can be done as a day trip. Entering the field, watching the auction, making something together in the workshop. Connections that exist nowhere else become the reason to stay the night.</li>
<li><strong>What the guest does — participation, not watching</strong> — The difference between a look-only tour and pickling plums, weaving a net, splitting firewood is the difference between staying in memory or not. What the hands did remains in the body.</li>
<li><strong>What they can take home — a product, photos, a story</strong> — What they made in the experience, the producer's own goods, and the tale of "that miso from back then — we still have some at home." The take-home becomes the seed of the next booking.</li>
</ol>

<h2>The design of inclusion — do not make it an add-on</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Sell it inside the package</strong> — "One night + miso making." "Two nights + morning market and breakfast." The experience becomes not "something you choose" but "something included." An experience placed at the end of the options does not get chosen.</li>
<li><strong>State the times</strong> — Not "experience available" but "9:00 depart the inn, 9:30–11:00 miso warehouse, 11:30 return." Inbound itineraries are built on clock times.</li>
<li><strong>Prepare words and gestures</strong> — One sheet in English at the experience site. Share with producers the English — and the gestures — for "thank you" and "delicious." The arrival of intent, over perfect interpreting.</li>
<li><strong>Designing the price — return it to the region</strong> — The cost breakdown: the producer's honorarium + transfer + insurance. Keeping the inn's share to 10–20% is what makes producers engage seriously. For reference, many experiences start at roughly ¥2,000–5,000 per guest (one example).</li>
</ul>

<h2>The other side — when experiences exhaust the inn</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Bound to the producers’ trade and the seasons</strong> — Days canceled by harvest, fishing, or weather always come. Prepare rainy-day and off-season alternatives before selling.</li>
<li><strong>For tired guests, "doing nothing" is also a product</strong> — An experience-only package drives away the guest too tired to want anything. Offer both: included, and opt-in.</li>
<li><strong>Safety and insurance</strong> — An accident during an experience happens inside an itinerary the inn built. Write the scope of insurance and the line of responsibility with the producer, in advance.</li>
<li><strong>Trust broken over the share</strong> — A design where the inn takes most of the experience fee exhausts the producer within a year. A design that returns is not ethics; it is the condition of continuing.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is small. On a map within fifteen minutes of the inn, write three producers, fishers, or workshops you can speak to, and build "one experience" with one of them. Once the time, the take-home, and the honorarium are summarized on one sheet, that is already the prototype of a sellable product. The satisfaction already exists. All that remains is to give it a buyable form.</p>

12:T151f,<p>入境旅客价值<strong>“在该地区花费的时间和金钱”而不是“每晚单价”</strong>确定方式为：一名客人每晚花费 10,000 日元不含餐住宿并离开，另一名客人在当地花费 5,000 日元用于体验，3,000 日元用于食品和饮料，2,000 日元用于购买当地纪念品，并在回家后在 SNS 上发布 3 次。哪个对该地区更有价值？本文概述了延长停留时间、增加消费、加深口碑的“本地体验计划”的三个要素，以及如何将其融入住宿的设计。</p>

<h2>1. 为什么是体验式项目？ 3种成长方式</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>停留时间增加：</strong>只要过夜就够了，但如果你打算明天做味噌，后天和渔民一起去早市，你就有理由过夜了。这种体验是过夜的理由。</li>
<li><strong>本地消费增长：</strong>体验费、体验场地餐饮、交通。除住宿费用外的消费将下降到该地区。当旅馆成为“一个地区的门户”时，整个地区就会变得丰富起来（参见有关地区振兴的文章）。</li>
<li><strong>评论深度增加：</strong>“我和奶奶一起做了酸梅”比“我的房间很漂亮”更好。经验创造了“可以谈论的旅馆”。</li>
</ul>

<h2>2. 体验计划三要素</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>“留下来的理由”——不是旅游，而是与生产商和该地区的联系：</strong>如果只是想参观旅游景点，可以选择一日游。参观生产者的田地，在渔港观看拍卖，并在工作室一起创作。只有在那里才能找到的“与人的联系”成为留下来的理由。例如：凌晨 5 点的渔港、味噌啤酒厂之旅、竹工艺品制作。</li>
<li><strong>“客人做什么”——参与，而不是被动：</strong>不要只是看和听，要用你的双手。腌李子、织网、劈柴。通过参与，记忆会留在你的身体里。它是“迎宾礼遇”（参见文章）的延伸，可在 5 分钟内完成。</li>
<li><strong>“你可以带回家的东西”——产品、故事和照片：</strong>体验期间制作的物品（味噌、竹篮）、生产商的产品、体验中的照片和故事。即使回到日本后，“我家里还保留着当时的味噌。”您可以带回家的东西将成为您下次预订的理由。</li>
</ol>

<h2>3.“内置”设计——不要让它成为一个额外的选项</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>包装内包含：</strong>如果您将其设为附加选项，则不会选择它。套餐包括“1晚+味噌制作体验”和“连续2晚+渔港早市和早餐”，并且从一开始就包含体验。经验不是可以选择的东西，而是包含在其中的东西。</li>
<li><strong>明确时间：</strong>不是“包含体验”，而是“9:00 从酒店出发，9:30-11:00 参观味噌啤酒厂，11:30 到达酒店。”要清楚何时、何地以及做什么。入站对时间敏感。</li>
<li><strong>多语言导游和外籍工作人员：</strong>有关体验目的地的信息有英文版本。参考外籍人员的文章，准备20个可以现场使用的短语。我们还与我们品尝的生产者分享了简单的英语和手势，例如“谢谢”和“美味”。</li>
<li><strong>价格设计：</strong>当体验费包含在住宿费用中时，费用中包含“制作者报酬+交通+保险”。如果住宿的设计将份额保持在10-20%并回馈当地社区，那么继续下去会更容易。作为参考值，每次体验的客人费用为2,000-5,000日元，住宿费为500-1,000日元。</li>
</ul>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">数值示例</span><strong>例如，平均住宿1.8晚的旅馆若延长0.6晚就是2.4晚。当地追加消费也可以这样理解——“为每位客人增加一项约3,000日元左右的体验”，这是便于想象的数值示例。</strong> — 体验不是“成本”，而是“延长停留时间的投资”。判断效果时，请用相同的统计方法记录并比较施策前后30天的平均住宿天数与可选项参与率。</div>

<h2>行动清单</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>第 1 周：3 名合作候选人</strong> — 列出三个可以作为体验的当地生产商、旅游景点和作坊。味噌、酱油、钓鱼、竹工艺品、陶器等</li>
<li><strong>第 2 周：设计 1 体验</strong> — 根据三个要素设计体验：入住原因、要做的事情和带回家的东西。明确时间、地点以及带什么回家。</li>
<li><strong>第 3 周：纳入包装中</strong> — 通过 OTA 和我们自己的网站作为“体验包”出售。第一张照片显示了体验。请在描述中注明时间和外卖物品。</li>
<li><strong>第 4 周：试用 + 记录</strong> — 让你的员工或朋友尝试一下并记录所需的时间和改进之处。记录客人的反应（无论是拍照、发布到 SNS 还是购买产品）。</li>
<li><strong>续：回馈社会</strong> — 与制作人分享体验费用的细目。如果设计旨在回馈当地社区，生产者就会变得更加积极主动，体验质量也会提高。</li>
</ul>
