11:T1c6f,<p>The memory of a stay <strong>continues by "being taken home."</strong> After check-out, opening the jam at home recalls that breakfast; using the sea salt recalls that seaside inn. Retail is not "revenue" but "the taking-home of memory." This article is the foundation for designing a marketplace on one lounge shelf, without building a big shop (how to add story to souvenirs — the roots of the word miyage and the story of Vienna’s sweets — is treated in detail by <a href="#idea-souvenir-story-takehome">Tell Story of Souvenir - Ideas for Adding Story to Souvenir to Take Home</a>. This article is the step before it: making the marketplace).</p>

<h2>Retail that began at 6.5 tsubo</h2>

<p>In 1976, in a small street-front store in Harajuku, Tokyo, "American Life Shop BEAMS" opened. The floor area of this store, later the pioneer of Japan’s select shops, was <strong>6.5 tsubo</strong> — about thirteen tatami mats. Inspired by a Los Angeles student dormitory room, it began as a shop that "edited" and sold the American lifestyle. The winning line was neither floor area nor assortment but <strong>the eye that chooses</strong>.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>BEAMS’ first store (Harajuku, Tokyo) opened in 1976 with 6.5 tsubo of floor space (about 13 tatami mats). It set out as a lifestyle-proposal shop inspired by a student dormitory room, and became the pioneer of the select shop that edits and sells goods from Japan and abroad (based on the company’s published history)</strong> — A small marketplace is not a constraint but proof of "editing."</div>

<p>What carries over to properties is this point: <strong>the strength of a marketplace is decided not by area or item count but by what was not chosen.</strong> 6.5 tsubo does not hold hundreds of items. Precisely so, each item placed speaks the fact that it was "chosen." One lounge shelf is the spirit of 6.5 tsubo, exactly.</p>

<h2>3 conditions — what to place</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Buyable only in that region</strong> — The farm’s jam, the sea’s salt, the mountains’ woodwork, the local brewery’s beer. The moment you place what can be bought anywhere, the shelf falls to "a goods shelf."</li>
<li><strong>Connected to the property’s story</strong> — The jam served at breakfast, the same soap as the rooms, the herbal tea poured in the lounge. Goods already experienced become the act of "taking that experience home."</li>
<li><strong>Easy to carry home</strong> — Unbreakable, light, shelf-stable, no leaks. Jam in small jars, salt in small bags, woodwork as small cutlery. What meets the conditions of travel meets the conditions of purchase.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Designing the shelf — one shelf, 3–5 items, margin</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Do not build a big shop</strong> — Inventory management and staff burden eat the revenue. One shelf in the lounge is enough.</li>
<li><strong>Lay out 3–5 items, with margin</strong> — Light the wooden shelf. A crammed shelf looks cheap; a shelf with margin makes each item stand out.</li>
<li><strong>Add the price tag and one line about the producer</strong> — "This salt comes from the sea in front of you." With one line, the guest buys not a good but a story.</li>
<li><strong>Create chances to try</strong> — Serve it at breakfast, pour it in the lounge, place it in the rooms. Do not specially stage a tasting — <strong>build it into the stay’s experience</strong>: that is the clever form (how to craft the one item served in the lounge is in <a href="#herb-tea-satoyama">Forest Herbal Tea: Translating Satoyama Bounty into a Drink</a>).</li>
</ul>

<h2>Operation — read in gross profit, hold no stock</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Read in gross profit, not revenue</strong> — A once-a-month calculation is fine. High-margin goods are not unusual, and the small shelf’s worth comes into view correctly.</li>
<li><strong>Little stock, restock on sale</strong> — An agreement with producers to "restock when it sells" cuts inventory risk. The discipline of <a href="#sourcing-sustainability">Protecting Nature's Gifts — Talking Through Procurement & Sustainability</a> applies as is.</li>
<li><strong>Swap one item once a month</strong> — Keep what sells; move on from what does not. Keeping the repeater’s "what will be on the shelf this time?" updated is the shelf’s lifeline.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Properties it fits, properties it does not</h2>

<p>This mechanism works most strongly at properties <strong>with many repeat guests and an axis in the experience (food, baths, walks)</strong>. Conversely, at properties where lodging is the main event and the morning of departure is rushed, before the assortment you should design <strong>placement and the speed of settlement</strong>. Add it to the bill, or pay self-serve — the form that takes not one extra minute of the morning comes first.</p>

<h2>The other side — when one shelf exhausts the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The marketplace becoming a shell</strong> — A shelf no one touches remains "in form only," together with the dust. If a month brings no sales, swap the items; if three bring none, consider resting the shelf itself.</li>
<li><strong>One step short of selling</strong> — A word at the previous evening’s breakfast may be natural, but a pitch at settlement can stain the last memory. The send-off hour is not marketplace hour.</li>
<li><strong>The trap of watching gross profit only</strong> — The moment you swap in "what sells well anywhere," the shelf’s meaning disappears. Do not let go of the editing (what only that land, that property, can mean).</li>
<li><strong>Law and display</strong> — Selling requires price display; food requires expiry labeling. The caution on import restrictions for overseas guests (meat, fruit, etc.) is treated in detail in the souvenir article.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is <strong>writing down three regional goods "already tried" by your guests</strong>. Breakfast’s jam, the rooms’ soap, the lounge’s herbal tea — look, and there are always three. This week, place those three on one shelf beside the front desk, with price tags and one line each. Retail is "the taking-home of memory" — your property’s shelf, with the editing power of 6.5 tsubo, can become a shop that sells memory.</p>

12:T1852,<p>客栈的回忆<strong>继续“带回家”</strong>。休息室一角设有一家小型零售店，客人可以带一些东西回家，即使在回家后也会让他们想起旅馆。每次退房后在家打开果酱，我都会想起那顿早餐。每次用海盐，我都会想起那家海边旅馆。零售的目的不是销售，而是带回记忆。本文将解释如何在休息室的单个货架上设计“记忆外卖”，而不需要创建一个大商店。</p>

<h2>1. 卖什么——三个条件和一个故事</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>只能在该地区购买：</strong>农产品果酱、海盐、山地木工和当地啤酒厂的啤酒。只能在当地购买的物品值得带回家。任何在任何地方都能买到的东西都不值得带回家。通过直接与当地生产商打交道，故事就诞生了。</li>
<li><strong>与客栈的故事相关：</strong>早餐提供果酱，和房间里的设施一样的肥皂，还有我在休息室喝的花草茶。客人可以通过购买与在旅馆中体验过的相同物品来“将这种体验带回家”。与旅馆故事相关的零售可以加深记忆。</li>
<li><strong>方便带回家：</strong>不易破碎、重量轻、常温下正常、无泄漏。瓶子破裂，重物很难带回家。果酱小瓶、盐袋、木工用小餐具。方便带回家降低了购买门槛。轻松带回家不是“销售”，而是“轻松带回家回忆”。</li>
</ol>

<h2>2. 放在哪里——休息室的架子以及如何展示</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>我们不建大商店。休息室里的一个架子就足够了：</strong>当你开一家大商店时，库存管理就会变得困难，员工的负担也会增加。休息室里一个架子就足够了。每个架子上仅放置 3 至 5 件物品，并在物品之间留出空间。留白可以让每件物品都脱颖而出。如何打造酷炫的咖啡馆外观：仅点亮木架上的 3 到 5 件物品。</li>
<li><strong>请务必包含价格标签和生产商的说明：</strong>除了价格标签之外，还包括生产商的说明。 “这种果酱是隔壁农民做的。”“这种盐是用我们面前的海水制成的。”其中有一句台词告诉客人“相信这个故事”。如果有故事，即使价格再高，人们也会买。</li>
<li><strong>使其“可尝试”：</strong>早餐时尝试果酱、休息室的花草茶以及客房内的肥皂。如果你能在购买前试用一下，购买的门槛就会降低。通过尝试，“体验”变成“销售产品”。尝试也可以“增强你的记忆力”。</li>
</ul>

<h2>3、如何查看毛利和库存管理</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>看毛利润而不是销售额：</strong>从销售额来看零售额往往“没什么大不了”。但从毛利润来看，也有毛利率在60-70%的产品。您可以通过查看毛利润来了解零售价值。每月计算一次毛利润并可视化零售价值。</li>
<li><strong>库存不足，售出后补货：</strong>如果保留大量库存，就会导致食物浪费和库存损失。库存有限，售完即会补充。食物浪费同样的想法。通过与生产商签订“售后补货”合同，可以降低库存风险。当生产者知道他们的产品已经售出时，他们也会很高兴。</li>
<li><strong>继续一件已售出的商品，替换一件未售出的商品：</strong>我们每月一次分析畅销产品和不畅销产品。一项已售出的商品将继续销售；一件未售出的商品将被替换。通过更换三到五个项目中的一个，总会有一个新项目在展示。当新产品推出时，回头客会想再次查看。</li>
</ul>

<ul>
<li><strong>第 1 周：列出早餐和客房中使用的 3 种当地产品。</strong> — 列出您在旅馆使用的三种当地产品，例如您早餐使用的果酱、房间内的设施以及您在休息室喝的花草茶。优先购买只能在当地购买的物品。</li>
