11:T1b79,<p>"The booking decision is made in the few seconds of the thumbnail." For anyone who looks at property booking screens every day, this should feel not like exaggeration but like lived experience. And recent surveys have begun putting numbers on that experience. This article unpacks the three principles shared by photos that bring bookings — <strong>light, lived-in warmth, and empty space</strong> — and organizes the 12-shot must-have list and where, within it, to spend your effort.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>In a survey of attitudes toward accommodation (an internet survey of 314 people, conducted in March 2026, published on a TV Tokyo news site), the final deciding factors for choosing a property were "location and access" (70.9%) and "lodging cost" (69.2%), with "room photos on the official site and booking sites" third (67.8%). The higher the nightly spend, the more amenity and equipment photos become the decider (63.2% weigh them heavily in the ¥20,000–30,000 band), and the scene guests most want to share is "the view and interior in the moment of entering the room" (46.7%) (as of writing; an attitudes survey)</strong> — Right behind price and location, photographs are already lined up.</div>

<h2>What the "90%" really is — the "reason to choose" after the conditions</h2>

<p>If you look at that survey and think "isn’t that third place?", you are reading it correctly. Price and location are, in most cases, <strong>"conditions" entered before the search</strong>. What creates the final reason to choose among the candidates that meet the conditions — that is the photograph. Read the "90%" of the title as a metaphor for the felt reality in "the contest that begins once the conditions are lined up." OTA behavior follows a strict funnel: <strong>thumbnail (the list screen) → click → the first three photos → details → booking</strong>. The largest lever over whether you are clicked on the list screen is the photograph, and it is a well-worn rule of thumb among operators that swapping a photo moves the click rate sharply. Optimizing photos is therefore not "design work" but <strong>revenue work</strong> at the very top of the funnel.</p>

<h2>Principle 1 — light</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Shoot in the morning.</strong> The window is 60 to 90 minutes after sunrise — soft light, long shadows.</li>
<li><strong>Face the window.</strong> Shoot at the angle where natural light crosses the room diagonally.</li>
<li><strong>Turn the ceiling lights off.</strong> Photos lit by interior lighting alone flatten the room — the most common mistake.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Principle 2 — lived-in warmth</h2>

<p>The best styling is the moment "just before the guest enters the room." Towels placed at the foot of the bed, a cup and water on the table, one cushion slightly askew. A photo that is "tidy but empty" looks like a showroom — beautiful, but not somewhere you could stay. <strong>A small trace of "life" raises conversion</strong> because the guest’s brain is simulating "I spend time here," and that simulation is what creates the booking.</p>

<h2>Principle 3 — empty space</h2>

<p>Leave thirty percent of the frame open, and convey not the "furniture" but the "view." Do not pack the frame with furniture; let the sky, the mountains, the streetscape breathe. The "place" in the photograph is a differentiation the competitor next door cannot copy.</p>

<h2>The 12-shot must-have list</h2>

<p>The order of the list is the order of priority.</p>

<ol>
<li>The first photo — the guest room (in morning light)</li>
<li>The room’s second angle (from the window)</li>
<li>A close-up of the bedding (texture)</li>
<li>The bathroom (bright)</li>
<li>The view (what is seen from the room)</li>
<li>The restaurant / the breakfast table</li>
<li>The property’s signature space (the lounge, and the like)</li>
<li>Details that speak the brand (art, materials)</li>
<li>The surrounding area / access</li>
<li>The owner or the staff (human warmth)</li>
<li>Seasonal styling (the "sense of updating" that changes with the seasons)</li>
<li>Group and family use (broadening the audience)</li>
</ol>

<h2>Spend half the effort on "the first three photos"</h2>

<p>Do not perfect all twelve. <strong>Use half your shooting time on the first three.</strong> The first photo is "the signboard"; the next three are "the story." This is where the stumbling happens, where the scroll down the detail page stops. And as the survey above shows, the higher the nightly rate, the more the decider shifts to "photos of amenities and equipment" — the more a property wants to raise its rates, the more carefully it should treat No. 3 (the bedding’s texture) and No. 8 (the details). Photos are not a once-and-done shoot; they are something whose "sense of freshness" is maintained by reshooting with the seasons (the timing of shoots for the summer peak is in the seasonal-plays article).</p>

<h2>The other side — when photos hurt the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Over-staging becomes a complaint</strong> — "different from the photos" is Pattern 1 of inbound complaints. Wide-angle looks and embellished styling come back, in the moment of entering the room, as "the gap from the photos" (see the complaint-handling article).</li>
<li><strong>You need not be a pro — but the first three, yes</strong> — Even without the budget for a photographer for all twelve, reshooting just the first three in morning light is possible on a smartphone. Trying to fix everything and fixing nothing is what happens on the ground.</li>
<li><strong>Giving up on updates</strong> — A page selling summer with snow scenes left up quietly shaves trust. Review the first three at least twice a year.</li>
<li><strong>Don’t sell the "90%" literally</strong> — The fact that price and location sit above is something properties know best. The value of photographs is better told, to the field, not as exaggeration but as "the position of the final deciding factor."</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is, this weekend morning, to reshoot the first three photos. Sixty minutes after sunrise, curtains fully open, ceiling lights off — with that alone, the first photo at many properties is reborn. The 12-shot list is the map for after those three are settled.</p>

<h2>1. 为什么是“90%照片”？</h2>
<p>OTA 行动遵循严格的渠道：<strong>缩略图（列表页）→ 单击 → 前 3 → 详细信息 → 预订</strong>。列表页的点击率通常为 3-8%，而照片是最大的杠杆。在实际操作中，经常会发现仅仅改变照片，CTR就会变化±30%。详细信息屏幕也是如此，如果前三个图像没有传达空间感，则滚动将在那里结束。</p>
<p>换句话说，照片优化不是“设计工作”，而是漏斗的顶部。<strong>销售工作</strong>。</p>
<h2>2. 原则一：轻</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>我早上就拿。</strong> 黄金时间是日出后60至90分钟。光线柔和，影子很长。</li>
<li><strong>转向窗户。</strong> 以自然光斜射房间的角度拍摄照片。</li>
<li><strong>关掉顶灯。</strong> 仅使用室内照明的照片使房间看起来很平坦——这是最常见的错误。</li>
</ul>
<h2>3.原则2：生活感受</h2>
<p>最好的造型时刻是在客人进入之前：</p>
<ul>
<li>毛巾放在床尾</li>
<li>桌上有一杯或一杯水</li>
<li>1 个稍微移动的坐垫</li>
</ul>
<p>这张“布置但空荡荡”的照片看起来就像一个陈列室。很漂亮，但我觉得我不能住在那里。<strong>小小的“生活方式”可以提高转化率。</strong>——客人的大脑正在模拟“在这里度过时间”，并且该模拟创建了预订。</p>
<h2>4. 原则 3：空白</h2>
<p>留出 30% 的图片开放以传达视图而不是家具：</p>
<ul>
<li>不要用家具挡住画面</li>
<li>让天空、山脉和城市景观呼吸</li>
<li>让“地方”说话——这是竞争对手无法模仿的差异化</li>
</ul>
<h2>12个必拍镜头清单</h2>
<ol>
<li>第一张照片——客房（晨光）</li>
<li>客房第二角度（从窗户看）</li>
<li>床上用品特写（质感）</li>
<li>浴室（明亮）</li>
<li>景观（从房间可以看到的东西）</li>
<li>餐厅/早餐桌</li>
<li>旅馆的象征性空间（休息室等）</li>
<li>与品牌相关的细节（艺术/材料）</li>
<li>周边地区/交通</li>
<li>管理层或员工（人们传递温暖）</li>
<li>季节造型（随季节变化的更新感）</li>
<li>团体/家庭使用（扩大范围）</li>
</ol>
