焼き物は「土」と「炎」のかたまりです。同じ土に同じ釉薬をかけても、窯の中の酸素の加減で仕上がりの色は変わります。工業製品の均一性を捨てた場所に、焼き物の面白さの核心があります。この記事は、その核心を宿の食卓と見学の案内にどう翻訳するかの話です。

同じ釉薬が、炎の加減で別の色になります

窯の焼き方には「酸化焼成」と「還元焼成」があります。酸素を十分に送り込んで焼くのが酸化。900℃あたりから空気の供給を絞って焼くのが還元です。これが釉薬の発色を決めるのです。有名なのは銅を含む釉薬で、酸化焼成では緑（織部）、還元焼成では朱色（辰砂）に発色します。黄瀬戸の釉薬は酸化で黄色、還元で青磁系の青に変わります。焼成温度は陶器でおよそ1200〜1250℃、磁器で1280〜1300℃です。

FACT銅を含む同じ釉薬が、酸化焼成では緑（織部）、還元焼成では朱色（辰砂）になる — 発色は炎の中の酸素の量で決まる。陶器は1200〜1250℃、磁器は1280〜1300℃で焼かれる。

この話には続きがあります。炎を出さない電気窯は、基本的に酸化焼成しかできません。還元ができるのは、薪やガスなど炎の出る窯です。つまり「昔ながらの登り窯や薪窯で焼く」という産地のこだわりは、浪漫ではなく、色の可能性の選択なのです。窯元見学の案内にこの一行があると、見学は「建物見学」から「炎と化学の見学」に変わります。

六つの産地が、千年続きました

日本には、中世からほぼ途切れず窯が続いてきた産地が六つあります。瀬戸、常滑、越前、信楽、丹波、備前。この六つを「日本六古窯」と名付けたのは、昭和30年代に陶磁器研究家の小山富士夫でした。京都や有田のような「窯のブランド」の物語が比較的新しい層だとすると、六古窯は日用品の窯として生き延びた、もっと古い層の産地の物語です。

たとえば備前焼は、釉薬を一切かけずに1200〜1300℃の高温で、長いときはおよそ2週間をかけて焼き締めます。灰と炎のあたりが、そのまま「窯変」という景色になるのです。絵具も釉薬も使わず、土と炎だけで表現する焼き物です。地中海のテラコッタと同じ、釉薬のない土の器という、世界で最も古いかたちの焼き物でもあります。益子や信楽の土の色がそれぞれ違って見えるのは、土の鉄分や粒の質が産地ごとに違うからです。

「白い金」を追った王と、幽閉された錬金術師

17世紀のヨーロッパでは、オランダ東インド会社を通じて中国の景徳鎮や日本の伊万里（有田）の白磁が運ばれ、王侯貴族を熱狂させました。金や宝石に匹敵する「白い金」です。ザクセンのアウグスト強王も熱烈な収集家の一人でした。財政難の打開策として、王は錬金術師ヨハン・フリードリヒ・ベトガーに白磁の製造を命じます（1705年）。ベトガーは監視つきの実験室に閉じ込められ、試行錯誤の末、1709年に白磁の製法を解明しました。1710年、王は製法の機密を守るため窯をドレスデンからマイセンへ移し、西洋初の磁器窯「マイセン」が誕生します。ベトガーはその後も軟禁状態が続き、1719年、37歳で亡くなりました。金を作れなかった錬金術師が、代わりに「白い金」を作らされた物語です。

CASEマイセン磁器の誕生（1710年）は、日本の伊万里が源流のひとつ — アウグスト強王の命を受けたベトガーが、カオリンや長石・石英の原料を1300℃台の高温で焼く白磁の製法を解明。以後、磁器の製法はヨーロッパ各国へ広まった。

なぜこの話を焼き物の里の宿で語るのでしょうか。本質はこうです。ヨーロッパの王が国の財政を懸けて欲しかったのは、金でも兵站でもなく、日本や中国の「土と炎の技」でした。有田という九州の村の窯が、世界の奢侈品の歴史を動かしていました。器は、その土地の土そのものを世界に売っていたのです。焼き物の里の宿の器には、同じ資格があります。「うちの器は、この土地の土です」という一言が、実は世界史につながっているのです。

軒先のたぬきは、八つの約束

焼き物の里の風景に欠かせない置物の話をひとつ。滋賀・信楽のたぬきが全国に広まったのは1951年、昭和天皇の行幸の際、沿道に日の丸の小旗を持たせた信楽のたぬきが並べられ、その情景が新聞で報道されたのがきっかけです。天皇は御製を詠みました（「をさなき日 あつめしからに なつかしも 信楽焼の 狸をみれば」——信楽に歌碑があります）。このたぬき、よく見ると八つの意味が読める「八相縁起」です。笠は災難への備え、大きな目は正しい判断、笑顔は商売繁盛、徳利は人徳、通い帳はお客様との信頼、大きなお腹は冷静な決断、金袋は金運、尻尾は「終わりよければ全てよし」。宿の門に置くなら、この八つを一行ずつ説明できると、たぬきは置物ではなく「宿の約束の表示」になります。

器で始める、小さくて大きな変更

  • 朝食の器を産地で統一する — 窯元の名前と産地を、メニューに一行で書きましょう。果樹園のある宿なら、土地の器に盛る果物で「土と果物の繋がり」を語れます。
  • 窯入れの日に見学を組む — 窯の稼働は年数回。見学の日程を窯元の窯入れに合わせられれば、体験は「特別席」になります。
  • 新しく買わないという選択 — 割れた器を金継ぎで直して使い続けるのは、完璧さより履歴を大切にする美学。継ぎ目は隠さずに語っていいのです。
  • 何をしないか — 器を飾り棚に並べるだけにしません。使う器だからこそ、履歴が生きます。

体験と見学の安全設計

  • 陶芸体験 — 爪を切り、髪をまとめ、エプロンを着ましょう。粘土や釉薬の粉塵は吸い込まないようにします。手順は窯元・講師の指示に従います。
  • 窯の見学 — 窯の中は数百〜1300℃の高温。立ち入り範囲は窯元の指示に従い、子どもから目を離さないようにしましょう。
  • 衛生 — 吸水率の高い焼き締めの器は、においや水分が移りやすいのです。料理に使うか、花器にするかは窯元に確認しましょう。
  • 向かない宿 — 破損と在庫のリスクを保険と料金設計に織り込めない宿。窯元との連携がないまま「焼き物の宿」を名乗る宿（器は買えば揃いますが、履歴を語る相手がいません）。

Pottery is a lump of “clay” and “fire.” Even with the same clay and the same glaze, the color of the finished piece changes with the amount of oxygen inside the kiln. The heart of pottery’s appeal lies in the place where the uniformity of industrial products has been abandoned. This article is about how to translate that heart into the inn’s table and the guidance for kiln tours.

The same glaze becomes a different color depending on the fire

Kiln firing comes in “oxidation firing” and “reduction firing.” Firing with plenty of oxygen sent in is oxidation. Firing with the air supply cut back from around 900°C is reduction. This determines the color of the glaze. A famous example is a glaze containing copper, which turns green (Oribe) in oxidation firing and vermilion red (shinsha) in reduction firing. Kiseto glaze turns yellow in oxidation and a celadon-like blue in reduction. Firing temperatures are roughly 1,200–1,250°C for earthenware-type pottery and 1,280–1,300°C for porcelain.

FACTThe same copper-bearing glaze turns green (Oribe) in oxidation firing and vermilion (shinsha) in reduction firing — The color is determined by the amount of oxygen in the flame. Pottery is fired at 1,200–1,250°C, porcelain at 1,280–1,300°C.

There is more to this story. Electric kilns, which produce no flame, can basically do only oxidation firing. Reduction is possible only in kilns with a flame, such as wood-fired or gas kilns. In other words, a production area’s insistence on “firing in a traditional climbing kiln or wood kiln” is not romance but a choice of color possibilities. With this one line in the guidance for a kiln tour, the tour changes from “visiting a building” to “visiting fire and chemistry.”

Six production areas have lasted a thousand years

Japan has six production areas where kilns have continued almost without interruption since the medieval period: Seto, Tokoname, Echizen, Shigaraki, Tanba, and Bizen. It was the ceramics researcher Koyama Fujio who named these six the “Six Ancient Kilns of Japan,” in the 1950s–1960s (the Shōwa 30s). If the stories of “kiln brands” such as Kyoto and Arita are a relatively new layer, the Six Ancient Kilns are the stories of production areas in an older layer, which survived as kilns for everyday wares.

Bizen ware, for example, is fired hard at high temperatures of 1,200–1,300°C without any glaze at all, sometimes for as long as about two weeks. The marks of ash and flame become, just as they are, a landscape called “yōhen” (kiln transformation). It is pottery expressed with clay and fire alone, using neither paint nor glaze. Like Mediterranean terracotta, it is also an unglazed earthen vessel, the oldest form of pottery in the world. The colors of the clay of Mashiko and Shigaraki look different from each other because the iron content and grain of the clay differ from one production area to the next.

The king who chased “white gold,” and the imprisoned alchemist

In 17th-century Europe, white porcelain from Jingdezhen in China and Imari (Arita) in Japan was brought over through the Dutch East India Company and sent royalty and nobility into a frenzy. It was “white gold,” rivaling gold and jewels. Augustus the Strong of Saxony was one of its most ardent collectors. As a way out of financial trouble, the king ordered the alchemist Johann Friedrich Böttger to produce white porcelain (1705). Böttger was confined to a guarded laboratory, and after much trial and error, he worked out the method of making white porcelain in 1709. In 1710, to protect the secret of the method, the king moved the kiln from Dresden to Meissen, and “Meissen,” the first porcelain kiln in the West, was born. Böttger remained under house arrest afterward and died in 1719 at the age of 37. It is the story of an alchemist who could not make gold and was made to produce “white gold” instead.

CASEThe birth of Meissen porcelain (1710) has Japan’s Imari as one of its sources — On the orders of Augustus the Strong, Böttger worked out the method of making white porcelain by firing raw materials of kaolin, feldspar, and quartz at high temperatures in the 1,300°C range. After that, the method of making porcelain spread to countries across Europe.

Why tell this story at an inn in a pottery village? The essence is this. What a European king wanted, staking his country’s finances on it, was neither gold nor military supplies but the “skill of clay and fire” of Japan and China. The kilns of Arita, a village in Kyushu, were moving the history of the world’s luxury goods. The vessels were selling the very clay of that land to the world. The vessels of an inn in a pottery village carry the same credentials. The single remark “Our dishes are made of this land’s clay” actually connects to world history.

The tanuki at the eaves are eight promises

One more story, about an ornament that is indispensable to the landscape of a pottery village. The tanuki of Shigaraki, Shiga, spread nationwide in 1951: during an imperial visit by Emperor Shōwa, Shigaraki tanuki holding small Hinomaru flags were lined up along the roadside, and the scene was reported in the newspapers. The Emperor composed a poem (“Wosanaki hi / atsumeshi kara ni / natsukashimo / Shigarakiyaki no / tanuki wo mireba” — “How nostalgic, for I collected them in my childhood days, when I see the tanuki of Shigaraki ware”; there is a monument with the poem in Shigaraki). Look closely at this tanuki and you can read eight meanings in it, the “hassō engi” (eight auspicious features). The hat is preparation against misfortune, the big eyes are sound judgment, the smile is prosperous business, the sake flask is virtue, the account book is trust with customers, the big belly is calm decision-making, the money bag is good fortune with money, and the tail is “all’s well that ends well.” If you place one at the inn’s gate and can explain these eight one line at a time, the tanuki becomes not an ornament but “a display of the inn’s promises.”

Small but big changes that start with tableware

  • Unify the breakfast tableware with wares from the production area — Write the kiln’s name and the production area in a single line on the menu. An inn with an orchard can tell of “the connection between clay and fruit” through fruit served on the land’s own dishes.
  • Arrange tours on kiln-loading days — Kilns are fired a few times a year. If the tour schedule can be matched to a kiln’s loading day, the experience becomes “a special seat.”
  • The choice not to buy new — Repairing broken dishes with kintsugi and continuing to use them is an aesthetic that values history over perfection. The seams need not be hidden; they can be talked about.
  • What not to do — Do not simply line dishes up on a display shelf. It is precisely because they are dishes in use that their history comes alive.

Safety design for experiences and tours

  • Pottery experiences — Trim nails, tie back hair, and wear an apron. Do not inhale clay or glaze dust. Follow the procedures instructed by the kiln or the instructor.
  • Kiln tours — Inside a kiln, temperatures range from several hundred to 1,300°C. Follow the kiln’s instructions on the area that may be entered, and do not take your eyes off children.
  • Hygiene — Unglazed, hard-fired wares with high water absorption easily take on smells and moisture. Check with the kiln whether to use them for food or as flower vases.
  • Inns it doesn’t suit — Inns that cannot factor the risks of breakage and stock into insurance and pricing. Inns that call themselves “a pottery inn” without any partnership with a kiln (dishes can be bought, but there is no one to tell their history).
