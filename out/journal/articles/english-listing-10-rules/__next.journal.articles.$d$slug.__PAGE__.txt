11:T1a13,<p>The biggest mistake in English listing copy is literal translation. And the real problem with literal translation is not bad English. It is that <strong>a "design of reassurance" written in Japanese has been swapped into English and carries nothing across</strong>. Omotenashi, tasteful atmosphere, the way of lavishing care — these do not cross by changing words. What crosses is actions and facts. This article organizes, from the viewpoint of translating culture into actions, the ten rules that bring an English listing to the state of "understood and booked."</p>

<h2>Rules 1–3 — structure: the place is clear in three seconds</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Location first</strong> — Not the property name but the form "5 minutes’ walk from XX Station." Inbound guests choose by place (see the search-behavior article).</li>
<li><strong>Fifteen words per sentence at most</strong> — Long sentences are not read. Cut, and line them up.</li>
<li><strong>Make "you" the subject</strong> — Not the autobiography of "We offer..." but "You will sleep on a futon in a 100-year-old house." Write the guest’s experience, not the property’s description.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Rules 4–6 — words: culture into actions, ambiguity into facts</h2>

<ol start="4">
<li><strong>Delete "omotenashi" and convert it into actions</strong> — Concepts cannot be translated literally. A handwritten note carrying the guest’s name, carrying the luggage to the room, walking to the station at departure — this one sentence is understood anywhere in the world. "Heartfelt hospitality" is not.</li>
<li><strong>State what is included, with "included"</strong> — "Japanese breakfast is included." Breakfast, Wi-Fi, transfers, hot springs. Clarity of the total is the foundation of inbound trust.</li>
<li><strong>Use no adjective you cannot verify</strong> — "Luxury" and "exclusive" look, to the reader, like nothing but advertising. The year of the renovation, the number of rooms, what is seen from the window — facts are more luxurious than adjectives.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Rules 7–10 — details and proof: getting ahead of expectations</h2>

<ol start="7">
<li><strong>Write the "three points that may disappoint" first</strong> — "There is no elevator." No elevator, breakfast hours, the distance and slopes to the station. Written first, they read as consideration; discovered later, as dishonesty. Many low ratings can be eliminated here.</li>
<li><strong>Put a "guest guide" in the description</strong> — What to do in the area (the morning market, walking paths, the sento bathhouse). More than the property’s boasting, the imagination of the stay makes the difference.</li>
<li><strong>Align the title, the first photo, and the opening of the description</strong> — Only when the three make the same promise does "clarity" hold. A photo of the hot springs and a title about the sea — that misalignment is a seed of distrust.</li>
<li><strong>Have a native speaker look once, only once</strong> — Do not aim for perfection. Have them fix only the parts that feel strongly off, and call it done.</li>
</ol>

<h2>A rewriting example — two faces of the same property</h2>

<p><strong>Before (literal translation)</strong> — "Our inn offers heartfelt omotenashi hospitality with luxurious rooms and beautiful nature." A sentence full of good intent that carries nothing across.</p>

<p><strong>After (rewritten)</strong> — "A renovated 100-year-old farmhouse in the mountains of XX. You will sleep on a futon and hear the river from your room. Japanese breakfast with vegetables from the village is included. There is no elevator." Rules 1 (place), 3 (you), 4 (actions), 5 (included), 6 (facts), and 7 (disappointments) sit inside four sentences.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">DID YOU KNOW?</span><strong>Many readers of an English page are not native English speakers. Visitors to Japan gather travel information in their mother tongues too, and English is a second or third language for many of its readers</strong> — Plain English is not a courtesy to natives; it is a design decision that treats English as the world’s common language.</div>

<h2>The other side — when going English exhausts the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The swamp of perfectionism</strong> — Checking with a native speaker again and again, and by the time the perfect sentences are assembled, the season is over. The standard is "is it misunderstood?," not "is it beautiful?"</li>
<li><strong>The native-speaker myth</strong> — A native English speaker is not automatically an expert in travel copy. Clumsy English written by an innkeeper who knows what travelers worry about can bring more bookings.</li>
<li><strong>Dumping everything on machine translation</strong> — A translation app hands over vocabulary, not culture. For mirin or the customs of courtesy, the app’s dictionary holds none of the inn’s circumstances.</li>
<li><strong>The weathering of information</strong> — An English page left with old rates, breakfast times, or renovation dates is more serious than an outdated Japanese page. In English, "forgot to update" is discovered late.</li>
<li><strong>The degree of honesty</strong> — Writing the three points of disappointment is sincere; let it tip into a list of weaknesses and bookings fall. State the fact, and add the reason and the alternative ("There is no elevator. All rooms are on the ground floor.").</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is, tonight, to rewrite only your English title and your first sentence. Location, you, facts — swap it for one sentence containing just those three. The ten rules are enough to add in order, once that one sentence is working.</p>

<div class="note">This article is intended for sharing ideas. The English expressions are reference examples showing the approach. To check effects, record the English page’s views and conversion for the 30 days before and after a change, with the same counting method.</div>

<li><strong>在描述中添加“访客指南”</strong> — 在该地区可以做什么。这就是我们和其他住宿的区别。</li>
<li><strong>匹配标题、第一张照片和描述的开头</strong> — 只有当这三者同时存在时，才能实现“清晰”。</li>
<li><strong>只查看一次原生</strong> — 通过一项母语测试（不要追求完美）。</li>
</ol>

<h2>为什么现在值得关注？</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>客人期待的变化：</strong>《日本旅馆的英文房源清单 - 10条规则》 客人的期望与五年前相比发生了变化。过去“有了更好”的服务，如今已变成“没有就难以被选择”。适应这些变化，是住宿设施持续经营的前提。</li>
<li><strong>与地区关系：</strong>在网上吸引顾客的努力增加了整个地区的价值，而不仅仅是旅馆本身的价值。通过与当地生产者和周边商户合作，旅馆的行动会成为“当地故事”。社区共同参与，才更容易长期持续。</li>
<li><strong>不增加员工负担：</strong>新举措不应增加员工负担。通过建立机制，用一张表或每次15分钟的学习来运转，就能在不增加员工负担的情况下持续改进。</li>
</ul>

<h2>避免失败的3个要点</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>从小处开始——先做好一件事：</strong>一开始就想全部改变，往往难以持续。先从一件事开始。可以从一间客房、一个货架或一句话开始；即使效果不理想，影响也小，更容易调整。</li>
<li><strong>记录——用一张表看清楚：</strong>把行动记录在一张表里，效果就能被看见。你会知道哪些做法有效、哪些需要调整，记录也会成为下一轮改进的依据。</li>
<li><strong>持续——每周一次，每次15分钟：</strong>新行动需要持续。每周留出15分钟，逐渐形成习惯，最后才会成为团队文化。</li>
</ol>
