11:T1d2a,<p>The most common misunderstanding in consultations on multilingual support is the assumption that "every sign in the building must go into so many languages." The cost of making them, and the labor of retranslating at every change, swell until nothing ever begins. This article starts from the opposite end. The essence of multilingual support is not increasing the number of languages, but <strong>letting guests move safely and without getting lost even when they cannot read</strong>. And in Japan's inns, the symbol that is the origin of this way of thinking is already hanging in the corridor.</p>

<h2>The green running man — the birth of a symbol beyond words</h2>

<p>Many people assume that the "running man" mark at emergency exits is a foreign design; it was born in Japan. 1972 in Osaka, 1973 in Kumamoto — two department-store fires, each claiming more than a hundred lives. One of the causes that swelled the toll was "the exits were hard to find." To someone who cannot read it, a kanji signboard is just a board. From this lesson, the Fire and Disaster Management Agency solicited designs through a public competition (more than 3,000 entries arrived), and with the graphic designer Yukio Ota at the center, the present "white running man on green" was refined. This running man was later adopted into international standards, and keeps running at emergency exits around the world. A symbol born in Japan from two fires — one that crosses language.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>The emergency-exit pictogram was shaped into its present form — through a Fire and Disaster Management Agency public competition (over 3,000 entries), with designer Yukio Ota at the center — after the department-store fires of 1972 in Osaka and 1973 in Kumamoto (each with over 100 deaths), and was later adopted into international standards. It is a design born in Japan</strong> — The origin of multilingual signage lies not in more translation but in the pursuit of "design you understand without reading."</div>

<p>What the running man teaches is the first principle of multilingual signage: <strong>information about life and wayfinding sits one step before language.</strong> Before translating the words, can it be conveyed by figure and color? The signage of an inn that has done this accomplishes, with a single picture, work worth several languages of translation.</p>

<h2>Tier 1 — Safety and emergency (required): icons first, words added after</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Emergency exits, fire equipment, evacuation routes</strong> — This is the territory of law and of lives. The green of the exit, the red of the fire equipment, the arrows of evacuation. These are already built in an international grammar. What the inn should do is mount the prescribed emergency lights and signs correctly — not improvise substitutes.</li>
<li><strong>"In an emergency, come to the front desk"</strong> — In Japanese and English, in the guest rooms and a visible spot in the building. The phone number in digits (0, 9, and so on).</li>
<li><strong>Icons first, words added after</strong> — The iron rule of Tier 1. Can a guest move without reading? The running man proves it.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Tier 2 — The guest’s flow (recommended): Japanese and English, plus icons and arrows</h2>

<p>The front desk, toilets, elevator, breakfast room, Wi-Fi, the steps of check-in. The signs at the places guests get lost take two languages — Japanese and English — with icons attached. Two languages are enough because icons reach even guests who cannot read English (and a door lined with five languages is, if anything, harder to read). Add the "where next" arrow. The arrow is the only word that means the same thing in every language. From the front desk to the room, from the room to the breakfast room. A chain of arrows can stand in for the first guidance.</p>

<h2>Tier 3 — Appeal and experience (optional): later, by budget and demand</h2>

<p>Area guides, explanations of experiences, the inn's story. This layer is decided by each inn's budget and by the actual makeup of its guests. Inns with many Chinese-speaking guests add simplified and traditional characters; inns with continued bookings from Korea add Hangul — choosing by "who comes," not "how many languages." With Tiers 1 and 2 complete, Tier 3 can all be postponed. An inn that guarantees safety and flow is, in just two languages, fully "multilingual."</p>

<h2>The other side of multilingual signage</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The pitfall of machine translation</strong> — Put signage translation through a check for reversed meaning. As many people stop puzzled at strange English notices as foreigners laugh at strange direct-translated Japanese.</li>
<li><strong>Symbols change meaning with culture</strong> — Because there are regions where the green cross is taboo, the symbol for evacuation areas was redesigned (this too was Mr. Ota's work). Pictures that speak to everyone in the world are, in fact, few. Give icons a second look as well.</li>
<li><strong>Signage that does not rely on sight</strong> — Room numbers in braille, floors you can tell by touch. Assume Tier 1 means "visible symbols" and you leave behind guests who cannot see.</li>
<li><strong>The cost of updates</strong> — Make the signs that change — prices, hours — multilingual, and every change spawns a translation. One effective split: what changes stays in Japanese plus digits plus icons; only what never changes goes multilingual.</li>
<li><strong>Do not improvise emergency signage</strong> — Emergency lights and evacuation signs are equipment governed by fire-safety standards. If they look old or faint, replace them with standard products — do not patch with design.</li>
<li><strong>The honest reason for the five-language signboard</strong> — Languages lined up to advertise "we can serve you" backfire without the substance (staff and guides that can actually respond). Let the number of languages on the signboard equal the inn's actual strength.</li>
</ul>

<p>The two fires of the 1970s taught that poor signage takes lives, and out of that came "design you understand without reading." Your inn's multilingual work is best considered in the same order: first safety (Tier 1), next wayfinding (Tier 2), last appeal (Tier 3). Start small: tomorrow morning, walk the building once and write down three spots where a guest could lose their way and only Japanese appears. Translation can come after that.</p>
