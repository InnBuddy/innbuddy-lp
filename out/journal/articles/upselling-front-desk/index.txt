1b:T1bfd,<p>Upselling may be the most misunderstood word in a property's vocabulary. Hard selling. Pushy pitches. Soliciting business right after arrival. All of them have a bad reputation. Yet guests of inns that carefully design the front desk's "one more push" leave behind reviews saying they "received good information." This article reframes upselling — not as "selling" but as "showing the best choice" — and organizes the craft together with the full arc of the science behind it.</p>

<h2>Does the arrangement of options change the choice?</h2>

<p>First, the science. In 1982, three researchers — Huber, Payne, and Puto — reported a strange phenomenon in a paper on consumer choice. When there are two options, adding a "clearly inferior third option" makes the option that beats it more likely to be chosen: the decoy effect. Across six product categories, including cars and restaurants, adding the inferior option raised the target option's share by an average of roughly 9 percentage points.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">RESEARCH</span><strong>In the 1982 experiment (Huber, Payne, and Puto), adding a "clearly inferior third option" to two options raised the likelihood of the target option being chosen by an average of around 9 percentage points (the decoy effect)</strong> — Human choice is shaped not only by the content of the options but by their "arrangement." Menu design is itself an instrument.</div>

<p>Here comes the important sequel. In 2014, a large-scale study attempting to replicate the effect reported that under conditions close to reality, it mostly disappears. And in the same year, the three original authors themselves published a paper titled "Let's be honest about the attraction effect," acknowledging the effect's limits. In other words, the story that people can be moved simply by clever arrangement did not last. What this history teaches an inn is simple: a rigged menu will eventually be seen through. Only honest menus last.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">RESEARCH</span><strong>The decoy effect largely disappeared in replication attempts under realistic conditions, and the original authors themselves published a paper in 2014 acknowledging the effect's limits</strong> — Choice design needs no tricks. What it needs is to line up the genuine best choice for the guest.</div>

<h2>The shop that never created a "small"</h2>

<p>An example often told in the history of option design is the size naming of a certain coffee chain. The small shop that the future founder opened in Seattle in 1986 (which later became the present brand) had three sizes: Short (8 oz), Tall (12 oz), and Grande (16 oz) — names inspired by Italian café culture. When the larger Venti (Italian for "twenty") was eventually added, four sizes would no longer fit on the menu board, and the smallest, Short, was dropped from display. As a result, this shop's menu has no name equivalent to "small"; the smallest size is "Tall." Whatever the intention, a menu whose smallest name means "big" has been cited ever since in sales training as a model of the power of arrangement. The lesson: names and order themselves create the guest's baseline. On your inn's price list, which plan sits at the top? That becomes the guest's idea of "normal."</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>A certain coffee chain's size names began with the 1986 shop's three sizes (Short/Tall/Grande); when a larger size was added, the smallest size was dropped from the menu, making "Tall" the smallest</strong> — The structure of never creating a "small" is a model of how names and order create a baseline. An inn's price list is the same instrument.</div>

<h2>The front-desk move — 3 elements and 3 classics</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Timing: when they are not rushed</strong> — Right after arrival, while unpacking, once they have caught their breath. Approaching someone in a hurry makes even good information sound like "selling."</li>
<li><strong>Words: in the form of information</strong> — "A room on the ocean side is available tonight; we can arrange it if you would like." "Breakfast features a local vegetable soup as its main dish." Delivered not as a recommendation but as a report of facts.</li>
<li><strong>One option only</strong> — A, B, or C breeds choice fatigue. At the front desk, only proposals that fit "this one, or stay as you are" get through.</li>
</ul>

<p>Keep the three classics in mind as well: (1) room upgrades (only when a room is vacant — a move with a high price tag and almost no variable cost), (2) adding breakfast (even one item, even one person), (3) late checkout (within limits that do not conflict with the cleaning schedule for the next guests). Each is "the genuinely best option this inn can actually offer right now" — which is exactly why it works as information.</p>

<h2>The line against pushiness</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Make it easy to decline</strong> — Propose once. If declined, "Understood," and that is the end. The second proposal is where selling begins.</li>
<li><strong>Transparent pricing</strong> — Upgrade fees should be justifiable against market rates. An ambiguous charge will be published in a review.</li>
<li><strong>Honest about inventory</strong> — "Only a few left" is a phrase to use only when it is true. One lie turns every proposal into selling.</li>
<li><strong>No late-night proposals</strong> — Upselling to a tired guest or a late arrival is broken timing. The next morning's breakfast suggestion is natural.</li>
<li><strong>Accounting for incentives</strong> — Making one desk clerk compete on sales numbers turns proposals into coercion. Build a framework that does not judge the quality of proposals by numbers alone.</li>
</ul>

<p>Finally, turn the opening question upside down. Does your inn have a "best choice" tonight? A room with a view of the sea, breakfast at its seasonal peak, a quiet corner room on a silent floor. Completing check-in without mentioning it is a loss for the guest and a lost opportunity for the inn. Start small: write one line you can deliver tonight on a piece of paper, and place it at the desk. The honesty the decoy-effect researchers ultimately arrived at — line up only the genuine best. That is everything about the front desk's "one more push."</p>

<h2>1. 为什么追加销售——客人不知道他们的最佳选择</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>客人只有在看到“最佳选择”时才会了解到：</strong>客人不知道他们的“最佳住宿选择”。住宿有面向大海的房间、大房间、含早餐的方案等“最佳选择”。只有当向客人展示“最佳选择”时，他们才会认为“我想要购买它”。追加销售意味着“展示最佳选择”。</li>
<li><strong>建议让您安心，不会亏钱：</strong>通过鼓励追加销售，客人可以放心，他们不会遭受损失。当被告知有海边的房间时，客人会放心地入住海边的房间。追加销售是提供信息以避免损失。</li>
<li><strong>追加销售是一种“服务”：</strong>追加销售不是一种推销，而是一种服务。通过“向他们展示最佳选择”，客人会觉得自己获得了很好的信息。追加销售是一项提高客人满意度的服务。</li>
</ul>

<h2>2.三要素——时机、言语、一选</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>时间 - 当您不赶时间时（抵达后/整理行李时）：</strong>追加销售的最佳时机是当您的客人不着急的时候。在客人休息时，例如刚到达或正在整理行李时与客人交谈，客人会认为这是“好信息”，而不是“推销”。如果你在匆忙时向他们喊话，客人会觉得你在推销。</li>
<li><strong>文字 — “我们有很多客人在……”形式（信息，而不是推销）：</strong>追加销售短语的表达形式如下：“我们有很多客人……”例如：“许多客人喜欢面朝大海的房间。”“用当地蔬菜制成的早餐汤很受欢迎。”通过将信息作为信息而不是销售宣传来呈现，客人会将其视为“好信息”而不是“销售宣传”。</li>
<li><strong>一种选择——仅推荐一种（避免选择疲劳）：</strong>我只推荐一项追加销售。如果你提出A、B、C等多个选项，客人会很难选择。通过仅推荐一种选项，客人将有两种选择：“我会选择它，或者我会跳过它。”这将使客人更容易选择。</li>
