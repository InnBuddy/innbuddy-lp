11:T21db,<p>How should an inn handle memories of disasters and tragedies? Is leaving them unmentioned the respectful choice? Some communities feel the opposite: allowing memories to fade is the greatest harm. Drawing on the history of what the world calls dark tourism and approaches developed by inns in Japan’s disaster-affected areas, this article considers the craft of handling memory.</p><h2>The term emerged in academic writing in 1996</h2><p>The first use of “dark tourism” is generally attributed to a 1996 paper by researchers Malcolm Foley and John Lennon. Their book “Dark Tourism,” published in 2000, helped establish it as an academic term for tourism to places associated with death or disaster. Definitions vary, including the presentation and consumption of actual sites of death and disaster. Of particular interest is their distinction between two types of visitors: those with a special interest or personal connection, and the majority without such a connection. Most people visiting places of tragedy are not simply thoughtless spectators: they want to know. The question therefore concerns not only visitors’ attitudes, but how hosts tell the story.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>The term “dark tourism” first appeared in Foley and Lennon’s academic writing in 1996 and became established as a concept through their 2000 book</strong> — Visitors include those with a special interest or personal connection and the majority without one. The focus here is the quality of the host’s storytelling, rather than visitor etiquette alone.</div><h2>Chernobyl: from 8,000 visitors to more than 100,000</h2><p>Perhaps the best-known place in this field is Chernobyl in Ukraine. Much of the exclusion zone around the site of the 1986 nuclear accident opened to tours from 2011. Visitor numbers were around 8,000 in 2015, but exceeded 100,000 in 2019, following the popularity of an international television drama about the accident. Such a jump demonstrates how quickly media can awaken interest in memory. At the same time, the quality of guides and the arrangements for receiving visitors have helped determine whether that interest becomes learning or spectacle.</p><h2>In Japan, a hotel itself became a memorial site</h2><p>Japan’s examples are more immediate and specific. The Tarō Kankō Hotel in Tarō, Miyako City, Iwate Prefecture, was flooded up to its fourth floor by a tsunami said to have exceeded 17 meters in height. Its two lowest floors were swept away, but the building did not collapse. In March 2014, the city purchased it for preservation as a tsunami memorial site. Visitors who book a disaster-preparedness guide can tour the interior and view tsunami footage filmed at the hotel on the day of the disaster. The accommodation tells the story through its own building.</p><p>Minamisanriku Hotel Kanyo in Minamisanriku, Miyagi Prefecture, has taken on a similar role in another way. Hotel staff operate a “storyteller bus,” guiding visitors around disaster-affected sites in the town. Its adult fare of 500 yen expresses its philosophy: encourage people to ride so that memories do not fade, rather than maximize profit from a tourism product. The initiative won the Grand Prize at the 2017 Japan Tourism Awards. In Kesennuma, a memorial museum and preserved disaster site opened in 2019, retaining the damaged former building of a high school; storyteller guides continue to lead visitors. Inns and hotels thus play a central role in dark tourism in Japan’s disaster-affected areas.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>In 2014, Miyako City purchased the Tarō Kankō Hotel, flooded up to its fourth floor, to preserve it as a tsunami memorial site. Minamisanriku Hotel Kanyo operates a staff-led “storyteller bus,” with an adult fare of 500 yen, and won the Grand Prize at the 2017 Japan Tourism Awards</strong> — Accommodation itself can become a memorial site and its staff storytellers. Preserving and transmitting memory can become a core activity of hospitality.</div><p>Looking further back, Hiroshima stands near the beginning of this lineage. The Atomic Bomb Dome was built in 1915 as the Hiroshima Prefectural Commercial Exhibition Hall, designed by Czech architect Jan Letzel. In December 1996, it was inscribed as a World Cultural Heritage site under the name “Hiroshima Peace Memorial.” Preserved by postwar citizens as a legacy of tragedy, it is among the early examples showing the world how maintaining a place of sorrow can become an appeal to the future.</p><h2>Five approaches for an inn handling memory</h2><ul><li><strong>Receive the story from the community</strong> — Before independently telling stories of disaster or tragedy, connect with local memorial facilities or storyteller groups. A shared framework for telling the story requires community agreement.</li><li><strong>Guide guests to quiet places</strong> — Stone markers, memorials, traces of a tsunami, relocated buildings. Add a route for quiet visits to such places.
