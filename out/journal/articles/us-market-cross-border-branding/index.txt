1b:T180f,<p>In talk of the US market, the numbers — "millions of travelers" — tend to run ahead. But what matters to a property’s work is not the count; it is <strong>the depth</strong>. This article organizes the process of "translating" a Japanese property’s story for guests from the United States — the three gaps that never make it to the surface, and the four steps.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>By Japan National Tourism Organization estimates, visitors to Japan from the United States in 2024 reached about 2.72 million, a record high for a single year (a leading market after China and Taiwan). And according to an annual report in which JTB compiled the Japan Tourism Agency’s "Survey on Inbound Consumer Trends," travel spending per American visitor (leisure travelers) in 2024 was ¥332,000 (up 11.8% year on year) — about 1.5 times the average for all visitors to Japan (¥227,000) (as of writing)</strong> — A market to be told not by headcount, but by spending per traveler.</div>

<h2>The 3 translation gaps</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Gap 1: language</strong> — The difference between literal translation and localization. "Omotenashi," as the word itself, does not land in the United States.</li>
<li><strong>Gap 2: values</strong> — The Japanese emphasis on "craft and detail" against the American weight on "experience and story." The same fact arrives with different mass.</li>
<li><strong>Gap 3: visuals</strong> — Japanese "empty space and restraint" against American "boldness." Staging that is "just right" in Japan can look "empty" over there.</li>
</ul>

<p>None of the three is a problem of "can you speak English." It is a problem of <strong>different maps of expectation</strong> lying beyond the words.</p>

<h2>Translating "omotenashi" — turning concepts into actions</h2>

<p>The method of translation is <strong>converting concepts into concrete actions</strong>.</p>

<ul>
<li>✕ "We provide omotenashi hospitality" → ✓ "A handwritten welcome note carrying your name"</li>
<li>✕ "Impeccable cleanliness" → ✓ "Bedding changed daily; bathrooms checked three times a day"</li>
</ul>

<p>What guests from the United States trust is not beautiful words of praise but <strong>evidence of action</strong>. "Handwritten," "daily," "three times" — what can be counted crosses the language barrier.</p>

<h2>The 4-step process</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Understanding the audience</strong> — What guests from the US search for and expect from "a stay in Japan." The assumptions on the Japanese side and the actual expectations often differ.</li>
<li><strong>Message mapping</strong> — Build a one-to-one table of "Japanese value → American phrasing."</li>
<li><strong>Content production</strong> — Based on the map, produce the photos, video, and English copy.</li>
<li><strong>Test and learn</strong> — While watching click rates and conversion on the booking sites and your own site, rotate the copy.</li>
</ol>

<p>For properties that still see few American guests and cannot do the interviews of Step 1, a substitute source of information is already at hand: <strong>the English reviews</strong>. The American guests who have already stayed have written what they found value in. A property that can fill the left column of the mapping table (the Japanese values) but whose right column (the American phrasing) sits empty can begin by copying the English expressions out of the reviews (see the article on reading inbound reviews).</p>

<h2>The other side — when translation exhausts the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>"The American guest" does not exist</strong> — A vast and diverse country. Urban and rural, age and income, the purpose of the trip — expectations differ. The fixed idea "Americans are like this" breeds a failure deeper than the language gap.</li>
<li><strong>An over-recreated story breaks on the floor</strong> — If the actions written about are not happening on the ground, it comes back in the English reviews as "not as advertised." The quality of the translation is a translation of the property’s actual strength.</li>
<li><strong>Leaving the English page alone</strong> — A property updating only the Japanese while the English page ages shows the US market "the property of the past," forever.</li>
<li><strong>Misreading the tests</strong> — At a small property the numbers wobble. Bookings move with the season, the exchange rate, flight schedules. Do not change copy on the strength of one month’s win or loss.</li>
<li><strong>The limits of machine translation</strong> — The meaning gets through; it does not become "writing that gets chosen." Build into the first budget the cost of having a person with a feel for English do the final pass.</li>
</ul>

<p>Translation here is not "translation" but "re-creation." Re-create the core, in a form that understands the target culture. The "one line" that stays in the head is the deliverable of cross-border branding.</p>

<p>The first step is, this week, to pull three "pieces of action evidence" out of your English reviews. If there are none, read the reviews of American properties on the major booking sites. The first three rows of the mapping table fill themselves from those three.</p>
