11:T1db9,<p>First, a proposed experiment. Show your inn's booking page to someone who does not know the inn. Have them close it after three seconds, then ask: "What kind of inn was it?" Will an answer come back in a single line? Many pages are beautiful, yet no answer comes. There are photos; the design is tidy; but in three seconds, "what kind of inn" does not come across. This "three seconds" has a basis, not just a feeling.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">RESEARCH</span><strong>In Google and SOASTA's 2017 analysis (Think with Google), the probability of bounce rises 32% as a mobile page's load time stretches from one second to three — 90% at five seconds, 123% at ten</strong> — The first fork between staying and leaving happens inside three seconds.</div>

<h2>There are two three-seconds — loading, and understanding</h2>

<p>What Google's research speaks of is the speed at which the page "opens" — call it the loading three seconds. But a property's page has another three seconds: the speed at which the opened page "comes across" — the understanding three seconds. However fast the loading, if what kind of inn it is does not come across, the guest likewise leaves in three seconds. And while load-speed improvement depends on contracts with vendors, the understanding three seconds is the part an inn can fix tonight, on its own. That is this article's main battlefield.</p>

<h2>Three things to convey in three seconds — for whom, what, where</h2>

<p>What the understanding three seconds must convey is the following three. <strong>For whom</strong> — people who love mountains, families with children, visitors coming for a hot-spring cure. <strong>What</strong> — coffee brewed with mountain water, an irori-hearth dinner, a room that is almost too quiet. <strong>Where</strong> — on the edge of a gorge, atop an island slope, in a snow-country village. Here is a sentence with all three: "For people who love mountains, an inn where you taste coffee brewed with mountain water, in nature." A page where that one sentence enters the head within three seconds earns the right to have everything else (rates, facilities, access) read. Conversely, a page vague on for-whom, what, and where will be closed as "a page that has nothing to do with me," however beautiful its photos.</p>

<h2>Three points of design — one photo, one sentence, one CTA</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Photo — the first image alone tells all three</strong> — The second slide and beyond are seen only by people the first one interested. The first photo must show "for whom, what, where" in a single frame — not an anonymous floor plan of a room, but a photo in which the inn's one line is visible.</li>
<li><strong>Copy — one sentence tells all three</strong> — Not the owner's greeting, not a facilities description. At the very top of the page, one sentence like "For people who love mountains, an inn where you taste coffee brewed with mountain water, in nature." If you cannot write the sentence, that is the sign that the inn's one line is not yet narrowed.</li>
<li><strong>CTA — the "next step" is visible in three seconds</strong> — "See rooms," "Book," "Learn more." The interested person's next move must be visible as a button. A button is not something to make people search for; it is something to place where the eye lands.</li>
</ul>

<h2>How to run the three-second test</h2>

<p>Whether the page conveys itself in three seconds is not something you can judge — you know the inn, so you read with corrections. So rely on other people. (1) Show the page for three seconds only to someone who does not know the inn (a staff member's friend, family). (2) Have them close it and ask, "What kind of inn was it?" "Who would want to go?" (3) If 3 out of 5 people return an answer resembling for-whom, what, and where, the page passes. If 2 or fewer do, fix the photo or the copy. Showing it to a child works surprisingly well — a page that does not reach a child is a mixture of jargon and self-praise. Run the test right after rebuilding the page, then once a quarter. A five-minute inn habit.</p>

<h2>The loading three seconds — three things the inn can guard</h2>

<p>As for load speed, what an inn can do is in fact simple. (1) Don't let the first photo weigh too much (giant images and autoplaying video eat the first three seconds). (2) Don't make a page where music or sound starts on its own. (3) Booking functions and maps are heavy — don't cram them into the first screen. Detailed measurement is the production company's job, but these three come across as instructions from the inn's side.</p>

<h2>The other side of three-second design</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The trap of beauty</strong> — Design polish and "what kind of inn" are different things. The more beautiful the page, the more easily the one line you want to convey gets buried. Beauty is a story for after it comes across.</li>
<li><strong>Old information loses trust in three seconds</strong> — Last year's rates, a restaurant under closure, a room photo five years old. However fast the loading and the understanding, stale content gets an instant exit.</li>
<li><strong>Price consistency with the OTAs</strong> — If your own page is more expensive than the booking sites, the hard-won three seconds are spent only on comparison. The role of your own page is to present "the reason to choose you."</li>
<li><strong>A parade of pop-ups</strong> — When cookie consent, notification prompts, and newsletter offers pile up, the three seconds end in admin. The first screen's only job is to deliver the inn's one line.</li>
<li><strong>Don't chase the bounce rate alone</strong> — A bounce from a guest who reads slowly and books is, in fact, normal. What to watch is "does it pass the three-second test" and "the flow into booking and inquiry." Read numbers in combination.</li>
</ul>

<p>An inn actually has three three-seconds. The three seconds when its thumbnail is chosen — or passed over — in OTA search results; the three seconds of first impression when a guest stands at the entrance; and this article's three seconds, when the page comes across. All three ask the same question: can your inn be said in a single line? Start small: tonight, look at your own page on your phone for three seconds, and check whether you can say, in one line, what kind of inn it is.</p>

12:T190d,<p>登陆页面设计<strong>“前3秒”要传达什么</strong>许多旅馆认为登陆页面是“美丽的页面”，并用美丽的照片和设计来创建它。然而，从不同的角度来看，着陆页重要的是它在“前3秒”传达的信息。客人在 3 秒内查看登陆页面并决定该住宿是否适合他们。如果3秒内未传达信息，客人将离开。在这篇文章中，我们将根据你在前3秒想要传达的内容来组织登陆页面的设计，而不是一个漂亮的页面。</p>

<h2>1.为什么是3秒？客人3秒内做出决定。</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>客人在3秒内决定该住宿是否适合自己：</strong>客人在 3 秒内查看登陆页面并决定该住宿是否适合他们。如果客人在 3 秒内感觉不合适，他们就会离开。登陆页面最重要的作用就是在3秒内传达出它适合你。</li>
<li><strong>如果3秒内未沟通，则离开：</strong>如果您的目标网页在 3 秒内未传达该消息，您的客人就会离开。即使你有漂亮的照片和设计，如果客人不能在3秒内弄清楚这是一家什么样的旅馆，他们就会觉得这对他们来说无所谓并离开。</li>
<li><strong>3秒内传达三件事：“谁、什么、在哪里”：</strong>您可以在 3 秒内传达三件事：谁、什么、以及在哪里。例如：“为热爱山地的人设计的旅馆，可以在大自然中享用山水冲泡的咖啡。”通过能够在短短三秒内传达谁、什么、在哪里，客人会觉得这里很适合他们。</li>
</ul>

<h2>2. 3秒传达设计——3分：照片、文案、CTA</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>照片 — 一张照片可以说明人物、事件和地点：</strong>着陆页上的照片应该是清晰显示“人物、内容和地点”的照片。例如：人们在山水里冲泡咖啡的照片、人们在大自然中享用咖啡的照片等。通过从一张照片中辨别出谁、什么、在哪里，客人可以在短短 3 秒内感受到它适合他们。</li>
<li><strong>文案——用一句话讲述“谁、什么、在哪里”的文案：</strong>着陆页副本应该是一句话副本，用一句话解释“谁、什么和哪里”。例如：“为爱山人士设计的旅馆，可以在大自然中享用山水冲泡的咖啡。”通过一句文案就能明白“谁、什么、在哪里”，让客人在短短3秒内就能感受到“很适合自己”。</li>
<li><strong>CTA — 一个在 3 秒内告诉您下一步该做什么的按钮：</strong>着陆页上的 CTA（号召性用语）应该是一个按钮，告诉您 3 秒内下一步该做什么。例如：“查看空房情况”、“进行预订”、“查看详细信息”等。通过 CTA，客人可以知道下一步该做什么，并且无需离开即可继续下一步操作。</li>
</ol>

<h2>3、操作——测试3秒内能否传达信息，并持续改进。</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>测试3秒能否传输：</strong>测试你的着陆页能否在3秒内交付。向你的员工或朋友展示登陆页面3秒钟，并询问他们这是什么类型的旅馆。如果你无法在3秒内判断出这是一家什么样的客栈，我们会完善照片和文案。</li>
<li><strong>改进照片、文案和 CTA：</strong>改进您的着陆页涉及改进照片、文案和 CTA。照片将改进为显示“谁、什么、在哪里”的照片，文案将改进为显示“谁、什么、在哪里”的一句话文案，CTA 将改进为显示“下一步做什么”的按钮。</li>
<li><strong>登陆页面不是“漂亮的页面”，而是“可以在3秒内传达的页面”：</strong>登陆页面不是“漂亮的页面”，而是“可以在3秒内传达的页面”。即使你有漂亮的照片或设计，如果不能在 3 秒内传达出来，客人也会离开。请以3秒传达信息为首要设计。</li>
</ul>
