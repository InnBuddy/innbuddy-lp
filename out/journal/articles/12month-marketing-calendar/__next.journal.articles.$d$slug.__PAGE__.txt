11:T1bf2,<p>Marketing is <strong>not "inspiration" but "a calendar."</strong> Yet most property calendars stop moving within three months. Trying to fill in twelve months of boxes in January, only the cherry-blossom and autumn-leaf squares take color, and the rest stays white. The reason it stops moving is almost exactly one thing: <strong>you went out to "make" the calendar.</strong> It is not something to make. It is something to gather. This article is the foundational method for organizing a year onto one sheet.</p>

<h2>The saijiki — seasonal information is "an accumulation of observation"</h2>

<p>In the world of haiku there is a guidebook called the <strong>saijiki</strong> — an index that organizes kigo, the seasonal words, in seasonal order, and poets opened it before composing about "the now." Behind the seasonal words lie the fine calendar divisions of the 24 sekki and 72 kō (the calendar world’s detailed story is handled by <a href="#idea-season-english-24sekki">Tell Seasonal Greetings in English - Ideas for Conveying 24 Sekki and 72 Ko in English</a>).</p>

<p>What makes the saijiki interesting is that it is not somebody’s inspirational plan but <strong>a record of observation stacked up over long years</strong>. The day the paddies fill with water; the night the insect voices begin; the morning the wind turns cool. Seasonal information was gathered not in meeting rooms but on the soil. Put another way, the saijiki is <strong>a calendar of "what is here now," written on and on for hundreds of years</strong>.</p>

<h2>Gathering in 3 layers — season, events, demand</h2>

<p>A property’s twelve months can be gathered with the same posture. Line up the past year’s booking data and the region’s schedule, and sort into three layers. That is all.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Season</strong> — Divide into five seasons: cherry blossoms (March–April), fresh green (May), summer (June–August), autumn leaves (October–November), snow (December–February).</li>
<li><strong>Events</strong> — Festivals, fireworks, marathons, harvest festivals, brewery open houses. Write out everything within 30 minutes of the property.</li>
<li><strong>Demand</strong> — Inbound (spring and autumn), domestic families (summer and year-end), weekday business, weekend couples. Color-code the past data’s guest-nights by month.</li>
</ol>

<p>These three layers are not a matter of inspiration. <strong>They are the work of rearranging records.</strong> The booking data is, precisely, the source material of your property’s saijiki.</p>

<h2>Decide on just one protagonist</h2>

<p>Twelve months across, three layers down. And for each month, decide <strong>just one "protagonist of the month."</strong> May is "fresh green and mountain vegetables"; August is "the sea and fireworks"; November is "autumn leaves and the harvest." The criterion for choosing is one: <strong>"what can only be done in that month."</strong> What can be done anytime does not become the protagonist.</p>

<p>Haiku has the manner of "one kigo per poem." Just as stacking seasonal words clouds the poem’s expression, <strong>the month’s protagonist, the moment you set two, stops being either</strong>.</p>

<p>Once the single protagonist is set, things start coming together with almost uncanny ease. The photograph to take settles to one (<a href="#hotel-photos-90">"The Photo Is 90%": What Bookable Hotel Photos Have in Common</a>), the pricing matched to the demand peak turns concrete (<a href="#revpar-18x-dynamic-pricing">RevPAR Up 1.8×: Pricing From "Gut Feeling" to "Design"</a>), and what to post on social shrinks to one photograph and one sentence. The calendar’s greatest usefulness lies in reducing the amount you send.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">DID YOU KNOW?</span><strong>In the world of haiku, the manner of "avoiding season-overlap (kigasanari)" — narrowing each poem to a single kigo — has long been cherished</strong> — The month’s protagonist is the same. The moment you choose two, both the message and the photograph thin to half each.</div>

<h2>Operation — always keep two months ahead ready</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Always prepare two months ahead.</strong> At the last minute, neither the photographs nor the words make it in time. For overseas guests, the principle is to move even earlier — there is a way of thinking that starts 8 weeks out (<a href="#seasonal-inbound-demand">Seasonal Inbound Demand: The Basics of When to Prepare What</a>).</li>
<li>Once a month, a 15-minute check confirms "the protagonist of the month after next." One person in charge is enough.</li>
<li>Stick the calendar <strong>where the front desk can see it.</strong> The ones who need to know the protagonist are not the PR officer but the whole staff. A breakfast dish, a word at send-off — both can carry the month’s protagonist.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The other side — when the calendar exhausts the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>"Filling it in" becomes the purpose</strong> — The aim is not filling but deciding on one. The posts of months filled out of difficulty show the writer’s struggling face in the photographs. Months that stay empty: do not force them full.</li>
<li><strong>Leaning on spring and autumn</strong> — A calendar that leans on cherry blossoms and autumn leaves only strengthens the concentration of demand. Look again at the three layers — is there no protagonist to unearth in summer and winter?</li>
<li><strong>The illusion of "same as last year"</strong> — The past year is, after all, one sample. Festivals do get canceled. Before writing it in, confirm this year’s holding with the organizer.</li>
<li><strong>The fiscal-year trap</strong> — A property’s year is cut neither at April nor at January; cutting it at the break of the peak season fits reality better. Making next year’s sheet in the off-season is the practical move.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is to line up the past year’s booking data by month and color the demand peaks. Next, annotate the cause of each peak (a festival, a holiday run, the autumn leaves). With that alone, the first page of your property’s saijiki exists. After that, you only keep adding observations, year after year.</p>

<div class="note">This article is intended for sharing ideas. The saijiki, kigo, and the manners of season-overlap are introduced as traditional general knowledge of haikai poetry. Please confirm event schedules with the organizers. Preparation lead times are one example.</div>

<h2>为什么现在值得关注？</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>客人期待的变化：</strong>与五年前相比，《旅馆的 12 个月 - 即用型营销日历》改变了现在客人的期望。过去“有了更好”的服务，如今已变成“没有就难以被选择”。适应这些变化，是住宿设施持续经营的前提。</li>
<li><strong>与地区关系：</strong>在网上吸引顾客的努力增加了整个地区的价值，而不仅仅是旅馆本身的价值。通过与当地生产者和周边商户合作，旅馆的行动会成为“当地故事”。社区共同参与，才更容易长期持续。</li>
<li><strong>不增加员工负担：</strong>新举措不应增加员工负担。通过建立机制，用一张表或每次15分钟的学习来运转，就能在不增加员工负担的情况下持续改进。</li>
</ul>

<h2>避免失败的3个要点</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>从小处开始——先做好一件事：</strong>一开始就想全部改变，往往难以持续。先从一件事开始。可以从一间客房、一个货架或一句话开始；即使效果不理想，影响也小，更容易调整。</li>
<li><strong>记录——用一张表看清楚：</strong>把行动记录在一张表里，效果就能被看见。你会知道哪些做法有效、哪些需要调整，记录也会成为下一轮改进的依据。</li>
<li><strong>持续——每周一次，每次15分钟：</strong>新行动需要持续。每周留出15分钟，逐渐形成习惯，最后才会成为团队文化。</li>
</ol>
