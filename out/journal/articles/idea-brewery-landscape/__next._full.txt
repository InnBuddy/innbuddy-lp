16:T2023,<p>「発酵」は、旅の売り文句としても使いやすい言葉です。しかし発酵の正体は、待つことの技術です。味噌も醤油も酢も、人間にできるのは材料を整えて環境を用意することまで。あとは微生物が、決められた時間をかけて仕事をします。醸造のある風景を宿に呼び込むとは、要するに、この「待つ時間」を見せるということです。</p><h2>麹は、日本の「国菌」です</h2><p>2006年10月12日、日本醸造学会は麹菌をわが国の「国菌」に認定する宣言を行いました。提唱したのは醸造学研究者の一島英治氏（東北大学名誉教授）です。「国鳥」「国花」はあっても、「国菌」を持つ国はいまのところ日本だけとされます。デンプンをブドウ糖に、タンパク質をアミノ酸に変える麹菌の働きが、味噌・醤油・みりん・酒・酢という日本の調味料のほとんどを支えています。その事実への敬意が、この宣言の正体です。</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>麹菌は2006年、日本醸造学会によって日本の「国菌」に認定された</strong> — 国菌を持つ国は世界で日本だけとされる。味噌・醤油・みりん・酒・酢の味のほとんどが、麹菌の働きに由来する。</div><p>宿の食卓でこの話を一行にすると、こうなります。「今朝のお味噌汁は、国菌の仕事です」。少しだけ気恥ずかしいかもしれませんが、これほど正確で、これほど話の広がる一行はほかにありません。</p><h2>味噌の「半年後に送る」は、時間そのものを贈っています</h2><p>味噌作り体験の定番に「仕込んだ味噌を半年後に郵送する」という企画があります。なぜ半年なのでしょうか。米味噌の辛口は6か月から1年かけて熟成するからです（白味噌は数週間〜1か月、麦味噌は数か月、豆味噌は1年以上。塩と麹の配合によって変わります）。ゲストが持ち帰るのは大豆と麹の入った容器ではなく、実は「待つ時間」です。だから送るときに、仕込んだ日の写真と一行を添えると、体験の続きが手紙として届きます。</p><p>醤油はさらに長い時間がかかります。昔ながらの天然醸造は、仕込みから搾りまで早くても半年から1年。寒の候に仕込んだもろみは、夏に活発に発酵し、秋に静まって円やかになります。この年輪のような時間を、宿が自分で作るのは現実的ではありません。醤油は「作る」対象ではなく「蔵で聞く」対象です。近くに醤油蔵があれば、その蔵の履歴と主人の話を一枚の紙にして宿に置きましょう。それだけで、食卓の醤油差しに履歴が生まれます。</p><h2>モデナでは、時間そのものが家の財産でした</h2><p>イタリア・モデナのアチェート・バルサミコ・トラディツィオナーレは、ぶどうの果汁だけを煮詰め、木の樽で長年熟成させる酢です。熟成には、材質の異なる5つほどの木樽（オーク、栗、桜、トネリコ、桑など）が1セットとして使われ、樽を移し替えながら水分を飛ばしていきます。規定は厳格で、最低12年熟成のものだけが「トラディツィオナーレ」を名乗れ、最低25年のものは「エクストラヴェッキオ」と呼ばれます。100ミリリットルで数万円という参考価格は、いってみれば「時間の値段」です。</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>バルサミコ・トラディツィオナーレの熟成は最低12年、エクストラヴェッキオは最低25年</strong> — 材質の異なる木樽を組み替えながら熟成するモデナの家の技術。最後には仕込んだ量の数％まで濃縮される。</div><p>なぜこの話が面白いのでしょうか。樽は「待つことの資産」だからです。日本の味噌樽も、発想としては同じものです。宿に小さな樽をひとつ置き、ゲストの名前と仕込んだ日付を書いた紙を添えましょう。「この味噌が食べ頃になるのは、来年の春です」——再来訪の理由を、仕込んだその日に作れるということです。</p><h2>果実酢と、蔵の匂いを消さないこと</h2><p>果樹園や果物畑のある宿では、果実酢が醸造の入門になります。果物と砂糖と酢を漬けるだけの果実酢は失敗が少なく、季節の果物をその年の瓶に保存する仕組みです。ブルーベリー酢、みかん酢、レモン酢。瓶に年月を書いて並べるだけで、ラウンジが「小さな蔵」になります。子どもと一緒に漬けて、翌年の再来訪で味わう約束にするのもいいでしょう。</p><p>もうひとつ、見落とされがちな資産が「蔵の匂い」です。味噌や醤油の蔵には、発酵の香りが染み込んでいます。無香料に慣れた宿の感覚では消したくなるこの匂いは、醸造のある風景では、消してはいけないものの筆頭です。匂いの正体（麹と熟成）を一枚の紙で説明できれば、匂いは「苦情」ではなく「案内」に変わります。ちなみに、自家の味噌を自慢するところから「手前味噌」という言葉が生まれたとされます。宿が自信を持って出す味噌汁は、まさに手前味噌であるべきものです。</p><h2>衛生・アレルギー・向かない場合</h2><ul><li><strong>衛生</strong> 1b:T202b,<p>“Fermentation” is an easy word to use as a travel selling point. But what fermentation really is, is the craft of waiting. With miso, soy sauce, and vinegar alike, all humans can do is prepare the ingredients and set up the environment. After that, microorganisms do the work, taking the time it takes. Bringing a landscape with a brewery into the inn means, in short, showing this “time of waiting.”</p><h2>Koji is Japan’s “national microorganism”</h2><p>On October 12, 2006, the Brewing Society of Japan issued a declaration recognizing koji mold as the country’s “national microorganism” (kokkin). It was proposed by brewing researcher Ichishima Eiji (professor emeritus at Tohoku University). There are national birds and national flowers, but Japan is said to be, for now, the only country with a “national microorganism.” The work of koji mold, which turns starch into glucose and protein into amino acids, supports almost all of Japan’s seasonings: miso, soy sauce, mirin, sake, and vinegar. Respect for that fact is what the declaration is really about.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>In 2006, koji mold was recognized as Japan’s “national microorganism” by the Brewing Society of Japan</strong> — Japan is said to be the only country in the world with a national microorganism. Most of the flavor of miso, soy sauce, mirin, sake, and vinegar comes from the work of koji mold.</div><p>Put into a single line at the inn’s table, this story becomes: “This morning’s miso soup is the work of the national microorganism.” It may feel a little embarrassing, but there is no other line so accurate and so rich in places for the conversation to go.</p><h2>“Sending it half a year later” with miso is a gift of time itself</h2><p>A standard miso-making experience is the plan of “mailing the miso you made half a year later.” Why half a year? Because salty rice miso matures over six months to a year (white miso takes a few weeks to a month, barley miso a few months, and soybean miso more than a year; it varies with the ratio of salt and koji). What the guest takes home is not a container of soybeans and koji but, in fact, “the time of waiting.” So when sending it, add a photo of the day it was made and a single line, and the continuation of the experience arrives as a letter.</p><p>Soy sauce takes even longer. Traditional natural brewing takes at least half a year to a year from preparation to pressing. Moromi mash prepared in the cold season ferments actively in summer, then settles down and mellows in autumn. It is not realistic for an inn to create this time, like the rings of a tree, by itself. Soy sauce is not something to “make” but something to “hear about at the brewery.” If there is a soy sauce brewery nearby, put the brewery’s history and the owner’s story on a single sheet of paper and keep it at the inn. That alone gives the soy sauce cruet on the table a history.</p><h2>In Modena, time itself was a family’s fortune</h2><p>Aceto Balsamico Tradizionale of Modena, Italy, is a vinegar made by boiling down only grape must and aging it for many years in wooden barrels. For aging, a set of about five barrels of different woods (oak, chestnut, cherry, ash, mulberry, and others) is used, and moisture is driven off as the vinegar is moved from barrel to barrel. The rules are strict: only vinegar aged for at least 12 years may be called “Tradizionale,” and that aged for at least 25 years is called “Extravecchio.” A reference price of tens of thousands of yen per 100 milliliters is, so to speak, “the price of time.”</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>Balsamico Tradizionale is aged for at least 12 years, Extravecchio for at least 25 years</strong> — A Modena family craft of aging while rearranging barrels of different woods. In the end, it is concentrated to a few percent of the amount originally prepared.</div><p>Why is this story interesting? Because a barrel is “an asset of waiting.” Japan’s miso barrels are, as an idea, the same thing. Place one small barrel at the inn, with a paper bearing the guest’s name and the date it was made. “This miso will be ready to eat next spring” — it means the inn can create a reason to return on the very day the miso is made.</p><h2>Fruit vinegar, and not erasing the smell of the brewery</h2><p>At inns with orchards or fruit fields, fruit vinegar is an introduction to brewing. Fruit vinegar, made simply by steeping fruit and sugar in vinegar, rarely fails, and it is a way of preserving the season’s fruit in that year’s jar. Blueberry vinegar, mandarin vinegar, lemon vinegar. Just lining up jars with the year written on them turns the lounge into “a small brewery.” It is also nice to steep a jar together with children and make a promise to taste it on a return visit the following year.</p><p>One more asset that tends to be overlooked is “the smell of the brewery.” The aroma of fermentation has soaked into miso and soy sauce breweries. To an inn’s sensibility accustomed to fragrance-free spaces, this smell is something one might want to erase; in a landscape with a brewery, it is first on the list of things that must not be erased. If the source of the smell (koji and maturation) can be explained on a single sheet of paper, the smell turns from “a complaint” into “guidance.” Incidentally, the expression “temae miso” (self-praise, literally “my own miso”) is said to have come from boasting about one’s homemade miso. The miso soup an inn serves with confidence should be exactly that — its own miso.</p><h2>Hygiene, allergies, and when it doesn’t suit</h2><ul><li><strong>Hygiene</strong> — Miso has a high salt concentration in which unwanted bacteria struggle to grow, making it a fermented food that is easy to handle even at home. However, telling undesirable mold from maturation is a craftsperson’s job. Design the preparation experience under the guidance of a koji maker or similar.</li><li><strong>Allergies</strong> — Ingredients derived from soybeans, wheat or barley, and rice are handled. Always state allergy information clearly when guiding guests to the experience.</li><li><strong>Children</strong> — Mixing and kneading koji is an introduction to fermentation that even children can do safely. Avoid handling sake lees and mirin, which contain alcohol.</li><li><strong>When it doesn’t suit</strong> — Inns whose guests are mainly sensitive to smells. Inns that cannot take on the management of maturation (temperature, location, regular checks). At inns that do not serve miso soup at breakfast, the guidance loses persuasiveness.</li></ul><h2>InnBuddy VIEW</h2><p>What a landscape with a brewery really is: the work of microorganisms and the human craft of waiting. The inn can take part in only a small portion of it, and that is enough. Place one barrel. Write the year on a jar. Do not erase the smell. Add one line of history to the morning miso soup. Not “making” but “keeping time in trust” — that is the role of an inn that works with brewing.</p><div class="note">This article is intended for sharing ideas.
