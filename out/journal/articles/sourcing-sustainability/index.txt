地域性より誠実さ</h2><p>この数字が教えるのは、逆説的なことです。<strong>「すべて地元で」という主張は、この国では物理的に近づきにくい</strong>。コーヒーも、塩も、(多くの地域では)小麦も、そしてチーズの多くも、地元では手に入らない。だからこそ、語るべきは「地域性の理想像」ではなく<strong>「どこから来たかを、全部言えること」</strong>です。遠い産地を隠す宿と、遠い産地まで地図に書く宿。後者を選ぶゲストが、明日から増えます。サステナビリティの言語は「地元」ではなく「正直」から始まります。</p><h2>サステナブル仕入れの3軸</h2><ol><li><strong>地域性(短い供給網)</strong> 1b:T19cd,<p>The most persuasive expression of sustainability is "procurement." Guests do not believe slogans. They believe "what is on the plate" and "what is in the room." Procurement is <strong>evidence of choices made every day</strong> — which makes it the front line of telling a sustainability story. This article, however, is not a "use local ingredients" article. <div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>According to the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries’ Food Balance Sheet, Japan’s food self-sufficiency rate on a calorie basis is 38% (fiscal 2023). A strikingly low level among major countries, with much of the feed and fertilizer also dependent on imports. The food of this country, in other words, is structurally built in combination with "things that come from far away" (as of writing)</strong> — A property flying the banner of "all local" lives with this fact every day.</div>

<h2>The essence — honesty before localness</h2>

<p>What this number teaches is paradoxical. <strong>The claim "everything local" is, in this country, physically hard to approach.</strong> Coffee, salt, wheat (in most regions), and much of the cheese cannot be had locally. Which is why what should be told is not "an ideal image of localness" but <strong>"being able to say, of everything, where it came from."</strong> A property that hides the distant origin, and a property that puts the distant origin on the map. Guests who choose the latter will increase from tomorrow. The language of sustainability begins not with "local" but with "honest."</p>

<h2>The 3 axes of sustainable sourcing</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Regionality (short supply chains)</strong> — Buying from local producers, in season. Purchasing that follows the rhythm of the fish landings and the vegetable harvests.</li>
<li><strong>Fairness (fair trade)</strong> — Paying producers a fair price and keeping long-term relationships. Stepping down, from the property’s side, from the purchasing common sense of "the cheaper the better."</li>
<li><strong>Reduction (less waste)</strong> — A purchasing plan that reduces food loss. The design of prep volume is handled in the food-loss article.</li>
</ol>

<p>Note that only the first of the three axes is about "local." The second (fairness) is an axis that works precisely for distant origins — with coffee or wine alike, how you pay remains the property’s choice. The third is unrelated to distance. <strong>Evaluated on the three axes, sourcing can be told not by "local or not" but by "how it was chosen."</strong></p>

<h2>The design of the telling — who chooses, where it arrives, why it was chosen</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The face of the "sourcing team"</strong> — Who chooses the ingredients? Tell it with names and photographs (the structure by which a name becomes a guarantee was handled in the cheese article).</li>
<li><strong>A menu origin map</strong> — Where the ingredients come from, visually. And here is the point: <strong>put the distant origins on the map too.</strong> A 30-km radius, and two places across the sea. An honest map is trusted more than a filled-in one.</li>
<li><strong>A quarterly "sourcing report"</strong> — What was bought, what was changed, what was stopped. One page is enough. The discipline of numbers (publishing with the measurement method attached) is handled in the zero-waste article.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The economics — the honest cost</h2>

<ul>
<li>Local sourcing tends to raise costs by 5–10% (a reference value). Buying small amounts from small producers, the unit price normally rises.</li>
<li>But that increase is recoverable through the value of the brand (reviews, repeat stays, reasons to be chosen).</li>
<li>"Cheap and bad" sourcing wounds both sustainability and quality — a choice made on price alone is paid for somewhere, in the end.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The other side — when sourcing exhausts the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The simplification "local = good for the environment"</strong> — Environmental load is not decided by transport distance alone. Local greenhouse growing versus field growing far away — which is lighter is, in fact, not a simple question. Tell not "local, therefore right" but "how we chose, and why."</li>
<li><strong>The lightness of the words "local production, local consumption"</strong> — Define your own radius, and be able to state the range honestly. A word raised while still vague is transparent to a sharp guest.</li>
<li><strong>Where the added cost goes</strong> — A practice of filling the cost of honest sourcing not through price but by eating into staff wages or cleaning hours will not continue.</li>
<li><strong>Swapping things for display</strong> — Suddenly replacing existing sourcing to have something to show creates the contradiction of wasting old stock. If you switch, switch while using things up.</li>
<li><strong>The report becoming a hollow shell</strong> — The reason the quarterly report becomes unwritable by the third issue is that the first issue was made too lavish. One page; handwritten is fine.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is, tonight, to add a "where from" column to your purchasing ledger. Write out the facts as they are — the wholesale market, the neighboring farm, imports — not as a table of guilt but as a table of departure points. When that column is entirely filled, the property becomes, for the first time, "a property that can say where everything came from." Sustainability is not a department; it is the daily choosing — and what guests believe is not the slogan but the evidence on the plate.</p>
