11:T1aa1,<p>Sustainable and regenerative tourism sound similar, but point in different directions. If sustainability means reducing harm as much as possible, regeneration means leaving a place better than it was before you visited. Rather than shrinking a footprint, imagine that footprint enriching the soil. This article explores two innovations from two countries and five small approaches that can help an inn move toward that way of thinking.</p><h2>Palau: the country that made its entry stamp a pledge</h2><p>In December 2017, the Pacific island nation of Palau became the first country in the world to turn its immigration stamp into a pledge. Everyone entering the country signs a promise addressed to Palau’s children before being admitted: “I vow to tread lightly, act kindly and explore mindfully. I shall not take what is not given. The only footprints I shall leave are those that will wash away.” With more than 160,000 tourists visiting annually in a country of around 20,000 people, this is a mechanism for protecting the country from tourism’s harms. Behind the pledge lies the traditional Palauan practice of “bul,” a moratorium that prevents overuse of resources. Penalties were also established for violations.</p><p>What matters is how it is written. It is not a list of prohibitions, but a promise to children. Palau rewrote house rules as a pledge. An inn can apply the same idea: rewrite notices about smoking, quiet, and taking items away as “Our promises while staying in this village.” A sign that demands compliance becomes a greeting that invites participation.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>In December 2017, Palau became the first country in the world to turn its immigration stamp into a pledge, requiring all arrivals to sign a promise to Palau’s children</strong> — Its background includes the traditional practice of “bul,” which prevents overuse of resources. The innovation was to rewrite prohibitions as promises.</div><h2>Flanders: the tourism authority that moved beyond growth</h2><p>Another innovation took a different direction. In 2018, the tourism authority of Belgium’s Flemish regional government adopted the vision “Travel to Tomorrow.” It shifted tourism’s purpose from increasing arrivals to enabling residents, businesses, and visitors to flourish together. Regenerative tourism pioneer Anna Pollock helped guide the change. In Dutch as spoken in Flanders, the shift is called “van groeien naar bloeien”: from growing to flourishing. Measure tourism’s success through a region’s resilience, rather than headcounts. At a time when tourism authorities around the world were still counting visitors, one region chose to move beyond that focus.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>In 2018, VISITFLANDERS adopted “Travel to Tomorrow,” shifting its goal from growth in arrivals to the shared flourishing of residents, businesses, and visitors</strong> — Regenerative tourism pioneer Anna Pollock helped guide the process. “From growing to flourishing” is its watchword.</div><p>For an inn, the translation is this: measure success not only by occupancy, but also by the money that stays in the region and the personal relationships built locally. Decisions that increase both are regenerative management.</p><h2>Five ways a stay can help a region become better</h2><ul><li><strong>Buy locally and show where you buy</strong> — Share the names behind your breakfast and supply purchases, even just once a month. A line saying “This month, we purchased from…” changes how you describe circulation within the region.</li><li><strong>A stay that includes helping</strong> — Put an optional “afternoon of helping” on the schedule during festival preparations or busy farming periods. This is explored further in the voluntourism article.</li><li><strong>A 15-minute morning cleanup</strong> — Make time for guests to join a beach or village cleanup for just 15 minutes in the morning. Offer it as a choice, not an obligation.</li><li><strong>Return part of revenue to the community</strong> — Allocate part of the inn’s revenue to a local festival or conservation fund. Transparency is essential: disclose the amount and how it is used.</li><li><strong>A tree to care for</strong> — Rather than ending a commemorative tree planting when the tree is planted, make it a tree that returning guests check on and care for. With each visit, the region’s trees can increase and grow.</li></ul><h2>The responsibility of talking about regeneration</h2><ul><li><strong>Do not impose contributions</strong> — Guests are not buying obligations. Present cleanup and helping opportunities as things they can choose to join. Regeneration and compulsion do not sit well together.</li><li><strong>Conditions for claiming “for the community”</strong> — Do not claim to act for the community without its agreement. Talk to local people before becoming involved in festivals or funds.</li><li><strong>Do not exaggerate numbers</strong> — Statements such as “All accommodation fees stay in the region” can be exaggerated even if not outright false. Clearly label percentages as estimates.</li><li><strong>Do not treat contributions as cancellation</strong> — Contributing does not erase tourism’s negative effects. Palau’s willingness to establish penalties as well as a pledge supports its credibility.</li></ul><h2>InnBuddy VIEW</h2><p>Palau made entry a promise, while Flanders moved beyond growth toward flourishing.
