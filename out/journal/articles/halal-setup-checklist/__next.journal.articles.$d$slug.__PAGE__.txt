11:T20c2,<p>The "strategy side" — the market’s size and honest positioning — was organized in the separate article "Muslim Inbound: A New Segment for Japanese Properties." This article is the setup edition. Enough of the theory on the desk — from here, we organize a checklist for small properties to start, within realistic bounds, the three blocks of <strong>a place to pray, food they can eat with confidence, and signals of cleanliness</strong>. To repeat: halal readiness is decided not by scale but by "can it be trusted."</p>

<h2>A map of the words — "certified" and "Muslim-friendly" are different things</h2>

<p>Before any setup, the words must be made precise. In this field, mixing the terms causes accidents.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>"Halal certification" is issued by a third-party certification body after examining the kitchen, the supply routes of the ingredients, and the cooking process. "Muslim-friendly," by contrast, is a self-declared expression indicating good-faith efforts such as removing pork and alcohol, with no third-party guarantee as certification has. In Japan there is no law or body governing certification bodies, and multiple private organizations issue certifications and marks under their own standards (publications of the Japan Halal Foundation and the Nippon Asia Halal Association; a paper in a culinary-science journal)</strong> — Only a property that has undergone the examination may write "certified."</div>

<p>This fact is good news for a small property, too. Certification takes money, time, and a structure, and does not come within reach at once. But <strong>even without certification, trust can be built</strong> — an earlier generation’s case shows the way.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>In the Hida Takayama Muslim-friendly project, with people from the Nagoya Mosque still involved, the path chosen was not that of "halal certification" but of developing local dishes using halal meat and non-alcoholic seasonings, carefully displaying ingredient information, and letting Muslims themselves judge (introduced in a culinary-science journal paper)</strong> — Instead of hiding, hand over the materials for judgment. The most honest path a property without certification can take.</div>

<p>The setup in this article runs on this same philosophy throughout. <strong>Do not feign perfection; hand over the facts.</strong> Here are the three blocks.</p>

<h2>Block 1 — a place to pray (4 items)</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>A quiet, clean small space</strong> — One room is enough. A corner partitioned by a curtain will do. What matters is being able to say, "We can show you here."</li>
<li><strong>A prayer rug</strong> — Keep the loaner rugs clean. One device for praying on the floor also covers guests who bring none.</li>
<li><strong>A place for ablution (wudu)</strong> — Before prayer, hands, feet, and face are washed (wudu); the washroom or the bathroom entrance can serve. Adding a water pitcher makes it easier to wash while crouching.</li>
<li><strong>A mark of the prayer direction (qibla)</strong> — Prayer is offered facing Mecca. If you prepare a mark showing that direction (the window’s orientation, an arrow, a card naming the direction), guests can confirm it themselves.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Block 2 — food they can eat with confidence (4 items)</h2>

<ol start="5">
<li><strong>A menu that shows "what is in it"</strong> — Write the ingredients used. Not proof of certification, but disclosure of facts builds trust (the Hida Takayama philosophy).</li>
<li><strong>Separation of cooking (managing cross-contamination)</strong> — Separate the utensils and the place for halal-oriented dishes from cooking that uses pork or alcohol. The mechanism of the vegan article can be reused as is.</li>
<li><strong>Making hidden ingredients visible</strong> — The pitfall of washoku. Mirin and cooking sake (alcohol), dashi stock made from bonito or pork, gelatin of pork origin — the "ingredients that trip up Muslims despite the food being Japanese" concentrate in these three lines. How far to remove them is the property’s call, but <strong>knowing what you use and being able to explain it</strong> is the minimum line.</li>
<li><strong>A staff script</strong> — "This is not fully halal, but this dish uses no pork or alcohol. We can show you the ingredients too." All staff being able to say this one sentence is a stronger signal than any equipment.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Block 3 — cleanliness and signals (3 items)</h2>

<ol start="9">
<li><strong>A clear display of what you offer</strong> — On the sign, the listing, the site, write what you can and cannot do. If you use the words "Muslim-friendly," add their substance too (what you actually do).</li>
<li><strong>One sheet of "what we can and cannot do"</strong> — The prayer place, meals, washing places, the state of the kitchen, on one page. An honest "we cannot" is trusted more than a vague "we accommodate."</li>
<li><strong>A record of training</strong> — Leave a memo of how far staff can explain. A record of external training received makes an even stronger display.</li>
</ol>

<h2>The order to start — the order of cost-effectiveness</h2>

<p>The 11 items need not all be done at once. Start with <strong>"honesty of displays" and "cleanliness"</strong> — two points that cost almost nothing and form the foundation of trust. Next, disclosure of food information (grasping the hidden ingredients), then preparing the prayer place — that order is realistic for a small property.</p>

<h2>The other side — when the setup hurts the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The weight of "we accommodate"</strong> — A display unmatched by reality is far more dangerous than no display. Before writing, check that what you wrote matches every staff member’s words.</li>
<li><strong>Washoku and mirin coexisting</strong> — Removing mirin entirely means rebuilding the flavor design from scratch. Non-alcoholic substitute seasonings exist, but the decision to introduce them belongs with the chef. Lying "we removed it" is the worst of all.</li>
<li><strong>The reversal of Ramadan (the fasting month)</strong> — In the fasting month, meals shift to before dawn and after sunset. The chance of a visitor’s stay overlapping it is always there, and because of the calendar’s nature, the timing shifts about 11 days earlier each year. If asked "from what time can breakfast be served," have the answer ready by the day before — with just that preparation, the stay of a fasting guest becomes dramatically easier.</li>
<li><strong>Don’t leap at expensive consultants</strong> — Standards and cases can be read in the publications of the relevant organizations. Grasp the whole picture from free information first, then judge what support you need.</li>
<li><strong>Don’t lump everyone together</strong> — Prayer, food, fasting — the degree of practice differs by person. Put what you have prepared in "a form they can choose," and leave whether to use it to the guest. Pushing is the opposite of consideration.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is, tonight, to line up the labels on the seasoning shelf and photograph them. Mirin, cooking sake, dashi, gelatin — count your property’s "hidden ingredients," and put on one sheet which dishes use them. That sheet becomes the material for the most honest answer to a question from a guest who will come someday.</p>
