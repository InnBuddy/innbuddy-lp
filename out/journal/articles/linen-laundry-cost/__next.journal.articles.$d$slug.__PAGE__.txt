11:T1d95,<p>Linen exchange tends to be discussed as a matter of "cleanliness." But open the wallet and the floor, and it has another face. <strong>Linen costs, laundry costs, water and utilities, staff labor</strong> — the daily change is a choice that inflates these four costs every single day. This article re-reads the "daily change" not as a natural given but as one choice, and organizes the design of shifting to "change when needed."</p>

<h2>Arai-hari — cloth was not a consumable</h2>

<p>Kimono had a care called <strong>arai-hari</strong> — taking the garment apart to wash it, drying it, and sewing it back together. It takes labor, but the cloth’s life extends greatly. Before the era of "wash after wearing," cloth was not a consumable but <strong>an asset maintained with labor</strong>. Look at linen through this lens and the view changes. Washing is an act of keeping the asset clean — and at the same time, <strong>an act of reducing the asset, little by little</strong>. The number of exchanges is a number of cleanings, and at the same time, a way of shortening the life.</p>

<h2>The 4 hidden costs</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Linen costs</strong> — The more frequent the exchange, the faster the fabric wears, and the shorter the replacement cycle.</li>
<li><strong>Laundry costs</strong> — Outsourced, a charge per piece; in-house, detergent and drying. Proportional to frequency.</li>
<li><strong>Water and utilities</strong> — The water to wash, the heat to dry. The environmental load rides here too.</li>
<li><strong>Staff labor</strong> — Collecting, washing, setting. On a short-staffed floor, this line is the heaviest.</li>
</ul>

<p>Three of the four move with "frequency × unit price." Which means reviewing the system is a lever that, as cost-cutting and as a labor measure, turns the same single place.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">RESEARCH</span><strong>In a towel-reuse experiment at a US hotel (Goldstein, Cialdini, and Griskevicius, 2008; 190 rooms, about 80 days), 35.1% reused with the "please help the environment" message. With "75% of this property’s guests choose to reuse their towels," 44.1%; with "75% of the guests who stayed in this room did," the figure rose to 49.3%</strong> — Words that state a fact move people more than words that ask.</div>

<h2>The science of the asking — "facts" over "requests"</h2>

<p>What this research teaches properties is that <strong>presenting facts moves people more than moral requests</strong>. "Please cooperate for the environment" is right but weak. "A majority of our guests reuse their towels" is strong simply by being a fact. One caution, though. In a German follow-up, where environmental awareness was already high and reuse was the norm, the effect barely reproduced. If your guest mix is already cooperative, changing the words will not move the number — knowing the current state is the first step.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>Hotelstars Union, the common hotel-star criteria of 21 European countries, revised its criteria for 2025–2030 to "additional changes of bed linen and towels only at the guest’s explicit request" (decided May 2024). The daily change is no longer a "star standard"</strong> — The very norm of cleanliness is starting to be rewritten, worldwide.</div>

<h2>The design of "change when needed"</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Offer the choice to consecutive-night guests</strong> — One card: "For stays of more than one night, linen is exchanged on request only." Add the reason (the environment, and caring for the cloth) and a token of thanks (a small benefit or a word of gratitude).</li>
<li><strong>Write the criteria for exchange</strong> — When soiled; from the third night onward; on request. Unwritten, and the staff’s judgment wavers by person (how to write standards is handled by <a href="#service-standard-basics">Service Basics: The "Standard" That Does Not Vary by Person</a>).</li>
<li><strong>Towels: just "where they are placed"</strong> — Towels in the basket, on the floor, or in the tub: exchange. Towels hung up: leave. This signal reaches guests worldwide. Not letters, but the design of placement.</li>
</ol>

<p>Operation begins with <strong>writing the frequency and cost of exchanges on one line</strong>. One line a week, four a month, is enough. When the savings become visible, return a part of them to local activities — the moment saving changes from "the property’s convenience" into "a mechanism of the region" (the thinking of stays that show the cycle is in <a href="#idea-sustainable-tourism-stay">Incorporate Sustainable Tourism into Inn - Stay Ideas Showing Circulation of Living</a>).</p>

<h2>The other side — the pitfalls of reducing exchanges</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Headwinds from hygiene</strong> — Japan’s humid summer; guest segments who seek cleanliness itself; towels after sunburn. "Reducing" is realistically considered by type (towels vs. sheets) and by season.</li>
<li><strong>Cheapening the words "eco-conscious"</strong> — As the research shows, generalized requests do not move people. Stack up the facts that fit your property before turning them into words.</li>
<li><strong>How the saving looks</strong> — The moment it looks like "the property making things easy for itself," it backfires. Add the reason, and a token of return or of thanks.</li>
<li><strong>Standards becoming a shell</strong> — Post the criteria and let the floor’s own judgments continue, and the system is not moving. Once a month, check the reality once.</li>
<li><strong>Being danced around by the numbers</strong> — Do not judge results on two weeks of savings. Laundry swings widely with weather and occupancy — read it on a three-month average.</li>
</ul>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">DID YOU KNOW?</span><strong>"Towels on the floor or in the tub mean exchange" — this signal works as a gesture shared by guests almost worldwide</strong> — The criteria for exchange can be conveyed by "where they are placed" alone, more surely than by a long explanation.</div>

<p>Cleaning was originally an act of welcome — so another article told (<a href="#cleaning-checklist-experience">Turning Housekeeping From "Cost" Into "Experience": A Checklist</a>). Linen is the same. Stopping the "daily change" is not cutting corners; it is <strong>designing the time that protects the cloth</strong>. The first step is to write this month’s laundry counts and costs on one sheet — the first page of your property’s ledger of cloth.</p>

Please design hygiene management along public-health guidelines, and the criteria for odor and stains at the floor’s discretion. The European star criteria are based on public information as of writing.</div>

12:T15de,<p>床单和衣物<strong>“每日兑换”系统的隐性成本</strong>。很多旅馆都把每天更换床单视为理所当然的事情，并通过每天更换来尽量保持清洁。但如果换个角度来看，每天更换就是一笔“隐性成本”。亚麻布成本、洗衣成本、公用事业成本和员工工作量。这四项成本随着每天的交换而增加。本文整理了减少隐性成本的方法，并通过在需要时更换物品而不是每天更换物品来讨论可持续性。</p>

<h2>1. 四项隐性成本——床单、洗衣、水电费和人工</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>床单费：</strong>每天更换它们会导致您的亚麻布磨损得更快。床单、枕套和毛巾等床单是消耗品。每天更换床单会缩短床单的使用寿命并增加您的床单费用。床单费用占住宿费用的很大一部分。</li>
<li><strong>洗衣费：</strong>如果每天都换，你的洗衣费用就会增加。如果洗衣是外包的，就会有洗衣费，如果洗衣是在公司内部完成，则需要水电费、清洁剂和员工的人工费用。洗衣费用与更换床单的次数成比例增加。</li>
<li><strong>公用事业：</strong>洗衣需支付公用事业费。如果你每天更换它，你的水电费就会增加。公用事业成本也给环境带来了负担。</li>
<li><strong>员工努力：</strong>每天更换它们会增加员工的时间和精力。工作人员每天负责收集、清洗和更换床单。对于人手不足的旅馆来说，人员配备是一个主要问题。</li>
</ul>

<h2>2.“按需更换”设计——3种选择</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>为入住多晚的客人提供“不交换”选项：</strong>我们为入住多晚的客人提供“不更换床单”选项。例如：“为了环保，请不要在连续入住期间更换床单。如果您配合，我们将为您提供一杯免费的当地啤酒。”通过提供选择，客人会觉得旅馆有环保意识。</li>
<li><strong>明确“何时需要更换”的标准：</strong>明确何时需要更换床单的标准。例如：当有污垢时、当连续住宿3晚或以上时、当客人有要求时等。通过明确标准，工作人员将更容易了解何时更换产品。</li>
<li><strong>当毛巾放在地板上时更换它们：</strong>更换毛巾的标准简单易懂，就是“落地就换”。只更换放在地板上的毛巾，而不更换挂在毛巾架上的毛巾，可以减少更换毛巾的次数。</li>
</ol>

<h2>3. 运营——谈可视化和可持续</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>记录更换布草的次数和费用：</strong>通过记录更换布草的次数和成本（布草成本、洗衣成本、水电费、员工时间），您可以发现隐藏成本。通过记录，你可以看到减少的效果。</li>
<li><strong>告诉他们，“请爱护环境，不要更换床单。”</strong>通过告诉客人“请爱护环境，不要更换床单”，客人会感受到您是一家环保旅馆。谈论可持续发展也涉及到旅馆的品牌塑造。</li>
<li><strong>将减少的成本返还给当地社区：</strong>通过将节省的部分成本返还给当地活动（例如当地清洁、当地活动），工作人员认为他们通过减少床单更换次数为社区做出了贡献。</li>
</ul>
