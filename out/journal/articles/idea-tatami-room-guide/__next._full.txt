1b:T1d9f,<p>What is the hardest thing in guiding guests through a tatami room? Actually, it is not “explaining tatami.” It is the guests’ hesitation: “How should I sit?” That is why this article starts not with tatami but with ways of sitting. Once you know the history of sitting, the way you guide becomes far freer.</p><h2>Seiza is actually a new “tradition”</h2><p>Many people think “seiza is how Japanese people have sat since long ago.” Yet before that, the common ways of sitting in Japan were cross-legged (agura, or anza) or with one knee raised. The people depicted in yamato-e paintings and ukiyo-e prints sit cross-legged regardless of status. The seiza posture came from China in the Nara period as a ceremonial way of sitting, but it was only in the Meiji era that it was taught in schools nationwide as “the proper way for Japanese people to sit” and took root among ordinary people. Even in the world of the tea ceremony, Sen no Rikyū is said to have made tea sitting cross-legged, and making tea in seiza is said to have spread from the Meiji era onward.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Seiza spread nationwide through school education in the Meiji era. Until then, the common ways of sitting were cross-legged (agura, or anza) or with one knee raised</strong> — Seiza is not a thousand-year tradition but a way of sitting designed and popularized in the Meiji era as “Japanese etiquette.”</div><p>Why does this fact work in guidance? Because it gives grounds to the single remark to guests, “Please sit however is comfortable.” “In Japan, both sitting cross-legged and seiza are traditions. Please take a comfortable posture” — this guidance lets you stand not on the side that forces seiza but on the side that explains the freedom of sitting. Add one picture of the manners of relaxing your legs (resting by switching which big toe is on top, or the “raku” posture of lowering your bottom to the floor), and even long mealtimes become easier.</p><h2>Tatami is the building material that most became a “unit” in Japan</h2><p>Counting the size of a room by the number of tatami mats — six mats, eight mats, ten mats. This custom is rare even worldwide, a culture in which tatami became a unit of area as they are. What is more, tatami sizes differ by region. Kyōma (honma) is 191 × 95.5 cm, Chūkyōma is 182 × 91 cm, Edoma (Kantōma) is 176 × 88 cm, and Danchima is 170 × 85 cm. Even the same “one mat” differs in size between Kyoto and Tokyo. This difference is a remnant of the history in which Kyoto and Edo each decided the spacing between pillars (hashira-wari) separately.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">DID YOU KNOW?</span><strong>Even the same “one mat” differs by more than 15 cm between Kyōma (191 cm) and Edoma (176 cm)</strong> — The tatami size is a regional fingerprint carrying the history of pillar spacing. Measure the inn’s tatami, and you will know the lineage of that land.</div><h2>The scent of tatami comes from the fields of Kumamoto</h2><p>The raw material of the tatami surface (tatami-omote) is igusa, a rush grown in paddy fields. More than 90% of domestic tatami facing is produced in Kumamoto Prefecture, grown especially around Yatsushiro City. Cultivation takes about a year and a half from planting to harvest. Thousands of rush stems are used in the facing of a single mat, and that scent is the smell of a freshly mown meadow. The tatami-doko core beneath it is made of many layers of rice straw, compressed. In other words, tatami is a building material born from rice paddies. The inside of a rush stem has a spongy hollow structure that regulates humidity by absorbing and releasing moisture, and it is also known to adsorb substances such as formaldehyde. The guideline for replacing the facing (omote-gae) is said to be every 5–10 years because tatami is not “done once laid” but a living material that keeps breathing.</p><p>Put this story on a single card at the inn, and it becomes: “The scent of this tatami is the scent of a grass called igusa. Ninety percent of the domestic igusa used in Japan was grown for a year and a half in the fields of Kumamoto.” The explanation of the scent is worth placing at the center of the tatami room’s guidance.</p><h2>The futon is an invention that “changes the room”</h2><p>To sum up the function of a Japanese-style room in a word, it is “a room that changes with time.” By day, a spacious room without furniture; by night, a bedroom. What makes this transformation possible is the futon. The experience of laying out a futon is not merely an introduction to bedding. It is the experience of making a bedroom for that night alone with your own hands. There are three steps. Lay the shikibuton mattress, put the kakebuton cover over it, and place the pillow. In the morning, fold them in reverse order and put them in the oshiire closet. Turn this sequence into a “picture guide” and place it in the guest room — just by making the timetable of setting up and clearing away visible, the Japanese-style room is understood not as a stage set but as a system of living.</p><h2>Protecting tatami means explaining tatami</h2><ul><li><strong>The culture of socks and bare feet</strong> — “No shoes” is not a rule but a manner that protects tatami. Explain the meaning of changing footwear in a single line.</li><li><strong>Food and drink stains</strong> — Stains on igusa are hard to remove. Do not leave it vague whether eating and drinking are allowed in the guest room.</li><li><strong>Humidity and mites</strong> — Fold the futon every morning and air them in the daytime. Do not leave rugs laid on the tatami. This routine is almost everything for the life and hygiene of tatami.</li><li><strong>The burden of cleaning</strong> — Explaining cleaning along the weave of the tatami and the manner of not stepping on the tatami border (tatami-beri) also reduces the burden on the cleaning side.</li><li><strong>Inns it suits</strong> — Every inn with Japanese-style rooms. The less an inn forces seiza on the tatami, the more this guidance shines.</li></ul><h2>InnBuddy VIEW</h2><p>The heart of tatami-room guidance is three facts.
