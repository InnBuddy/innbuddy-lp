11:T1dbc,<p>The guest experience is <strong>not "inside the room" alone</strong>. Guests find photographs of mountains and sea on social, save the posts that catch their eye, search on a map, and arrive carrying both anxiety and expectation. And after arriving home, the memories continue, a review gets written, the seed of a return stay remains. This article is the foundation of the method for seeing this "line" on a single map.</p>

<h2>The roji — the experience began at the gate</h2>

<p>In the world of the tea ceremony, the beginning of the experience was not placed inside the tea room. The <strong>roji</strong> — the path leading to the tea room — is the first half of the experience. Passing through the gate, waiting at the <strong>machiai</strong> arbor, rinsing the hands at the <strong>tsukubai</strong> water basin, walking the <strong>tobi-ishi</strong> stepping stones one by one, and entering low through the <strong>nijiriguchi</strong> crawl-in entrance. In the few minutes of walking the roji, the guest leaves "the everyday self" behind, little by little. The tea ceremony is journey design from hundreds of years ago — the movement itself, designed as the experience.</p>

<p>The story of the "hakibarai" sweeping of the roji is handled in the cleaning article (<a href="#cleaning-checklist-experience">Turning Housekeeping From "Cost" Into "Experience": A Checklist</a>). What we want to see here is not the cleaning but the idea of <strong>"lining up the road the guest travels into the experience itself."</strong></p>

<h2>Modern properties have a roji too</h2>

<p>A property without a roji still has a roji. The first page of search results, the booking-site photographs, the booking-confirmation email, the road from the station, the fifty steps from the parking lot to the entrance, the first minute of check-in, the dining room the next morning, the send-off, one email after arriving home. <strong>Un-designed though it is, the line already exists.</strong> The map is the single sheet of paper that shows this line, for the first time, in visible form.</p>

<h2>How to make the map — 5 phases × 3 rows</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Discovery</strong> — The moment of being found on OTAs, social, map searches.</li>
<li><strong>Booking</strong> — Deciding, hesitating, inquiring.</li>
<li><strong>Before arrival</strong> — The anxieties of access, parking, check-in times.</li>
<li><strong>The stay</strong> — Five touchpoints: the welcome, the room, the bath, the meals, sleep.</li>
<li><strong>After returning home</strong> — Memories, the review, the trigger of a return visit.</li>
</ol>

<p>Lay these five phases across; lay <strong>"the guest’s feelings, actions, and the property’s response"</strong> — three rows — down the side. Put one sticky note in each cell for "the touchpoints that exist now." For example, if in the "before arrival" column you "send an access-guide email the day before," that is one sticky note. Color-coding the notes by theme (nature, food, people, information) also shows which colors the balance leans to.</p>

<h2>The blanks are the treasure — the hours nobody owns</h2>

<p>What to look at first on the map is not the crowded cells but <strong>the blank cells</strong>. A blank means "an hour nobody owns." Especially <strong>"before arrival" and "after returning home"</strong> go entirely blank at most properties. And the quality of the experience gets cheaper, the more these are filled.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Before arrival</strong> — One email with the route plus one neighborhood card (places to eat, a walking path). Anxiety turns into expectation. Polishing the pre-booking photographs is handled by <a href="#landing-page-3-seconds">Landing Page Design: What to Say in the First "Three Seconds"</a>.</li>
<li><strong>The first 5 minutes of the stay</strong> — The welcome drink, the first glance into the room. Working examples are in <a href="#welcome-amenity-5min">Welcome Amenity: Designing the First Five Minutes</a>.</li>
<li><strong>After returning home</strong> — One seasonal note. The mechanism for continuing is handled by <a href="#email-crm-direct">Email and CRM: The Direct-Booking Engine That Does Not Disappear After Checkout</a>.</li>
</ul>

<p>The research on "which moment of the stay stays in memory" (the peak-end rule) is treated in detail by <a href="#small-experiences-5-touchpoints">The "Small Experiences" Guests Talk About After Checkout</a>. See the whole line on the map, and design the moments within it — the two articles divide the work that way.</p>

<h2>Make it on the floor — walk your own roji</h2>

<p>The map completes on the floor, not in the meeting room. The shortest path is <strong>staying one night at your own property.</strong> Step down into the parking lot, reread the booking-confirmation email, unlock your own room door, sit in the morning dining room. The other short path is asking the staff one question — "Yesterday’s guest: what did they do first, after arriving?" Half the map’s sticky notes fill with these two.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">DID YOU KNOW?</span><strong>The tea room’s entrance, the nijiriguchi, is made deliberately low. Lay down your sword, bend, and enter — a design that has you step out of your standing before stepping into the experience</strong> — In a modern property, the same structure as leaving the work email behind and entering the preparations for the evening meal.</div>

<h2>The other side — when the map exhausts the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Trying to fix everything</strong> — Find ten blanks, and fix only one. The aim of the map is not a perfect diagram but finding one weak touchpoint.</li>
<li><strong>The illusion that blank = bad</strong> — Not every stretch of the line needs filling. A quiet hour where nothing happens is not a blank to fill but a margin to protect.</li>
<li><strong>Over-selling after the return home</strong> — Selling to someone whose experience has ended tends to backfire. After the return home, narrow it to one message that "arrives," not one that "is sent."</li>
<li><strong>Making it and being done</strong> — The map is not a sheet to frame and hang but a living paper to re-post once a quarter. Touchpoints increased? Add sticky notes.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is to prepare one sheet with only the grid — five phases × three rows — and for this one week, write touchpoints you notice onto sticky notes and put them up. In a week, one blank cell will be facing you. Fixing that one is how the map is used. The guest’s journey is a line, not points — and then the property’s work, too, can be lined up as a line, not points.</p>

<div class="note">This article is intended for sharing ideas. The roji, machiai, tsukubai, and nijiriguchi are introduced as general manners of the tea ceremony. Please adjust individual measures to your property’s scale and staffing.</div>

12:Tfeb,<p>住宿体验<strong>不只是“在客房里”</strong>。客人在社交媒体上查找山脉和海洋的照片，保存有关时尚咖啡馆的帖子，在谷歌地图上搜索“日本乡村旅馆”，然后带着焦虑和期待抵达。本文是一篇关于如何映射从预订前到回家后的联系点的基础文章。</p><h2>1. 5个阶段</h2><ol><li><strong>发现</strong> — 可在 OTA、Instagram（保存）和 Google 商家资料中找到。</li><li><strong>预订</strong> ——决定、犹豫、询问。照片占了战斗的90%。</li><li><strong>抵达前</strong> — 消除对通道、停车和入住时间的担忧。</li><li><strong>停留</strong> — 5个接触点：接机、客房、洗澡、用餐、睡觉。</li><li><strong>回国后</strong> ——回忆、评论和重温的原因。</li></ol><h2>2. 如何制作地图——在一张纸上可视化</h2><p>将 5 个阶段放在横轴上，将“宾客感受、行动和住宿反应”的 3 条线放在纵轴上。</p><ul><li>在每个方格内<strong>“目前的联络点”</strong>在便利贴上写下一件事（例如，您在抵达之前是否发送了访问电子邮件？）。</li><li>您可以通过将便签的颜色分为山（自然）、大海、酷咖啡馆和食物等主题来看到平衡。</li></ul><h2>3. 找到弱点——空白是宝藏。</h2><ul><li>空白单元格表示有改进的空间。尤其<strong>“抵达前”和“回国后”</strong>很多旅馆都是空的。</li><li>只需在您到达之前发送一张“附近很酷的咖啡馆地图”，您的焦虑就会变成期待。</li><li>回家后只需发送一封“季节性信件”就会鼓励他们回来。</li></ul>
<h2>为什么现在值得关注？</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>客人期待的变化：</strong>当谈到“宾客体验概述-如何在地图上查看接触点”时，宾客的期望与五年前相比发生了变化。过去“有了更好”的服务，如今已变成“没有就难以被选择”。适应这些变化，是住宿设施持续经营的前提。</li>
<li><strong>与地区关系：</strong>体验设计举措增加了整个地区的价值，而不仅仅是住宿本身。通过与当地生产者和周边商户合作，旅馆的行动会成为“当地故事”。社区共同参与，才更容易长期持续。</li>
<li><strong>不增加员工负担：</strong>新举措不应增加员工负担。通过建立机制，用一张表或每次15分钟的学习来运转，就能在不增加员工负担的情况下持续改进。</li>
</ul>

<h2>避免失败的3个要点</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>从小处开始——先做好一件事：</strong>一开始就想全部改变，往往难以持续。先从一件事开始。可以从一间客房、一个货架或一句话开始；即使效果不理想，影响也小，更容易调整。</li>
<li><strong>记录——用一张表看清楚：</strong>把行动记录在一张表里，效果就能被看见。你会知道哪些做法有效、哪些需要调整，记录也会成为下一轮改进的依据。</li>
<li><strong>持续——每周一次，每次15分钟：</strong>新行动需要持续。每周留出15分钟，逐渐形成习惯，最后才会成为团队文化。</li>
</ol>
