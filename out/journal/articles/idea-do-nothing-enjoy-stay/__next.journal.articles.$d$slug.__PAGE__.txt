This article explores that design paradox. Doing nothing might seem to happen simply by leaving people alone, yet it can require more design than almost any other experience.</p><h2>The year doing nothing became a book</h2><p>In 2019, American artist Jenny Odell published “How to Do Nothing.” It became a New York Times bestseller and one of former President Barack Obama’s favorite books of that year. Its subtitle is “Resisting the Attention Economy.” Odell’s doing nothing is not laziness: it is reclaiming attention captured by platforms for oneself and one’s surroundings. The attention the book attracted speaks to the mood of the times. Doing nothing has become not merely a name for idleness, but something people must actively choose. An inn can be a custodian of that chosen time.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>Jenny Odell’s “How to Do Nothing” became a New York Times bestseller in 2019 and was selected among Barack Obama’s favorite books of that year</strong> — Doing nothing can be a deliberate act of reclaiming attention, rather than laziness. An inn entrusted with that time needs to design for it.</div><h2>Daydreaming is not simply an idle brain</h2><p>Brain research also offers background to doing nothing. In 2001, a paper by Washington University neurologist Marcus Raichle and colleagues described brain regions with greater activity at rest than during certain tasks, calling this a “default mode.” Subsequent work associated the network with self-related thought and memory, and research continues. This is not a claim that daydreaming cures illness. The point is simpler: during apparently idle time, the brain may be doing a different kind of work. Time that looks empty is not necessarily empty. An inn can keep this as background knowledge when describing the time it protects, rather than presenting it as wasted time.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">RESEARCH</span><strong>In 2001, Raichle and colleagues at Washington University reported a “default mode” involving brain regions active during rest</strong> — Later research linked this network with self-related thought and memory. Research supports the possibility that daydreaming involves different work, rather than the brain simply idling.</div><h2>Four tools for doing nothing</h2><ul><li><strong>Place: a seat for doing nothing</strong> — One chair facing a view. No desk, no book, gentler lighting. Designing this seat means reducing what is placed around it. Hammocks and engawa verandas follow the same principle.</li><li><strong>Time: a rhythm for a nap</strong> — As in southern European afternoon-rest traditions, offer a schedule that makes the afternoon a time to linger. Present doing nothing as something for now, rather than merely available at any time, and guests can hold on to that time.</li><li><strong>Words: one decisive line</strong> — “Nothing in particular to do. Take your time.” Such a line can be more memorable than a packed itinerary. Its craft follows the same principle as the way a single sentence in guest guidance can shape an inn’s character.</li><li><strong>Operations: the skill of not interrupting</strong> — Well-meant conversation can be the greatest interruption. Set times for cleaning and guidance, and leave guests undisturbed outside them. One door-handle sign can protect a guest doing nothing.</li></ul><h2>The responsibilities of offering doing nothing</h2><ul><li><strong>Compulsion has the opposite effect</strong> — “Please do nothing” is poor guidance. People escaping busy lives do not need enforced inactivity. Keep it available as a choice.</li><li><strong>Boredom and children</strong> — With water, stones, and time, children can find their own play. Establish safe boundaries and leave room for boredom. It is part of their work of discovering things.</li><li><strong>What happens behind the scenes</strong> — An inn for doing nothing can require more work backstage. Guests’ idle time is supported by the inn’s activity. The offering works only alongside thoughtful staff workload planning.</li><li><strong>Safety boundaries</strong> — Relaxed guests may be less alert. Check ponds, cliffs, steps, and hammock heights. Quiet, unobtrusive safety checks are core work for an inn offering idleness.</li><li><strong>Do not use the name as an excuse for neglect</strong> — Poor cleaning is not doing nothing. The appeal of idleness rests on a space that is well cared for.</li></ul><p>Return to the opening question. What should an inn offering doing nothing write?
