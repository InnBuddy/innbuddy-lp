11:T20a7,<p>"What do you think we should charge for our rooms?" — the question owners ask most often has a pitfall in the way it is asked. The price is not something the inn decides. There is a market, there are competitors, there are travelers' judgments; and only on top of all that is the price of your room settled. The owner's job is not to decide but to take part in that decision. This article is about the three steps of participation — find the market price, choose your position, adjust with time — and, at the end, the "law of price increases" behind the numbers.</p>

<h2>Step 1 — Find the market price</h2>

<p>First, record the price band of five neighboring competitors: the low, the middle, and the high. Ten minutes on the booking sites collects it. What this builds is a ruler — "the width of the market's price." Where in that width does your inn's current price sit? Most inns do not carry this ruler. The moment you hold it, half the worry over pricing disappears (not an exaggeration: half the worry comes from not knowing what the market is doing).</p>

<h2>Step 2 — Choose your position</h2>

<p>Where on the ruler to stand. The materials for judgment are three — quality (the level of the rooms and facilities), experience (food, the bath, how time is spent), and location (views, access, surroundings). Inns that can stand above on these three points stand above; those that cannot stand in the middle. <strong>If you cannot tell, starting in the middle is safe</strong> — the middle is the position from which you can move both up and down. An inn that has stood at the top cannot easily come down (discounting destroys "the true price"), but from the middle upward, you can move while watching the reviews.</p>

<h2>Step 3 — Adjust with time</h2>

<p>An inn that posts the same price for the same room all year is discarding four changes: season, day of the week, lead time, and occupancy. The design of adjustment (moving prices with demand) is left to an article further down the road, but the sentence to memorize as a foundation is this: <strong>a price is not something you set once; it is something you adjust</strong>. Move it with the season, with the day of the week, with the rooms remaining. From here on, it is the story of "pricing as design."</p>

<h2>The law of price increases — why the same increase is accepted sometimes and resented at others</h2>

<p>From here, the story of the human heart behind the three steps. In 1986, three researchers — Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler — published a study of people's "sense of price fairness," conducted as a telephone survey. The famous question: "A hardware store has been selling snow shovels for $15. On the morning after a blizzard, it raises the price to $20." Fully 82% judged this "unfair." Raise the price because demand rose — a store behaving exactly as the economics textbook prescribes is resented by eight in ten. And in the same study, an increase made because the wholesale cost had risen was accepted by many as "unavoidable." The researchers named this feeling "dual entitlement." The guest holds a right to "the usual price"; the store holds a right to "the usual profit." What the guest permits is only the increase a store makes to protect that profit. An increase that rides a surge of demand feels like injustice.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">RESEARCH</span><strong>In the Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler study (1986, a U.S. telephone survey), 82% of respondents (of 107) judged a hardware store that raised a snow shovel from $15 to $20 on the morning after a blizzard "unfair," while increases attributed to rising wholesale costs tended to be accepted</strong> — The line between increases people permit and refuse lies not in the market but in the sense of fairness.</div>

<p>Translating this into the inn's language: an increase is accepted when the reason can be told. <strong>When costs have changed</strong> (the breakfast ingredients changed, the amenities were upgraded). <strong>When the content has changed</strong> (the season of autumn leaves, a room overlooking the festival, a dinner not served on weekdays). <strong>When custom supports it</strong> (Saturdays cost more, Obon costs more — long-standing custom becomes part of "the usual price"). The reverse — an increase with no tellable reason, only "the inn nearby sold out" or "we got popular" — creates the same impression as the shovel on the snow morning. It may still bring bookings, but the moment a review says "expensive," the future discounting has begun.</p>

<h2>Two common mistakes</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The cost-plus trap</strong> — Pricing by "add construction and labor costs, put profit on top, and it comes to about this" misses the amount the market would actually pay. Cost is material that teaches the floor of a discount, not the ruler that sets the price.</li>
<li><strong>The constant-discount trap</strong> — The "cheapness" of an inn that is always cheap eventually gets recognized as "the real price." Climbing back from there requires content with a reason (a renovation, an added experience) — and it takes time.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The connection to RevPAR</h2>

<p>Finally, where the number called "rate" lives. A rate alone means nothing. A 10-room inn selling 8 rooms at ¥10,000, or 5 rooms at ¥15,000 — which is better? What makes that comparison possible is RevPAR (revenue per room), paired with occupancy. How to read the three numbers belongs to the revenue-management basics article, and the design that moves them to the dynamic pricing article. The three steps of this article are the stage before them: carry the market's ruler, choose a position, adjust with time. And when you raise the price, be able to add the reason in one line.</p>

<h2>The other side of pricing</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Competitor records decay</strong> — Five inns' price bands move with the season and the market. Re-shoot the ruler once a quarter. An old ruler becomes nothing but the basis of a mistaken position.</li>
<li><strong>The cheapest seat</strong> — Standing at the market's lowest price increases bookings — and increases the guests who come for the cheapness alone. A group that leaves the moment you raise the value. If you stand, stand on a reason (location, experience), not on cheapness.</li>
<li><strong>The steep slope of increases</strong> — As the fairness research teaches, acceptance of the same increase changes with the explanation. Even so, a large one-shot increase is resented even with the explanation. Price increases are stairs, not an elevator.</li>
<li><strong>Management that watches only price</strong> — Price is a reflection of how the rooms and the experience are valued. An inn that polishes only the ruler and not the rooms will, in time, be corrected by the market.</li>
<li><strong>Secrets in the numbers</strong> — A rate table is read by people. Hidden costs (cleaning fees, bathing taxes slipped in) create the same impression as the shovel on the snow morning. The inn that shows the total wins in the end.</li>
</ul>

<p>What to do this week is only this: write out the price bands of five nearby inns on the booking sites. Three points — low, middle, high — and a circle around where your inn sits. That single sheet becomes, from now on, the foundation of every discussion of your inn's prices.</p>
