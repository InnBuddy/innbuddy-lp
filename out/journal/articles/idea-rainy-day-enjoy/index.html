州を挙げて「雨季こそベストシーズン」と売る場所があります

インド南部のケーララ州は、モンスーンの雨が続く土地です。一般に観光のオフシーズンとされるこの雨季を、州は逆に売り出しています。ケーララ州観光局は公式に、雨季をアーユルヴェーダの治療に最適な季節として紹介しています。伝統的なテキストは雨季を治療に最適とし、空気は涼しく埃がなく、体が最も治療を受け入れられる — そう説明されるのです。雨季に合わせた宿泊プランを用意する施設もあります。「雨が降るから来ないで」ではなく「雨だからこそ来て」。州の規模でこの発想を実現しているのがケーララです。

雨上がりの匂いには、名前があります

雨が乾いた土に降ったときの、あの土の匂い。世界中の人が知っているこの匂いに、1964年、オーストラリアの研究者イザベル・ジョイ・ベアとリチャード・トーマスが、Nature誌の論文で「ペトリコール」と名付けました。ギリシャ語の「石」と「神々の血」から作った名前です。匂いの正体は、主に土の中の放線菌が作る「ゲオスミン」という物質。雨粒が土に当たると空気中に放たれ、人間はごくわずかな濃度でも感じ取れるといわれます。さらに興味深いのは、二人の研究者が1960年代のインドで、この雨の匂いを蒸留して作る香水が既に売られていることを確認していたことです。雨の匂いは、世界共通の感性でありながら、瓶詰めにして売られるほどの価値でもあったのです。

雨の日は、和傘の産地の歴史でもあります

雨を楽しむ道具の代表が傘です。日本の和傘の国内生産の約9割は、岐阜の加納地区です。江戸時代から、長良川流域の竹と美濃和紙、柿渋という材料に恵まれた土地で、分業による大量生産がこの産地を作りました。最盛期の昭和20年代半ばには年産1,000万本を超え、加納には500軒以上の傘屋があったといわれます。洋傘の普及とともに軒並み減り、今では数軒が続けるのみです。つまり、番傘を何本か宿に置くことは、雨の日の貸出道具を備えると同時に、一つの産地の歴史を預かることでもあります。骨の見える傘を差して濡れた道を歩くと、雨の日は写真の時間に変わります。

雨音を聞く建築は、もう建っています

雨の音を聞くために、新しいものは要りません。縁側は、雨を屋内と屋外の間で聞くための装置です。軒は雨だれの音を一定の間隔に並べ替え、雨戸は雨粒を太い音に変えます。録音した雨音を流すのではなく、本物の軒の下に椅子を一つ置きましょう。それが「聞こえるようにする」設計です。音を地域の資源として案内する視点は「音で記憶に残る」の記事で書きましたが、雨音はその中でも最も予報で計画できる音です。明日雨なら、明日は雨音の案内が出せます。

Rainy-day guidance tends, however far it goes, to become “it’s fine” guidance. It’s fine if you get wet; it’s fine if you change your plans. But for inns in rainy regions, rain is not a nuisance but the protagonist of the season. This article is about redesigning the rainy day from “covering a shortcoming” to “a value found only on that day.”

There is a place that sells “the rainy season is the best season” as a whole state

Kerala in southern India is a land of persistent monsoon rains. The state promotes this rainy season, generally regarded as tourism’s off-season, the other way around. Kerala Tourism officially presents the monsoon as the ideal season for Ayurvedic treatment. Traditional texts regard the monsoon as the best time for treatment: the air is cool and dust-free, and the body is most receptive to treatment — so it is explained. Some facilities offer stay plans timed for the monsoon. Not “don’t come because it rains,” but “come precisely because it rains.” Kerala is a place that has realized this idea on the scale of a state.

CASEIndia’s state of Kerala officially promotes the monsoon season, through its state tourism department, as the ideal season for Ayurveda — It rewrote the common wisdom of “monsoon = off-season” into “monsoon = the season of treatment and rejuvenation.” Redefining rain is possible even on the scale of a state.

Translated, the rainy-day guidance of Japanese inns is still stuck at “it’s fine.” The first step for a rainy-day card is not a list of alternatives for wet weather but a list of “things you can see, hear, and smell because it is raining.”

The smell after rain has a name

That earthy smell when rain falls on dry soil. In 1964, in a paper in the journal Nature, the Australian researchers Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Thomas named this smell, known to people all over the world, “petrichor.” It is a name made from the Greek for “stone” and “the blood of the gods.” The smell comes mainly from a substance called “geosmin,” produced by actinomycetes in the soil. When raindrops hit the soil it is released into the air, and humans are said to be able to sense it even at extremely low concentrations. What is more interesting is that the two researchers had confirmed that in 1960s India, a perfume made by distilling this smell of rain was already being sold. The smell of rain, while a universal sensibility, also had enough value to be bottled and sold.

The translation an inn can make is this: “When the rain stops, step outside for ten minutes. It’s the time of the smell of the earth.” Petrichor is something that happens for a short while after rain. An inn that guides guests to scent ends up guiding them to timing. The rainy day is also the history of a wagasa production area

The classic tool for enjoying rain is the umbrella. About 90% of the domestic production of Japanese wagasa umbrellas comes from the Kanō district of Gifu. Since the Edo period, mass production through division of labor built this production area, in a land blessed with the materials of bamboo from the Nagara River basin, Mino washi, and kakishibu persimmon tannin. At its peak in the mid-1940s to early 1950s (the mid-Shōwa 20s), annual production exceeded 10 million umbrellas, and Kanō is said to have had more than 500 umbrella makers. With the spread of Western umbrellas, they declined across the board, and now only a few continue. In other words, keeping a few bangasa umbrellas at the inn is both equipping it with rainy-day loan items and keeping in trust the history of a production area. Walk along a wet road under an umbrella whose ribs show, and the rainy day turns into a time for photos.

Architecture for listening to the rain is already built

Nothing new is needed to listen to the sound of rain. The engawa veranda is a device for listening to rain between indoors and outdoors. The eaves rearrange the sound of raindrops into regular intervals, and the amado storm shutters turn raindrops into a thick sound. Rather than playing recorded rain sounds, place one chair under real eaves. That is the design of “making it audible.” The viewpoint of guiding guests to sound as a regional resource was covered in the article “Remain in Memory with Sound - Ideas for Remaining in Memory with Bell, Wind Chime, River Sound,” and the sound of rain is the one sound among them that can best be planned for with a forecast. If it rains tomorrow, tomorrow you can offer guidance on the sound of rain.

What goes on a rainy-day card

  • A “because it’s raining” list — Things you can see because it is raining (the color of moss, the sheen of wet stone, mist), things you can hear (raindrops from the eaves, the sound of the river), things you can smell (the smell of earth, the way the scent of incense rises). Swap the list of alternatives for this.
  • Lending bangasa and rain boots — A bangasa is a tool that also makes a photo. Add one line beside the loan rack — “Mino wagasa from Gifu: about 90% of domestic production” — and the tool gains a history.
  • The ten minutes after rain — “When the rain stops, go outside to smell the earth.” Guidance on petrichor is the cheapest experience program there is.
  • Rainy-day photo spots — Raindrops on leaves, the sky reflected in puddles, stone paving that gains sheen when wet. A rainy day is the day photos change for the better most.
  • A chair under the eaves — A chair put out on the engawa or under the eaves only on rainy days. A seat reserved for the sound of rain.

The realities of a rainy day

  • Wet floors — Wet entrances lead to slips. Wiping the floor and setting out umbrella stands are the basic moves of rainy-day hospitality.
  • Humidity and mold — “The pleasure of rain” for guests and humidity management for the building are separate matters. There is more work to keep turning on rainy days: dehumidifying, ventilating, drying towels and linens.
  • Care at arrival — Arriving in the rain is the most tired moment of a trip. Having one towel and one cup of tea ready writes the rainy-day review.
  • Safety of walking courses — Inns in the mountains or along rivers should include cautions about rising water and landslides in their guidance. Design rainy-day walking courses separately from fine-weather ones.
  • Inns it suits — The rainier the region, the better it suits. A scarcity of sunny days can, depending on the guidance, become a season only that inn has.
