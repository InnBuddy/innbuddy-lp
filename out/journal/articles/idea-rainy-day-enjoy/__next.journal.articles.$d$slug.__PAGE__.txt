11:T227c,<p>Rainy-day guidance tends, however far it goes, to become “it’s fine” guidance. It’s fine if you get wet; it’s fine if you change your plans. But for inns in rainy regions, rain is not a nuisance but the protagonist of the season. This article is about redesigning the rainy day from “covering a shortcoming” to “a value found only on that day.”</p><h2>There is a place that sells “the rainy season is the best season” as a whole state</h2><p>Kerala in southern India is a land of persistent monsoon rains. The state promotes this rainy season, generally regarded as tourism’s off-season, the other way around. Kerala Tourism officially presents the monsoon as the ideal season for Ayurvedic treatment. Traditional texts regard the monsoon as the best time for treatment: the air is cool and dust-free, and the body is most receptive to treatment — so it is explained. Some facilities offer stay plans timed for the monsoon. Not “don’t come because it rains,” but “come precisely because it rains.” Kerala is a place that has realized this idea on the scale of a state.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>India’s state of Kerala officially promotes the monsoon season, through its state tourism department, as the ideal season for Ayurveda</strong> — It rewrote the common wisdom of “monsoon = off-season” into “monsoon = the season of treatment and rejuvenation.” Redefining rain is possible even on the scale of a state.</div><p>Translated, the rainy-day guidance of Japanese inns is still stuck at “it’s fine.” The first step for a rainy-day card is not a list of alternatives for wet weather but a list of “things you can see, hear, and smell because it is raining.”</p><h2>The smell after rain has a name</h2><p>That earthy smell when rain falls on dry soil. In 1964, in a paper in the journal Nature, the Australian researchers Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Thomas named this smell, known to people all over the world, “petrichor.” It is a name made from the Greek for “stone” and “the blood of the gods.” The smell comes mainly from a substance called “geosmin,” produced by actinomycetes in the soil. When raindrops hit the soil it is released into the air, and humans are said to be able to sense it even at extremely low concentrations. What is more interesting is that the two researchers had confirmed that in 1960s India, a perfume made by distilling this smell of rain was already being sold. The smell of rain, while a universal sensibility, also had enough value to be bottled and sold.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>The smell of earth after rain is called “petrichor,” named in a 1964 paper in Nature</strong> — It comes mainly from geosmin produced by actinomycetes in the soil. Humans can detect it at extremely low concentrations. In 1960s India, a perfume of the smell of rain was also being made.</div><p>The translation an inn can make is this: “When the rain stops, step outside for ten minutes. It’s the time of the smell of the earth.” Petrichor is something that happens for a short while after rain. An inn that guides guests to scent ends up guiding them to timing. That smell is directly linked to memory was covered in the article “Remain in Memory with Scent - Ideas for Remaining in Memory for Overseas Guests with Scent, Tea, Incense.” A walk after the rain may be the cheapest implementation of a memorable experience.</p><h2>The rainy day is also the history of a wagasa production area</h2><p>The classic tool for enjoying rain is the umbrella. About 90% of the domestic production of Japanese wagasa umbrellas comes from the Kanō district of Gifu. Since the Edo period, mass production through division of labor built this production area, in a land blessed with the materials of bamboo from the Nagara River basin, Mino washi, and kakishibu persimmon tannin. At its peak in the mid-1940s to early 1950s (the mid-Shōwa 20s), annual production exceeded 10 million umbrellas, and Kanō is said to have had more than 500 umbrella makers. With the spread of Western umbrellas, they declined across the board, and now only a few continue. In other words, keeping a few bangasa umbrellas at the inn is both equipping it with rainy-day loan items and keeping in trust the history of a production area. Walk along a wet road under an umbrella whose ribs show, and the rainy day turns into a time for photos.</p><h2>Architecture for listening to the rain is already built</h2><p>Nothing new is needed to listen to the sound of rain. The engawa veranda is a device for listening to rain between indoors and outdoors. The eaves rearrange the sound of raindrops into regular intervals, and the amado storm shutters turn raindrops into a thick sound. Rather than playing recorded rain sounds, place one chair under real eaves. That is the design of “making it audible.” The viewpoint of guiding guests to sound as a regional resource was covered in the article “Remain in Memory with Sound - Ideas for Remaining in Memory with Bell, Wind Chime, River Sound,” and the sound of rain is the one sound among them that can best be planned for with a forecast.
