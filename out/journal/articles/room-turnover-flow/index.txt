1b:T2145,<p>Room turnover is <strong>not "cleaning" but "a flow that cuts the guest’s waiting time."</strong> The same room still carrying the traces of the previous guest before noon becomes, by evening, the room welcoming the next. This article organizes the design of time that lies behind the cleaning procedure.</p>

<h2>Kabuki perfected the conversion centuries ago</h2>

<p>Kabuki has a staging device called <strong>hayagawari</strong> — the quick change. A single actor, within one scene, transforms in an instant in status, age, good and evil, sometimes even gender. Two techniques support it. <strong>Hikinuki</strong> — two costumes layered, the outer one held by quick-release threads; when the kōken stage assistant pulls the thread, the under-costume appears. <strong>Hayaganae</strong> — the conversion backstage, at an improvised dressing-table in the wings or inside the agmaku curtain, changing costume, wig, and makeup alike.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Kabuki’s "hikinuki" is a device in which costumes held by quick-release threads are worn in layers, and the kōken pulls the thread to strip off the outer costume, changing it in an instant on stage. "Hayaganae" is the technique of changing even wigs at an improvised dressing-table set up in the stage wings, behind the sets, or inside the agmaku curtain</strong> — The instant of the conversion is bought with the length of the preparation before it. (Based on the National Theatre of Japan’s kabuki commentary and dictionary entries.)</div>

<p>A guest room is the same. A checked-out room turns, hours later, into "someone else’s room" — the lodging business is an industry of backstage performances, staging <strong>a quick-change of guest rooms every day</strong>. And as with kabuki, the key to success lies not in the moment’s technique but in the preparation. The cart’s assigned position, the linen stock, the routine’s fixed form — these are tomorrow morning’s <strong>quick-release threads</strong>.</p>

<h2>3 wait times — waiting is born outside the room too</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>The arrived guest’s wait</strong> — The time hearing "your room is not ready yet." Fatigue and anxiety stack: the most expensive wait of all.</li>
<li><strong>The housekeeping staff’s wait</strong> — With checkout times scattered, the time spent waiting for the next room to open piles up.</li>
<li><strong>The next guest’s wait</strong> — The time early check-ins and late check-outs cannot be accommodated. Accommodate them, and it becomes revenue and ratings.</li>
</ol>

<p>Waiting arises not only inside the room but <strong>between people, and between jobs</strong>. Designing turnover is the work of cutting all three at once.</p>

<h2>The psychology of waiting — "how it feels" over how long it is</h2>

<p>Before speeding up the cleaning, there is a study worth knowing. In 1985, David Meister of Harvard Business School showed, in "The Psychology of Waiting Lines," that the experience of a wait is decided not by its actual length but by <strong>how it feels</strong>. Three principles that work especially well on a property’s floor, translated:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>A wait that has begun feels shorter</strong> — The moment we take the luggage, the check-in experience has "begun." Hand over a claim tag; guide them to the lounge. Sitting and looking at a map waits shorter than standing and talking at the front desk.</li>
<li><strong>A wait with a visible end feels shorter</strong> — Not "we are preparing it now" but <strong>"your room will be ready at 3 p.m."</strong> People can endure a wait when they are told when it ends.</li>
<li><strong>A wait with a reason feels shorter</strong> — "We are full today, so cleaning is taking extra time." A single line of reason changes the quality of the wait.</li>
</ul>

<p>The thinking that dissolves anxiety before arrival (seeing access guidance and the design of waiting as a line) is handled by <a href="#guest-journey-map">The Whole Guest Experience: Mapping the Touchpoints</a>. Here, let us return to the design of the waiting itself.</p>

<h2>3 flow devices</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Align checkout times</strong> — By aligning at 10 or 11 a.m., the housekeeping staff’s "waiting for the next room" shrinks. Guests, told in advance that "checkout is at 10," can plan their morning packing too.</li>
<li><strong>Set the cleaning order</strong> — Start with the rooms with early check-in requests; leave consecutive-night rooms for later. Deciding the order alone changes the day’s total waiting time.</li>
<li><strong>One checklist on one sheet</strong> — Procedure, checkpoints, and a completion sign, together on a single page. The quality of cleaning itself (the story of cleaning as an act of welcome, and the three-layer checklist) is handled by <a href="#cleaning-checklist-experience">Turning Housekeeping From "Cost" Into "Experience": A Checklist</a>, and how to write standards by <a href="#service-standard-basics">Service Basics: The "Standard" That Does Not Vary by Person</a>. This article takes the time side.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Operation — write out the times, share the aim</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Write down the cleaning time, room by room</strong> — In pencil is fine. Variation is not material for scolding but material for improvement (rooms with more storage, rooms with larger baths — each room’s habits come into view).</li>
<li><strong>Share that "the aim is cutting waits"</strong> — The essence of housekeeping is not polishing floors but <strong>not making the next guest wait</strong>. Share this one line, and ideas for the order will be born on the floor.</li>
<li><strong>Sell the time you create</strong> — Being able to answer early check-in and late checkout requests is, as is, a service. The other business of the check-in moment is handled by <a href="#upgrade-sales-checkin">Upgrade Sales: One Room Up at Check-In</a>.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The other side — when speed hurts the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Speed kills quality</strong> — Thoroughness of cleaning is the item that drops ratings hardest the moment it slips. Build speed with procedure and preparation — never by cutting steps.</li>
<li><strong>The side effects of aligning</strong> — Align checkout flatly, and the guest who wants to leave late feels rushed. Pick up late requests as "late checkout," and both sides gain.</li>
<li><strong>Depending on the magic of "3 p.m."</strong> — Announcing the time shapes how the wait feels; it does not speed the turnover itself. The announcement and the improvement of conversion are separate jobs.</li>
<li><strong>The pressure of speed</strong> — When time records feed individual evaluations, the floor starts dressing the records. Use records to evaluate rooms and order, not people.</li>
<li><strong>A failed conversion shows to the audience</strong> — Accidentally opening a room mid-cleaning is no different from a quick-change failing in view of the seats. Managing keys and the progress board is preparation as important as speed.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is writing tomorrow’s cleaning order on one sheet. Checked-out rooms, early-request rooms, consecutive-night rooms — in that order, add the times taken, for one day. In a week, you will see where your property’s backstage conversion stands, and its thickest quick-release thread. Turnover is not a contest of cleaning speed; it is <strong>the design of waiting time</strong>.</p>

<h2>1. 为什么要减少等待时间——3次等待</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>办理登机手续等待时间：</strong>当客人登记入住并被告知“我们还没有房间”时，他们会抱怨等待时间。如果周转速度很快，客人会很高兴能够无需等待就进入房间。减少等待时间可以提高客人满意度。</li>

<li><strong>等待下一位客人的时间：</strong>如果周转速度快，可以满足提早入住或延迟退房的要求。当周转速度快时，您可以响应下一位客人“早到”和“晚走”的要求，提高客户满意度。</li>
</ul>

<h2>2. 流程设计——3个想法</h2>

<li><strong>优先清洁：</strong>通过优先清洁，您可以减少等待时间。例如：优先清洁下一位客人要求提前入住的房间，或推迟清洁客人入住多晚的房间。通过设置优先级，您可以减少客人的等待时间。</li>
<li><strong>一页清洁清单：</strong>通过将清洁清单整理成一张纸，您可以明确清洁程序并缩短清洁时间。检查表应包括清洁步骤、检查点和完成标志。拥有清单可以让清洁人员更轻松地了解该做什么并减少清洁时间。</li>
