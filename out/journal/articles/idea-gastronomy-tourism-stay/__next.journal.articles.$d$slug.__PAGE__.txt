11:T1cc6,<p>Gastronomy tourism is not travel that places eating somewhere along the way to the purpose. It is eating becoming the very reason for the trip. Yet the “food offering” at many inns stops at serving meals. This article is about extending eating into “meeting, knowing, making, and taking home,” and designing the whole stay as a food journey.</p><h2>How a city of 180,000 became a capital of food</h2><p>San Sebastián, a seaside city in the Basque Country of northern Spain. Its population is about 185,000, yet 16 Michelin-starred restaurants are concentrated within about 25 km of the city, giving it one of the highest densities per capita in the world. Arzak, the celebrated restaurant that won Spain’s first three stars, is also in this city. So why did food concentrate in such a small city? The key was not the number of restaurants but the system. Local chefs studied in France, brought techniques home, and had a culture of “sharing,” teaching one another. In 2011, the Basque Culinary Center opened as a university faculty teaching cooking, drawing students from around the world. Moreover, in 2015, the “World Forum on Gastronomy Tourism,” co-organized by the UN World Tourism Organization (UNWTO) and this school, began in this city. Top restaurants, culinary education, international discussion — a “food ecosystem” is complete within a single city.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>San Sebastián in Spain has 16 Michelin-starred restaurants concentrated within about 25 km for a population of about 185,000. In 2011, the Basque Culinary Center opened as a university culinary faculty, and from 2015 the city became the stage for UNWTO’s World Forum on Gastronomy Tourism</strong> — What a “capital of food” really is, is not individual restaurants but a system in which education, industry, and tourism come together.</div><h2>Tsuruoka turned “food found anywhere” into food found only here</h2><p>Japan has a story with the same structure. In 2014, Tsuruoka City in Yamagata Prefecture became the first in Japan to join the gastronomy field of UNESCO’s “Creative Cities Network.” The food that triggered this was not special cuisine but the everyday food of the Shōnai region: dadacha-mame soybeans, kandara-jiru winter cod soup, and heirloom crops. Faith, domain governance, and heirloom crops. What Tsuruoka put together was the “history” of the land’s food. Since the designation, town-building with food as its entrance has continued: tours of a long-established pickle shop, shōjin ryōri (Buddhist vegetarian cuisine) experiences at temples, tours where visitors harvest dadacha-mame in the field and eat them on the spot. In other words, Tsuruoka’s method was not winning stars but giving the land’s food “reasons, stories, and experiences.” This is a design any inn, however small, can imitate.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>In 2014, Tsuruoka City in Yamagata Prefecture became the first in Japan to join the gastronomy field of the UNESCO Creative Cities Network</strong> — The core of the evaluation was organizing the “history” of a food culture tied to heirloom crops such as dadacha-mame and to faith and domain governance. Town-building through food continues, including experiences of harvesting in the field and eating on the spot.</div><h2>Extend “eating” into four stages</h2><ul><li><strong>Meet</strong> — Have guests meet the makers before the meal. Going along to the morning market, a tour of the pickle shop, a farmer’s field. A meal after seeing faces tastes different.</li><li><strong>Know</strong> — Write a history for each dish. Since when have these beans been in this land? On what kind of cold day is this soup a feast? It is a scaled-down version of Tsuruoka’s method.</li><li><strong>Make</strong> — A cooking experience of just one dish is enough. Miso rice balls, a seasonal simmered dish. The memory of making lasts several times longer than the memory of eating.</li><li><strong>Take home</strong> — Connect the last food of the trip to a souvenir. Jam, tea, pickles. Take them home with a recipe card, and the land rises up once more at home.</li></ul><h2>How an inn can turn the town with food</h2><ul><li><strong>A “day of food” course</strong> — Morning market (meet) → midday experience (make) → evening meal (know) → souvenirs on the last day (take home). It is just linking the inn’s guidance into a day through food.</li><li><strong>Send guests out to the town’s restaurants</strong> — Rather than increasing the inn’s own food output, guide guests to the town’s good restaurants. In San Sebastián too, the capital of food was not one restaurant but the whole city. The inn becomes the table of contents of food.</li><li><strong>The season’s “only now” on one sheet</strong> — A small calendar listing this month’s ingredients and next month’s. A single piece of guidance conveys that this is a land with things you can eat only once a year.</li></ul><h2>The responsibility of those who handle food</h2><ul><li><strong>Hygiene and food poisoning</strong> — Hands-on food and take-home food are also subject to hygiene management. Design temperature, expiry, and containers from the start.</li><li><strong>Allergies and raw food</strong> — Explain raw food (raw eggs, raw fish, meat) clearly to overseas guests. Advance interviews about allergies are a prerequisite of a food journey.</li><li><strong>Don’t exaggerate</strong> — Words like “Japan’s best” and “legendary” hold only when there is a real history behind them. Tsuruoka’s strength was description, not exaggeration.</li><li><strong>Relations with the town’s restaurants</strong> — If everything is completed within the inn’s own food, the town’s food grows thin. A culture of sending guests out nurtures both the inn and the town.</li></ul><h2>InnBuddy VIEW</h2><p>San Sebastián became a city that equipped its food with education and even international conferences; Tsuruoka became a city that gave its everyday food a history and experiences.
