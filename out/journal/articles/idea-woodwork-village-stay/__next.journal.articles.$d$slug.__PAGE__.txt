11:T2252,<p>There are mountains of texts that describe the scent of wood as “pleasant.” This article starts from a little-known fact: that scent already has a name. Once you know the name, the landscape of a woodworking village suddenly becomes readable.</p><h2>The scent of wood has a worldwide name</h2><p>The word “shinrin-yoku” (forest bathing) was coined in 1982 by Akiyama Tomohide, Director-General of the Forestry Agency, on the model of “nikkōyoku” (sunbathing) and “kaisuiyoku” (sea bathing). In July of that year, the Forestry Agency proposed the “Shinrin-yoku Concept.” This word has now spread around the world as “Shinrin-yoku” just as it is, and it is used in Japanese in the overseas wellness industry. The experience of the scent of wood already has a world language. Simply adding one line, “Shinrin-yoku (forest bathing),” to the experience of sitting on a hinoki cypress bench makes the explanation reach directly into a part of the world. There is no other material with which to start the guidance on the scent of wood at such an advantage.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>“Shinrin-yoku” was coined in 1982 by Akiyama Tomohide, Director-General of the Forestry Agency</strong> — In July of the same year, the Forestry Agency proposed the “Shinrin-yoku Concept.” It is now a world language originating in Japan, used in Japanese as “Shinrin-yoku” in the overseas wellness industry.</div><h2>Surprising numbers in a country of wood</h2><p>Japan is one of the world’s leading forest countries. About two-thirds of its land (about 25 million hectares) is forest, of which about 10 million hectares are planted forests of sugi (Japanese cedar) and hinoki cypress. Yet the wood self-sufficiency rate was 43.0% in 2023 (Reiwa 5) (Forestry Agency, Timber Supply and Demand Table). More than half of the wood is imported. In other words, the wood of “the country of wood” is not being used enough. The planted forests established after the war are now just reaching harvest age.</p><p>What these numbers mean for an inn is simple. Switching one fitting or one tray to local wood is not a matter of atmosphere but a matter of supply and demand. An inn in a woodworking village is “an entrance to a wood-producing area,” and using wood leads to maintaining the landscape. The day an inn in a wood-producing area can proudly write “This floor is local sugi” is a day when that region’s mountains are one step healthier.</p><h2>900 years without a single nail — a Norwegian church</h2><p>In Urnes, Norway, a wooden church built in the 12th century still stands. In 1979, it became the first building in Norway to be inscribed as a World Heritage Site. What makes this church interesting is that it was built by assembling timber without a single nail, applying Viking shipbuilding techniques. The skill of building ships built a church. Skills travel from one use to another. Of the wooden churches (stave churches) said to have numbered about 1,000 in Norway at their peak, only 28 survive. What remains are only those that kept being repaired for 900 years.</p><p>The essence is this. Woodworking skill is a skill of “joining and carrying on.” Fitting one piece to another, splicing where something is missing, repairing when something breaks. The joints (tsugite and shiguchi) of Japanese wooden architecture follow the same idea. If an inn has old fittings or ranma transoms, they are an exhibition of “things that have been carried on.” With one line in the tour guidance, “Try looking for the nails,” children and adults alike begin to read the building through their skin.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>The wooden church at Urnes (Norway, 12th century) was built without a single nail, applying Viking shipbuilding techniques</strong> — Norway’s first World Heritage Site, in 1979. There are 28 surviving stave churches, and those that remain are the ones that kept being repaired.</div><h2>A province that paid in carpenters instead of taxes</h2><p>Japan has an even more remarkable history. The Yōrō Code, put into effect in 757, set out a special tax system for Hida Province (today’s northern Gifu Prefecture) alone. Because Hida was a mountain province where rice could not be grown, it sent woodworking technicians to the capital in place of taxes (yō and chō). Ten from each village, a little over 100 people every year. Their term was one year, and they are said to have taken part in building some of Japan’s oldest temples and shrines, such as Tōdaiji, Ishiyamadera, and Kasuga Taisha. Their compensation in the capital was rice and salt, and fifty days of work is said to have equaled one person’s food for a year. The “craftsmen of Hida” (Hida no takumi) appear in the Man’yōshū and the Konjaku Monogatari; the system disappeared in the Kamakura period, but the skills live on today in the floats of the Takayama Festival and in Hida Shunkei lacquerware.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>From 757, Hida Province sent woodworking craftsmen to the capital in place of taxes (the Hida-no-takumi system)</strong> — They are said to have taken part in building Tōdaiji and Kasuga Taisha. Woodworking skill once served in this country as a “tax” paid by a region.</div><p>Why tell this story at an inn? The skills of a woodworking village were, before being a tourism resource, once the core of the regional economy. When an inn works with local woodworkers to furnish its tableware and furniture, it is putting that region’s former “currency” back into circulation.</p><h2>Start from plane shavings</h2><p>Practice can start from shavings. Wood shavings fresh from the plane are at the peak of their scent. A scent sachet filled with these “kannakuzu” (plane shavings) becomes the smallest souvenir of an inn in a woodworking village.
