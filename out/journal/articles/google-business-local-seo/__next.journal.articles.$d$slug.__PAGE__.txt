11:T1deb,<p>Picture the traveler who has just decided on a destination, and the screen they open next: the map. Search "area name + inn," and about three inns appear at the top of the map, with a long line continuing below. Getting into that top three — the place called the Map Pack — is the first win on the map. And the character of the people running this search matters: people who have already decided to visit that region, or who are in it right now. A demand different from the "where should we stay" browsing of booking sites. For a regional inn, managing the map is not an advertisement that reaches a distant, unspecified many; it is building a mechanism by which the person who has already decided to come is sure to find you.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>In a private survey (2022, 1,240 respondents), 99.4% named Google Maps among the map apps they had used — far ahead of the iPhone's stock Maps (43.5%) and Yahoo! Maps (35.6%). Worldwide, more than one billion people a month are said to use the map (per 2020 reporting)</strong> — The flow of people searching for inns on the map converges, in effect, on a single place.</div>

<p>Now, there is no shortage of articles explaining how to win on the map. Stuff in keywords, collect reviews, keep posting. But the first document a property should read is not a technique article — it is the guidelines for local business information on Google, published by Google itself. What makes the document interesting is that, more than a list of what to do, it plainly enumerates what not to do. This article organizes the basics in a slightly unusual order: working backward from that list of prohibitions.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Google's guidelines for local business information require registering the business name as the name actually in use, and prohibit stuffing the name with catchphrases, business hours, area or service keywords, phone numbers, or URLs</strong> — Many of the "techniques" for raising rank have been on Google's own list of prohibitions from the start.</div>

<p>Why read from the prohibition list? Because profiles that violate the guidelines may be corrected by Google — or, in some cases, suspended entirely. A first suspension can be appealed for recovery; a second is said to be effectively equivalent to deletion. Losing the inn's signboard on the map without the inn ever knowing — that risk sits quietly buried inside the measures recommended as "rank-boosting techniques."</p>

<h2>What remains when the prohibitions are removed — four points: information, photos, reviews, posts</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Completeness of information</strong> — Name, address, phone, hours, website: all of it, accurate and complete. A profile with blank fields is a signboard with missing characters. Check-in times, holiday-season hours, the presence of parking. Filling every fillable field is the cheapest measure there is.</li>
<li><strong>Photos</strong> — Add two or three a week, regularly. The same principles as booking-site photos (see the hotel photographs article) work on the map. One photo each time the season turns keeps the profile updated as "a living inn."</li>
<li><strong>Reviews</strong> — An asset separate from booking-site reviews. The count and the score on the map are judgment material for the people searching there. Note, though, that compensation or prizes for reviews are prohibited by the guidelines. The farthest you may go is "we would be glad if you wrote one."</li>
<li><strong>Posts</strong> — The profile has a "posts" feature for putting out seasonal information and events about once a week. With the inn's news flowing steadily, it reads as "an inn that is open now" to whoever is searching.</li>
</ol>

<h2>The area name belongs in the address, not the name</h2>

<p>There is a technique that says "adding a region or service name to the inn's name raises your placement." But the guidelines' list of prohibitions points at exactly this act. So what should a locally rooted inn do? The answer lies in the mechanics of the map itself. The inn's address already ties it to area searches on the map. The context of the area can be told naturally enough through the description, the posts, and the photos. Without naming itself "the inn of such-and-such town," an inn with an address is correctly found by the person searching in such-and-such town.</p>

<h2>The weekly routine — ten minutes</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Once a week, ten minutes</strong> — Add two or three photos, and one post. That alone keeps the profile moving.</li>
<li><strong>Reply to reviews within 72 hours</strong> — The craft of the reply's wording is left to the review-replies article. A map review is a public conversation with the people searching the map.</li>
<li><strong>Once a month, record the display</strong> — Check and record how your inn appears on the map, once a month, with the same search. This is both a ranking check and the "signboard inspection" described below.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The other side of the map</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Third-party edits and false reports</strong> — Profiles can change through suggestions from users, and there have been incidents of inns vanishing from the map through false "permanently closed" reports. The monthly check is not for rank; it is inspecting whether your signboard still stands correct.</li>
<li><strong>Freshness of information</strong> — If a temporary closure or a renovation is not reflected in the map, the "it was closed" experience remains in a review. The map is not a signboard; it is a front desk.</li>
<li><strong>Review manipulation is prohibited</strong> — Rewards, prizes, and self-reviewing are all guideline violations. Removal requests are limited to reviews containing factual errors.</li>
<li><strong>The reply is the inn's face</strong> — Long excuses and wording that identifies the reviewer backfire. Write replies for the people who will read them in the future.</li>
<li><strong>People in the photos</strong> — Consent from staff; no other guests in the frame. Photos on the map are public material.</li>
<li><strong>The map is a front desk, not a signboard</strong> — Calls to the number on the profile come from people who mean to stay at your inn. Who answers the ringing phone, and when, is part of running the map.</li>
</ul>

<p>Your inn's pin is already standing. Even if you do nothing, the map carries your inn. The question is who is tending that pin. What you can do this week takes ten minutes — open the profile, check the business hours, add one photo. Who waits on the map is the person who has already decided to come. Showing that person the right signboard: that is the foundation of local SEO for a regional inn.</p>

<div class="note">This article is intended for sharing ideas. Google's specifications and guidelines change; check the latest official guidelines. The survey cited is a private-sector survey.</div>
