11:T1b0e,<p>The senses most easily forgotten, and most strongly remembered, are scent and sound. What photographic marketing (booking sites, social media) conveys is sight. Which is exactly why <strong>the senses a photograph cannot capture become the differentiation</strong>. This article organizes the design that builds these two — scent and sound — into operations. How scent summons memory (that smell alone holds a path reaching emotion and memory directly, without passing the checkpoint of reason) was treated in detail in the "remain in memory with scent" article, so it is not repeated here. This article’s subject is one step further — <strong>how to design scent and sound</strong>.</p>

<h2>"Listening" to a fragrance — the design of time that kōdō teaches</h2>

<p>In the world of Japan’s kōdō, the way of incense, a fragrance is said to be <strong>"listened to"</strong> — kiku, "to listen" — not "smelled." There is a deep reason for the choice of this verb. The fragrances burned in kōdō rise, change, and fade. Incense has been treated not as an art that "is there whenever you look," like a painting or a sculpture, but as having <strong>the same structure as music — an art of time, with a beginning and an end</strong>. It is the culture that gave birth to kumikō, the game of listening to fragrances and telling them apart.</p>

<p>Here is a hint for properties. At most properties, scent and sound are "left running." The aroma diffuser, the lobby background music — once switched on, no one ever stops them. But if the essence of fragrance lies in time, what should be designed is <strong>the design of time</strong> — when it scents, when it sounds, when it is quiet. The "listening" of kōdō is precisely this viewpoint: making the fragrance not the background but the protagonist.</p>

<h2>Designing scent — 3 basics</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>One fragrance</strong> — Not the scent of a bar of soap, but one fragrance that is the property itself. Do not let them blend. The details of selection and placement are handled in the "hotel scent" article.</li>
<li><strong>Concentrated placement</strong> — The lobby, the corridor, the room entrance. Do not scatter it; place it where it will be remembered. The same thinking with which kōdō set its fragrance in the place of "one time, one meeting."</li>
<li><strong>Safety and consideration</strong> — Managing concentration, and alternatives for guests sensitive to fragrance (fragrance-free rooms, ventilation). The thinking on allergies is shared with the food-accommodation article.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Designing sound — 3 basics, and silence</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>One sound</strong> — Not "playing background music," but having the property’s own sound. Narrow the family of pieces to one.</li>
<li><strong>Volume zones</strong> — Lively at the front desk, silent in the rooms, warm in the restaurant. Set the band of volume for each place.</li>
<li><strong>The sound of arrival</strong> — The chime of the door, the first greeting. The sound of the first five seconds of the experience becomes the sound that is remembered.</li>
</ul>

<p>And what must not be forgotten is <strong>the design of silence</strong>. A room where nothing plays is also a designed sound. Japan’s living culture has listened to the voices of insects as voices, and made coolness with the sound of wind chimes — a sensibility not of erasing sound but of choosing which sounds to listen to. When quietness is not blank space but <strong>chosen quietness</strong>, it connects also to protecting the peak of the experience (the same thinking as pulling service back at peak moments).</p>

<h2>Telling it — what a photograph cannot capture, in words</h2>

<p>Scent and sound do not come through in photographs. Which is exactly why they are worth conveying in words. On the room card and on social, write one line each of "this property’s fragrance" and "this property’s sound." "Mornings begin with the voices of birds." "At the end of the corridor, the scent of hinoki cypress." <strong>A property that puts into words what cannot be photographed gives guests the impression of managing all five senses.</strong> When reviews begin to speak of "that fragrance" and "that quietness," the design is working.</p>

<h2>The other side — when designing the senses exhausts the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Pushing fragrance on guests</strong> — For sensitive guests, the fragrance itself is the complaint. Make the fragrance-free option selectable at booking.</li>
<li><strong>Forcing sound</strong> — In-room music must be switchable off. For guests seeking an irreplaceable quietness, background music is an obstacle.</li>
<li><strong>The blind spot of habituation</strong> — Staff grow used to the property’s own scent and stop noticing. Once a month, ask someone on their first day: "What did you smell?"</li>
<li><strong>The mistake "expensive = designed"</strong> — The design of scent is a design of choice and placement, not of expensive aromatics. One fragrance correctly placed is stronger than ten kinds scattered about.</li>
<li><strong>The sounds of working hours</strong> — The vacuum at midnight, the sounds of early-morning preparation. The design of sound is also a design that protects the guest’s hours.</li>
<li><strong>Recorded insect voices</strong> — A real wind chime, the real voices of birds stay in memory more than sound from a speaker. Before adding equipment, start by opening the window.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is, this week, to walk your own property for ten minutes with your eyes closed. What you write in the memo: three scents and three sounds. You should notice more sounds than expected, and fewer scents. That is the inventory. Scent and sound are "the brand a camera cannot shoot." Convey the visual in photographs, build memory with scent and sound — the design of the five senses begins from those two lines.</p>

<div class="note">This article is intended for sharing ideas. The practices of kōdō and "listening" to incense are introduced as a general account of Japanese traditional culture. Please start small with fragrances, with attention to concentration, hygiene, and allergy considerations.</div>

<h2>3.声音设计</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>与您的品牌相匹配的“一个声音”</strong> ——不只是“播放背景音乐”，而是旅馆的“声音”。</li>
<li><strong>体积区</strong> ——接待=热闹，客房=安静，餐厅=温馨。</li>
<li><strong>《到来的声音》</strong> ——门铃响起，问候。声音的前 5 秒。</li>
</ul>
<h2>4. 传输</h2>
<ul>
<li>通过客房卡和SNS传达“气味和声音”——用文字来说是“不能拍照的东西”。</li>
<li>示例：评论中提及“气味”和“音乐”，重复率增加（参考值）。</li>
</ul>

<h2>为什么现在值得关注？</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>客人期待的变化：</strong>与五年前相比，客人对“香与乐——五种感官都能记住的住宿”的期望已经发生了变化。过去“有了更好”的服务，如今已变成“没有就难以被选择”。适应这些变化，是住宿设施持续经营的前提。</li>
<li><strong>与地区关系：</strong>体验设计举措增加了整个地区的价值，而不仅仅是住宿本身。通过与当地生产者和周边商户合作，旅馆的行动会成为“当地故事”。社区共同参与，才更容易长期持续。</li>
<li><strong>不增加员工负担：</strong>新举措不应增加员工负担。通过建立机制，用一张表或每次15分钟的学习来运转，就能在不增加员工负担的情况下持续改进。</li>
</ul>

<h2>避免失败的3个要点</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>从小处开始——先做好一件事：</strong>一开始就想全部改变，往往难以持续。先从一件事开始。可以从一间客房、一个货架或一句话开始；即使效果不理想，影响也小，更容易调整。</li>
<li><strong>记录——用一张表看清楚：</strong>把行动记录在一张表里，效果就能被看见。你会知道哪些做法有效、哪些需要调整，记录也会成为下一轮改进的依据。</li>
<li><strong>持续——每周一次，每次15分钟：</strong>新行动需要持续。每周留出15分钟，逐渐形成习惯，最后才会成为团队文化。</li>
</ol>
