11:T1f62,<p>The experience of “staying at a farm” has, around the world, been nurtured as “a policy for protecting farms.” It was not a system for tourists but a system for rural communities. This article draws out a framework usable for inn experiences from the designs of two systems, in Italy and France.</p><h2>Italy, 1985 — a law with the condition that “farming is the main business”</h2><p>In 1970s Italy, the earnings of small farms were deteriorating, and young people were leaving for the cities. When farmers began taking in guests for lodging and meals, they were at first sometimes fined for “unlicensed lodging business.” The turning point came in 1985, when the first law setting out farms’ reception of tourists (agriturismo) was passed. The clever point of this system is that it attached conditions in exchange for granting preferential treatment: income from farming must exceed income from tourism. In other words, its positioning is not “farmers who want to run an inn” but “guests for the sake of continuing to farm.” This law became a model for other European countries, and according to research as of 2022, Italy’s agriturismi number more than 25,000, with an economic scale of more than 1.5 billion euros a year. The law stopped tourism from devouring farming.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>In 1985, Italy passed its first law setting out agriturismo, supporting farms’ reception of tourists on the condition that “farming income exceeds tourism income”</strong> — More than 25,000 farms and more than 1.5 billion euros a year (research as of 2022). The reversed design of “not giving up the fields for the sake of tourism” is the core of its institutional success.</div><h2>France, 1951 — inviting guests to protect empty houses</h2><p>The French story solved the same problem from another direction. In postwar rural areas, houses were falling into disrepair due to the agricultural slump, and the population was leaving. In 1951, Senator Émile Aubert opened the first “gîte rural” (country rental house) in an Alpine mountain village. Restoring a decaying farm building as a holiday facility and renting it to city people. In 1955, the national organization “Gîtes de France” was launched, and its first guidebook listed 146 properties. Today it has grown into Europe’s largest network of countryside stays, with more than 60,000 properties. Grades are shown by the number of ears of wheat. France’s invention was the shift in thinking of “renting to city people in order to protect country houses.” Guests were not only for profit but a device for keeping buildings and villages alive.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>In 1951, French Senator Émile Aubert opened the first “gîte rural” and began a network that restores decaying farmhouses as holiday rentals</strong> — 146 properties when the national organization was launched in 1955, more than 60,000 today. The discovery of “inviting guests to protect empty houses” is the prototype of today’s countryside stays.</div><h2>Japan’s path — from the Rural Vacation Act to “nōhaku”</h2><p>In Japan, the 1994 Act on the Promotion of Rural Vacations in Agricultural, Mountain, and Fishing Villages was the first step, and exchange was promoted under the name “green tourism,” with European rural stays in view. Since then, the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries has made fostering “nōhaku” (stay-based travel in agricultural, mountain, and fishing villages) a goal, and by fiscal 2023 it had created 656 nōhaku areas, achieving its target, and the policy is said to have moved into a growth phase. The distinctive feature of the Japanese model is that the center is not lodging itself but “farm experiences and exchange with people.” Doing fieldwork together, gathering around the table. Set alongside Italy’s condition that “farming is the main business” and France’s design of “renting to protect the house,” what Japan’s nōhaku has valued comes into view.</p><h2>A farming timetable an inn can use</h2><ul><li><strong>“Today in the field” in one line</strong> — A farm’s timetable changes every day with the season and the weather. “This morning we pinched the tomato tips.” “Today is potato harvest day.” Just putting one line at the entrance turns the work of farming into something to watch.</li><li><strong>The first harvest to breakfast</strong> — Send the field’s first harvest to breakfast. Not a story but a fact makes breakfast shine (the method of the article “Convey Region with Breakfast - Ideas for Breakfast Putting Regional Story on One Dish”).</li><li><strong>A line of history for farm tools and seeds</strong> — “This hoe was used in such-and-such place.” “The seeds for these beans have been kept going at our farm for 20 years.” Tools and seeds are the field’s longest memory.</li><li><strong>Be honest about “days you can help” and “days to just watch”</strong> — In the busy farming season, help can sometimes be a nuisance. Write “today is a watching-only day” in the guidance too. Honesty is a farmer’s credit.</li><li><strong>A children’s field duty</strong> — Entrust one task, such as watering or harvesting, as a “duty.” The work ends in a day, but the memory of being trusted remains.</li></ul><h2>The responsibility of turning farming into tourism</h2><ul><li><strong>Farm machinery and chemicals</strong> — Entering fields where tractors are moving, or areas where pesticides and fertilizers are being applied, carries risks of accidents and to health. Mark the boundaries, and leave spraying days out of the guidance.</li><li><strong>Contact with animals</strong> — Hand-washing after touching livestock is essential, especially for children and elderly people with weaker immunity. Feed animals only on the owner’s instructions.</li><li><strong>Heat and stamina</strong> — Summer fieldwork is genuinely hard labor.
