<h2>Half the world’s old firms stand in Japan</h2>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">RESEARCH</span><strong>In Teikoku Databank’s national analysis of "shinise" long-established companies (as of September 2024), companies with over 100 years of history number 45,284 in Japan</strong> — The company’s commentary introduces research finding that Japan’s old firms account for roughly half the world’s, and two-thirds of those over 200 years. Meanwhile, 145 long-established companies went bankrupt in 2024 — past the Lehman-shock year, an all-time high.</div>

<p>The country with the world’s largest inventory of long-lasting companies — that is Japan. But the number has another face. Long history is no longer proof of safety. Old firms are failing, questioned on the quality of the core business and on successors. So what did the properties that lived 100, 1,000 years leave behind as assets?</p>

<h2>The oldest inns — 705, 717, 718</h2>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Registered with Guinness World Records as the "oldest hotel" is the Keiunkan of Nishiyama Onsen, Yamanashi (founded 705)</strong> — Second, the Sennen no Yu Koman (Kinosaki Onsen, Hyōgo, 717); third, the Hōshi Ryokan (Awazu Onsen, Ishikawa, opened 718, said to be now in its 46th generation) — the top three are all Japanese hot-spring inns. (Per the Guinness registration and each property’s public information, as of writing.)</div>

<p>Why it is interesting: <strong>the top three "oldest" are all inns.</strong> And at every one of them, the buildings were rebuilt while the water kept springing in the same place. What survived 1,300 years was neither the building nor the signboard, but <strong>the one line — "we host people with this water" — and the mechanism that carried it on</strong>. The true identity of a long-term asset lies not in things but in sentences and relationships.</p>

<h2>There are 2 kinds of "what does not change"</h2>

<p>The Shikinen Sengu of the Ise Grand Shrine rebuilds the sanctuary buildings every 20 years (in recent years there have been postponements with world conditions). For over 1,300 years, to keep the same form, it kept renewing the buildings — <strong>"what does not change" is of two kinds: "what is never changed" and "what is kept changing."</strong> Transposed to a property: what never changes is the one line (for what, and for whom, we host). What keeps changing is the fit of buildings, equipment, and words. The older the firm, the more it has rebuilt — a brand’s long-term asset is not fixity but <strong>a one line kept renewing</strong>.</p>

<h2>3 questions for ten years out</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Ten years from now, what should this region be?</strong> — The property is part of the region. Work backward from the ten-year future of the mountains, the sea, and the people.</li>
<li><strong>Ten years from now, what do you want guests to call you?</strong> — "That mountain-water property." A name worth being called grows in units of ten years.</li>
<li><strong>Ten years from now, what should the staff be proud of?</strong> — Before pride secures people, it secures the story.</li>
</ol>

<h2>3 things to keep — building, relationships, story</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Buildings</strong> — Materials that do not fade in ten years. Wood, stone, earth. Trend interiors become liabilities, not assets, in ten years.</li>
<li><strong>Relationships</strong> — With producers, neighboring businesses, the people of the region (<a href="#regional-experience-program">Regional Experience Programs: The Basics for Extending Inbound Stays</a> takes the partnering side). Relationships are the only asset money cannot buy.</li>
<li><strong>Story</strong> — One line that can still be told in ten years. Because there is a story, guests can keep the property in memory.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The 10-year box — the smallest first move, starting today</h2>

<p>What you need is one box. Once a year, put in that year’s single sheet — the opening-day price list, a guest’s handwritten line, a photograph of the staff, a record of the renovation — and seal it. <strong>You open it in ten years.</strong> When ten years’ worth of single sheets are gathered, what lies there is neither advertising nor a photo collection, but the inventory of long-term assets unique to your property. At the same time, enclose one sheet of paper bearing the one line that does not change. Read it only once a year, and check whether you can still say it — the Shikinen Sengu, practiced at the scale of a property.</p>

<h2>The other side — 5 traps of long-term thinking</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Being bound by the past</strong> — Mistaking "the way it has always been done" for the essence is the leading cause of old-firm failures. What never changes is the one line, not the manners.</li>
<li><strong>Mixing ignoring trends with neglecting equipment</strong> — Keep the materials; renew the pipes and the insulation. A ten-year asset is the stacking of comfortable todays.</li>
<li><strong>Depending on the owner personally</strong> — Leave the one line as "Mr./Ms. ○○’s personality," and it vanishes in ten years. Translating the individual’s story into the property’s line is the second generation’s work.</li>
<li><strong>Leaving no records</strong> — A ten-year asset can be born only from today’s single sheet. Assets are a mountain of records, not of memories.</li>
<li><strong>Talking of the future and neglecting the now</strong> — Before the ten-year story, tonight’s bath temperature. A long-term asset stands only on the extension line of a good today.</li>
</ul>

<p>What to tell in ten years — half the answer is already in the past. The other half is in today’s single sheet. Tonight, prepare one box, and start by putting in this year’s sheet.</p>
