11:T1db7,<p>“An illustrated map isn’t accurate,” we think, and end up handing over an official map. But go back a little in the history of maps, and the story is the reverse. The more a map pursued unrivaled accuracy, the more it needed pictures. This article thinks about the design of an inn’s illustrated map from two stories of maps.</p><h2>Inō Tadataka had pictures drawn on an accurate map</h2><p>Inō Tadataka set out on his first survey in 1800, at the age of 55, and over the following 17 years and 10 expeditions he walked and measured all of Japan. Tadataka himself died in 1818, and his disciples took over the unfinished map, completing it in 1821 as the “Dai Nihon Enkai Yochi Zenzu” (Maps of Japan’s Coastal Areas). Centered on 214 large-scale sheets (at a scale of 1:36,000), this atlas has an astonishing precision, with latitude errors of about one part in a thousand compared with present-day values, and it is said that foreigners who came to Japan in later years were amazed when they compared it with their own countries’ charts.</p><p>So far, this is a famous story. What an article on illustrated maps wants to say comes next. The Inō maps include, in addition to coastlines and highways, the roadside scenery and mountains drawn in. Pictures that should be unnecessary if only survey figures mattered are engraved on Japan’s first measured map. The people who made a map close to the world’s most accurate chose, at the same time, “a map on which the scenery can be seen.” Accuracy and pictures were never opposed from the start. Because the job of a map is not only to hand over coordinates but to deliver the land to the eyes of the people who walk it.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>Inō Tadataka’s “Dai Nihon Enkai Yochi Zenzu” (completed 1821) is Japan’s first measured nationwide map, surveyed on foot over 17 years and 10 expeditions, with roadside scenery and mountains drawn in as pictures</strong> — Completed by his disciples after Tadataka’s death. Latitude error of about one part in a thousand. The more a map pursued accuracy to its limit, the more it needed pictures.</div><h2>The London Tube map imitated an electrical circuit and discarded distance</h2><p>There is another story, in the opposite direction. In 1931, Harry Beck, an engineer working for the London Underground, devised a new design for the route map. For Underground passengers, geographic accuracy above ground is not the point — realizing this, he took inspiration from electrical circuit diagrams and represented the lines with only vertical, horizontal, and 45-degree diagonal straight lines, making only the relationships between stations easy to see without worrying about distance. The design was not adopted at first, but once it was published in 1933 it won acclaim and was officially adopted. From then on, route maps in cities around the world came to imitate this approach. It was not geographically correct, yet the number of people getting lost fell dramatically. What makes a map good or bad is decided not by the standard of measurement but by “the purpose of the people who use it.”</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">CASE</span><strong>Harry Beck’s London Underground map (adopted 1933) took inspiration from electrical circuit diagrams, discarded geographic accuracy, and made only the connections between stations easy to see</strong> — This design, rejected at first, became the standard for route maps worldwide. A map may be distorted to fit “the purpose of use” rather than accuracy.</div><h2>What should an inn’s illustrated map distort, and what should it draw?</h2><p>The design principles for illustrated maps that these two stories teach are clear. First, you do not have to stick to an accurate scale (Beck’s lesson). Draw landmarks large: the big tree visible from the inn, the tallest roof in the village, the bridge, the small shrine. Second, draw in the scenery and daily life (Inō’s lesson). This is where farm work starts in the morning. You will meet a dog at this corner. This is exactly the kind of information a GPS map will never tell you. Japanese addresses use a block system of “chōme and banchi” rather than street names, and the roads have no names. That is precisely why guidance by pictures and landmarks is especially strong in Japanese villages.</p><ul><li><strong>Draw only the 15-minute zone</strong> — Rather than the whole region, put the area within 15 minutes of the inn on a single A4 sheet. A map that lives, however small, is a map with a narrowed range.</li><li><strong>Draw slopes, stairs, and shade</strong> — The trio a flat map cannot show. It connects directly to planning summer walks.</li><li><strong>Write honestly</strong> — Dead ends, stretches with heavy traffic, private land that cannot be entered. The appeal of an illustrated map is its flavor, not deception.</li><li><strong>Blank space for notes</strong> — Leave white space where guests can add “things they found.” The map turns into a record of the experience.</li><li><strong>Map-making with children</strong> — It can also be developed into a plan where children draw an exploration map for shrine hunts and treasure hunts.</li></ul><h2>Maintenance, and not ending with the illustrated map alone</h2><ul><li><strong>Update costs</strong> — Shops close, roads change. Write “the year and month it was made” on the illustrated map, and review it at least once a year.</li><li><strong>Use together with the official address</strong> — For taxis and explanations in emergencies, the official address and map are necessary. Set up two tiers: the illustrated map is “for walking,” the official map is “for arriving.”</li><li><strong>Privacy</strong> — When drawing residents’ houses or fields, get their consent to include them. The land’s information belongs to the land’s people.</li><li><strong>Inns it suits</strong> — Every inn. There is no inn that cannot draw one. There are only inns that “have not drawn one yet.”</li></ul><h2>InnBuddy VIEW</h2><p>The legitimacy of the illustrated map is guaranteed by the history of maps itself.
