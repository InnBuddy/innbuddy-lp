1b:T1f50,<p>Summer is the largest demand window of the year, one that swings annual revenue. And there is one easily forgotten fact. <strong>OTA search rankings and preparation are decided before the season begins</strong>. By the time you think, in July, "let’s go take the top spots," most of the contest is over. This article rebuilds the three plays for winning search position in the summer peak — taking "clicks" with photos and the title, taking "demand" with price design, taking "conversion and repeat" with reviews — into a <strong>60-day countdown calendar</strong> that reaches back to May (the numbers are reference values of a typical pattern, one example).</p>

<h2>Why 60 days ahead — bookings move earlier than you think</h2>

<p>Domestic demand starts moving two to four weeks before check-in, and inbound moves earlier still — the farther the market, the earlier; and there is also polarization with markets that decide at the last minute (the published data on visitors’ booking timing was organized in the search-behavior article). In other words, in May and June, as summer’s window begins to open, the shape of your search results is already being judged. Not making the first page is nearly the same as "not existing" in that market — stand on that premise, and what to do is decided by counting backward.</p>

<h2>The 60-day countdown calendar</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>By the end of May</strong> — Replace the first photo and the title (Play 1)</li>
<li><strong>By mid-June</strong> — Fix the three-tier price plan and the weekly pace of increases (Play 2)</li>
<li><strong>By the end of June</strong> — Prepare the review-request QR codes and reply templates (Play 3)</li>
<li><strong>Every week through the peak</strong> — Fixed-point checks: the price gap with competitors, clicks, conversion</li>
<li><strong>The week the season ends</strong> — Record the numbers and write next year’s memo (the day to rewrite this article)</li>
</ul>

<h2>Play 1 — taking "clicks" with photos and the title</h2>

<p>The thumbnail (the first photo) is your signboard. The three principles of a photo that works in summer: light, lived-in warmth, and empty space.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Light</strong> — Favor morning sun and natural light. Interiors lit only by ceiling fixtures come off the list of summer choices.</li>
<li><strong>Lived-in warmth</strong> — Towels at the foot of the bed, barley tea on the table. Photograph the air of "the moment just before the guest enters the room." In summer especially, the question is whether a sense of coolness enters the frame.</li>
<li><strong>Empty space</strong> — Leave thirty percent of the frame open, and sell not the furniture but "the view and the feeling of release."</li>
</ul>

<p>Changing the first photo moves how you appear in search results more than almost anything else, at nearly zero cost (see the room-photography article). The title pattern is <strong>[season] + [who it’s for] + [differentiator]</strong>, within 60 characters. The concrete — "Summer / for families / with river-play gear rental" — beats the abstract — "a room of comfort and refinement."</p>

<h2>Play 2 — a price design that takes "demand"</h2>

<p>Peak-season pricing is not "flat +30%." It is a three-tier design.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>The early-booking tier</strong> — The tier that secures more than half of revenue before summer proper. One to two notches below the standard price (a reference value). It lands on the long-distance markets that want to fix plans early.</li>
<li><strong>The standard tier</strong> — The price of the demand band during the season.</li>
<li><strong>The last-minute tier</strong> — The final lever on the remaining inventory. Raise it in small steps; the week the growth stops is the market’s ceiling.</li>
</ol>

<p>The crux is <strong>not striking the ceiling at once, but raising it little by little (the ratchet approach)</strong>. Reading the weeks as the rises stop, measure the market’s ceiling. Doing exactly what the property next door does, price creates no difference — what creates difference is the design of when, which tier, and at what price you release.</p>

<h2>Play 3 — taking "conversion" with reviews</h2>

<p>Before the booking button, guests line the scores up side by side. That 4.5 is the trust line, and 4.7 and above the premium signal that supports price, is as organized in the review-management article. In the peak season, run review operations at the same frequency as cleaning.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Reply to 100% within 72 hours</strong> — The replies are content the next guest reads.</li>
<li><strong>A review-request QR in the room</strong> — Satisfaction is highest in the quiet hour just after check-in.</li>
<li><strong>Put names and specifics in replies</strong> — Not "thank you" but "Mr. and Ms. XX, have you been on your morning walk yet?" What creates repeat guests is a reply that leaves a memory of the person.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The philosophy of the peak — a season to "harvest," not to "build"</h2>

<p>The thinking shared by the three plays, in one line. Summer is not the season to build demand but <strong>the season to harvest the results of the photos, price design, and review operations stacked up day by day</strong>. If the preparation is finished by the end of May, the work of summer can concentrate on the front line (cleaning, service, safety). A property that carries the preparation into July holds both the front line and the prep through the summer — the familiar picture of the properties whose front line collapses in the peak.</p>

<h2>The other side — what breaks after you take the top spots</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The weight of last-minute price hikes</strong> — Rooms pushed all the way to the ceiling may maximize that summer’s revenue while shaving reputation and breaking the foundation of the next year. Maximizing the numbers and sustaining the property are different yardsticks.</li>
<li><strong>Over-staged photos</strong> — An over-embellished first photo increases clicks and, with the gap on arrival, increases complaints (Pattern 1 in the complaint-handling article). The summer photo is best at "a little better than the real thing."</li>
<li><strong>The front line’s stamina</strong> — As occupancy nears full, the first things cut are the hours for review replies and cleaning. But what supports the benefit of high ranking to the very end is that front line. Do not leave the reply hours to "when there’s time."</li>
<li><strong>A summer-only property collapses in autumn</strong> — The demand mountains of visitors to Japan are not summer’s alone (see the seasonal demand article). A design that stakes everything on summer loses its stamina in September’s drop.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is to write the date 60 days from today. If it is May, the countdown can begin tonight; if it is November — that too is a fine beginning. The three summer plays start moving when you paste the candidate for your first photo onto the first line of the calendar you have written out.</p>

<h2>1. 旺季按“前60天”确定</h2>
<p>让我们看一下预订渠道。从出现在 OTA 搜索列表中到预订的点击率 (CTR) 通常为 3-8%。大多数点击将集中在前三个搜索结果上。换句话说，不在首页就等于“不存在”。</p>

<h2>2. 措施 1：通过照片和标题获得“点击”</h2>

<li><strong>生活感</strong> ——床脚放一条毛巾，桌子上放一个杯子。营造“就在客人进来之前”的氛围。</li>
<li><strong>利润</strong> — 留出 30% 的画作空白，并出售“景观”而不是“家具”。</li>
</ul>
<p>在 OTA A/B 测试中，第一个单一更改是提高 CTR 的最可靠杠杆，20-40% CTR 的改进属于常见范围。投入成本几乎为零，是最具成本效益的措施。</p>

<h2>3.措施2：根据价格捕捉“需求”的设计</h2>

<li><strong>标准层次结构</strong> — 季节期间需求带的价格。</li>
<li><strong>上一层</strong> — 剩余临街面的最终控制杆。每周加价1.1至1.2倍，达到市场上限时停止。</li>
</ol>
