Nordic inns have valued firewood, lighting, and the warmth of wood not simply as matters of taste or aesthetics. In a place where much of the year brings long nights, these are practical ways to live through winter. This article takes apart those design elements and brings them back to inns in Japan.</p><h2>Seven hours of daylight at the winter solstice</h2><p>First, understand the conditions through numbers. Copenhagen has seven hours and one minute of daylight at the winter solstice and about seventeen and a half hours at the summer solstice, according to a sunrise and sunset database. Daylight in the same place varies by more than a factor of two with the season. That range shapes Nordic approaches to everyday life: responding to long summer days and to winter darkness. For an inn, the latter is especially important. A long stretch of time remains between sunset and bedtime in a Nordic winter. Does an inn treat it as an empty margin, or make it the main part of the stay? Lighting is a first step toward answering that question.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Copenhagen has seven hours and one minute of daylight at the winter solstice and about seventeen and a half hours at the summer solstice</strong> — Daylight in the same place varies by more than a factor of two. According to a book by Meik Wiking of Copenhagen’s Happiness Research Institute, 85% of respondents associate candles with hygge, and 28% say they light candles daily. These figures are not merely about a preference for decoration. On Nordic winter nights, light both illuminates a room and shapes how the night is spent. Candlelight narrows the possibilities: reading, talking, drinking something hot, doing nothing. Tasks easily done under bright lighting become difficult. Here, the warmth of wood has two meanings.
