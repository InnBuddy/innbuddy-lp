1b:T1d02,<p>The welcome and the send-off are <strong>not "greetings" but "the beginning and end of a story."</strong> The guest’s memory of a property is decided between the 30 seconds of arrival and the send-off at departure — these two points. This article is the foundation for designing them. Of the five minutes of arrival, what belongs here is <strong>the 30 seconds of words and body</strong> (preparing for pre-arrival anxiety is <a href="#guest-journey-map">The Whole Guest Experience: Mapping the Touchpoints</a>; the first five minutes of resting the body are <a href="#welcome-amenity-5min">Welcome Amenity: Designing the First Five Minutes</a>; the one move at check-in is <a href="#upgrade-sales-checkin">Upgrade Sales: One Room Up at Check-In</a> — each has its post).</p>

<h2>The welcome and send-off at the village boundary — the kō invented "greeting the road"</h2>

<p>Of the Ise pilgrimage kō, old records leave descriptions like this. On departure day, the members <strong>gathered at the kō’s inn and held a celebration called "detachi"</strong>, sending off the proxy pilgrims. And on the day of return, they went out as far as the village boundary to greet them with a feast called <strong>"sakamukae"</strong> — seeing the traveler off and welcoming them home were not greetings between individuals but <strong>village-wide events</strong>.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Ise kō confraternities are said to have held a send-off celebration called "detachi" (departure) at the kō’s inn on the day of setting out, and, on the day of homecoming, gone out to the village boundary to greet the traveler with a feast called "sakamukae" (slope welcome) (as described in encyclopedias and studies of early-modern kō)</strong> — Welcoming and sending-off were once "events of the place." The community, not the individual, carried them.</div>

<p>Transposed to the modern property, the scheme is this. <strong>The welcome is "welcome"; the send-off is "again."</strong> At two points, once each, the property holds the "beginning" and the "end" of the guest’s story. It is, in old terms, work standing at the village boundary.</p>

<h2>The welcome — 30 seconds that turn anxiety into expectation</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Call the name</strong> — "Mr./Ms. ○○, we have been expecting you." The name is the property’s duty, written in the booking data (the relief of being called by name is the first line of trust). The manners of records are handled by <a href="#guest-data-basics">Guest Data Basics: What to Record, and How to Use It</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Touch the luggage</strong> — "May I take your bags." The one second of taking a heavy bag makes a relief worth ten sentences (the five minutes of what the hands hold belong to the welcome amenity article).</li>
<li><strong>Add one touch of the day’s nature</strong> — "The mountain mist is lovely today," "The sea should be calm this evening." Not a weather greeting, but one line only of this day, this land. The viewpoint of the season’s protagonist is shared with <a href="#12month-marketing-calendar">The 12 Months of a Property: A Ready-to-Use Marketing Calendar</a>.</li>
</ol>

<h2>The send-off — the road home is part of the stay’s experience</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Until they are out of sight</strong> — The car pulls away, they start walking: see them off until the figure disappears. A wordless watching-through stays in memory far longer than thirty seconds of small talk.</li>
<li><strong>Hand them the next season</strong> — "Next time, come in the fresh-green season, when the mountain vegetables are at their best." The one line that turns a farewell from "goodbye" into the words of <strong>"again."</strong></li>
<li><strong>Add one handwritten line</strong> — "Thank you for staying with us." With one card line, the property’s words travel with them, in the bag, down the road home.</li>
</ol>

<p>Stretch the send-off’s timeline a little. <strong>Settling the bill the night before</strong> (keeping the morning of departure free of paperwork = pulling the send-off forward), <strong>breakfast</strong> (the last meal = the closing dish of the trip), <strong>checking luggage and forgotten items</strong> (one of the biggest spoilers of the road home), and <strong>the send-off at the door</strong>. The morning of departure, like the day of arrival, is a line, not points.</p>

<h2>Operation — one checklist and 30 seconds at the morning meeting</h2>

<ul>
<li>Put the three welcome points and three send-off points on a single checklist.</li>
<li>For 30 seconds at the morning meeting, share today’s expected arrivals and departures. One sentence — "the Mr./Ms. ○○ we send off today is a repeat guest" — deepens the words of the send-off.</li>
<li>Preparing for forgotten items is part of the send-off mechanism too. Noticed right after departure: one call to the guest’s contact. A forgotten item can make both the worst road home and the best re-contact.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The other side — when welcoming and sending-off exhaust the property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Forcing the send-off</strong> — Some guest segments feel guilty being stood for until they vanish. To the corner, or just at the door — read the distance from how the person walks.</li>
<li><strong>The last sale</strong> — Product suggestions at settlement or pitches for the next booking can stain the memory of parting (the thinking of not making send-off time into sales time is shared with the retail article).</li>
<li><strong>Letters becoming shells</strong> — A handwritten card with the same words every time stops being handwritten by the third. On days you cannot write, the choice is not to hand one over.</li>
<li><strong>The wait of the welcome</strong> — If you promise a pickup at the station, design it so no one waits (the principles of how waiting feels are handled in the turnover article).</li>
<li><strong>Transfer safety</strong> — Snowy roads, night roads, fatigued driving go beyond the bounds of omotenashi. Have the courage to decline, and one line of alternative (the taxi’s phone number).</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is writing out the "road home" of one couple departing tomorrow. From the door, to the corner, to the station (airport) — of those three points, how far is the property’s scenery? If the road home is part of the stay’s experience, walk that distance once with your own eyes. The village boundary is closer than you think.</p>
