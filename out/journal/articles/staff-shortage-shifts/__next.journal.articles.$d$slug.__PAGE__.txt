11:T1c19,<p>Staff shortage is a structural condition of this industry. But within "not enough people," there is a part that design can change. This article organizes the design of shifts that run even on a small team — beginning with the real numbers underneath.</p>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>In the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare’s General Survey on Working Conditions (2025), the rate of annual paid leave taken in accommodation and food services was 50.7%, the lowest of all industries (survey average 66.9%), and scheduled weekly working hours were also the longest, at 40 hours and 2 minutes (survey average 39 hours 24 minutes). Though improved from the fiscal 2022 rate of 44.3% seen in the staff-retention article, the industry remains in last place (as of writing)</strong> — The difficulty of taking days off exists not as a feeling but as statistics.</div>

<h2>The essence — days off are written into the shift table</h2>

<p>When reading this number, the reading you easily fall into is "our staff just need to ask for days off." But an industry-wide last place in leave taken means this is <strong>not a matter of individual courage but of structure</strong>. A day off is not the taker’s effort; it is <strong>something written into the shift table from the start</strong>. Shift design under staff shortage is not the craft of cutting people — it is the craft of building a structure in which people can rest. Properties where days off can be taken keep their people. The entrance to this virtuous cycle lies not in hiring or in enthusiasm, but in the design of the shifts.</p>

<h2>Diagnosis — the "job audit"</h2>

<p>The starting point is not the shift table but an inventory of the work. List every job in the property and sort it into four classes.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>A: must be done by a person</strong> — Interaction with guests, work requiring judgment.</li>
<li><strong>B: can be done by a person, but is standardizable</strong> — Work that can be turned into manuals and checklists.</li>
<li><strong>C: can be automated</strong> — Procedural reception, chat, settlement, and the like.</li>
<li><strong>D: can be eliminated</strong> — Work that serves no one’s value.</li>
</ol>

<p>When this sorting is done, something surprising happens at many properties. <strong>Inside the hours thought to be "short of people," C and D were mixed in.</strong> Part of the true identity of staff shortage lies not in headcount but in the content of the work.</p>

<h2>The "person-hour" principle</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Assign person-hours only to A and B</strong> — People to the work where people’s value lives. The hours of A are precisely the hours that stay in guests’ memories.</li>
<li><strong>C is for automation and outsourcing</strong> — Self check-in, automated chat replies, external laundry. But define C not as "work a machine may do" but as "work that can be made procedural." The moment you automate even the conversation with guests, you are cutting A.</li>
<li><strong>D is to be reduced</strong> — The audit exists for the decision to stop the work whose only reason is "we’ve always done it."</li>
</ul>

<h2>The 3 designs of shifts</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Concentration on the peaks</strong> — Stack people into the busy hours (the breakfast and check-in waves) and gather tasks into the troughs.</li>
<li><strong>Cross-training</strong> — Make it so one person can cover several jobs. Japan’s small ryokan have always run inside a culture where everyone holds concurrent duties. Do it not "because there’s no choice" but "as design."</li>
<li><strong>Planning the "minimum operating team"</strong> — Decide in advance the minimum staffing for days when people are short.</li>
</ul>

<p>The third is this article’s core. <strong>Most properties write the "ideal shift," and watch it collapse with every vacancy. Do the reverse.</strong> What you write first is the staffing of the worst day — a cold going around, a resignation, holidays overlapping — a "minimum operating team" that runs even then. With front desk 1, housekeeping 2, kitchen 1: decide what you do, what you give up, and what you announce in advance. The ordinary shift then becomes a design that adds one layer onto that minimum. A shift table written in reverse order does not collapse, because it is written on the assumption of collapsing. And this table is at once a "staff-shortage contingency table" and <strong>the grounds on which staff can take days off</strong> — a property with a line of sight to running even on the worst day need not rely on courage to approve a day off.</p>

<h2>The other side — when the design exhausts the floor</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Misjudging D</strong> — The most dangerous audit judges work that creates the guest’s experience to be "eliminable." D is only work that "serves no one’s value." Look at the candidates for cutting together with the staff, always.</li>
<li><strong>The price of cross-training</strong> — A property where everyone can do everything is also a property where no one holds a specialty. Keep the core specialties (cleaning, cooking, service) as someone’s "main battlefield" in each.</li>
<li><strong>The beauty of the table versus the reality</strong> — A shift table with no blank spaces does not mean little overtime. Once a month, compare actual working hours with the table. That gap is what shaves the staff’s trust.</li>
<li><strong>The face of automation</strong> — On the night self check-in is introduced, there is no person beside the guest in trouble. The more you add C, the more the quality of the remaining A is questioned.</li>
<li><strong>Securing "meaningful work"</strong> — The design’s true intent is shifting person-hours toward A. Not "people become idle because C and D increase," but "people can be stood into A" — hold this order, or the design becomes mere reduction.</li>
</ul>

<p>The first step is, next week, to write the four-way job sorting on one sheet. Mark A through D down the left of the list, and choose two Cs and two Ds. Move one automation and one elimination within this month. The tool for fighting staff shortage is, before the job listing, this one sheet.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>仅将“人员时间”分配给 A 和 B</strong> — 让人们从事对他们有价值的工作。</li>
<li><strong>C是自动化/外包</strong> — AI聊天、自助入住、外部清洁。</li>
<li><strong>D 被排除</strong> — 减少对任何人都没有价值的工作。</li>
<li>例：作业审核工时减少20%（参考值）。</li>
</ul>
<h2>3、换档设计</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>聚焦高峰</strong> — 在繁忙时间增加拥挤（前餐厅）。</li>
<li><strong>交叉训练（多技能）</strong> — 一个人负责多项任务。</li>
<li><strong>“最小运营团队”计划</strong> — 人力短缺时的最低限度制度。</li>
</ul>
<h2>4、效果</h2>
<ul>
<li>降低劳动力成本（例如降低 15%）。</li>
<li>维持评论分数（例如 4.5 或更高）。</li>
<li>减少员工流动率（人们正在做有意义的工作）。</li>
</ul>

<h2>为什么现在值得关注？</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>客人期待的变化：</strong>“如何在人员短缺的情况下安排轮班”是客人的期望与五年前相比发生了变化的问题。过去“有了更好”的服务，如今已变成“没有就难以被选择”。适应这些变化，是住宿设施持续经营的前提。</li>
<li><strong>与地区关系：</strong>住宿运营的举措提升的不只是单个旅馆，也包括整个地区的价值。通过与当地生产者和周边商户合作，旅馆的行动会成为“当地故事”。社区共同参与，才更容易长期持续。</li>
<li><strong>不增加员工负担：</strong>新举措不应增加员工负担。通过建立机制，用一张表或每次15分钟的学习来运转，就能在不增加员工负担的情况下持续改进。</li>
</ul>

<h2>避免失败的3个要点</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>从小处开始——先做好一件事：</strong>一开始就想全部改变，往往难以持续。先从一件事开始。可以从一间客房、一个货架或一句话开始；即使效果不理想，影响也小，更容易调整。</li>
<li><strong>记录——用一张表看清楚：</strong>把行动记录在一张表里，效果就能被看见。你会知道哪些做法有效、哪些需要调整，记录也会成为下一轮改进的依据。</li>
<li><strong>持续——每周一次，每次15分钟：</strong>新行动需要持续。每周留出15分钟，逐渐形成习惯，最后才会成为团队文化。</li>
</ol>
