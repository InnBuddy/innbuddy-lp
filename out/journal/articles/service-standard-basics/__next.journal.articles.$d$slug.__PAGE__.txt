11:T1d03,<p>Whenever the subject of an inn's service comes up, this opposition always appears: "Omotenashi is given from the heart; it should never be standardized by manual" versus "quality changing from person to person is frightening; we should build standards." This opposition, in fact, needs no resolving. The world of Japan's traditional arts has long carried the wisdom of having both, handed down as a form called shu-ha-ri. Keep the form (shu), break the form (ha), and eventually leave the form (ri). In this view of training — of the tea ceremony and the martial ways — the form is not a frame that kills individuality; it is the skeleton that protects the craft before individuality has grown. The "standard" of service is the same. Make the best day's service happen on an ordinary day. The form for that is the standard, and this article is about how to build that form.</p>

<h2>The three elements of a standard — words, movement, time</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Words</strong> — The one sentence to be said. Not a script but a "form." Just as the forms of kabuki are an accumulation of generations of ingenuity, the inn's one-line forms are a storehouse of the front line's best lines.</li>
<li><strong>Movement</strong> — Order, distance, carriage. Where to receive the luggage, the half step ahead when guiding, the way the tray is held. What the body learns is preserved more surely as a form than as an explanation.</li>
<li><strong>Time</strong> — Deadlines. Answer the phone within three rings; respond to a request within ten minutes; the send-off is thirty seconds at the door. A time standard is not a boast of speed; it is the design of the waiting side's anxiety.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The five places to begin</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>The welcome</strong> — The first thirty seconds. Distance, eye line, one word. The story of the first impression's three seconds and five minutes is left to the welcome-amenity article.</li>
<li><strong>Check-in</strong> — The wait and the order of explanation. Once the order of what to hand over is fixed, forgotten items and missed explanations disappear at the same time.</li>
<li><strong>Complaints</strong> — Acceptance, then facts, then remedy, in that order. The back side of this form (the research and the design of authority) was dug into in the service recovery article.</li>
<li><strong>Requests</strong> — A form that returns the three points — "possible / not possible / by when" — in one line. A vague acceptance becomes a large betrayal later.</li>
<li><strong>The send-off</strong> — The last word. What decides the memory of an experience is its final moment (the mechanism is in the peak-end story of the "small experiences" article).</li>
</ol>

<h2>How to write a standard — "We say X. Example: Y."</h2>

<p>The biggest failure in standard documents is writing them as commands: "Do X." A command is forgotten the instant it is read. The form to write is the three layers of <strong>standard + example + judgment criterion</strong>. First write the standard in one line ("At the welcome, we stand up and offer a word of greeting"); next write the example in actual words ("Example: You must be tired. Welcome."); last, write the criterion ("However, for a guest clearly in a hurry, shift to a word while receiving the luggage"). A standard with an example gets read; a standard with a criterion protects the front line. The "please do X" manual dies in the places its writer never visits.</p>

<h2>Maintaining the standard — a monthly check, and a receptacle for "ha"</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>A monthly spot check (one place only)</strong> — Checking everything at once is impossible. This month the welcome, next month the send-off. In a year, every place gets its turn.</li>
<li><strong>Receive staff proposals as "ha"</strong> — "I think this form would work better changed like this" — that one line is the entrance of what shu-ha-ri calls "ha." At inns where proposals update the form, the standard becomes the front line's property and keeps being renewed. Where proposals are handled as a formality, the standard eventually becomes furniture no one guards.</li>
<li><strong>Hand it to newcomers first</strong> — The standard's greatest beneficiary is the staff member who joined yesterday. A workplace with forms halves, at once, the burden of teaching and the anxiety of being taught. Read together with the learning-design article, and it connects as the core of training.</li>
</ul>

<div class="stats"><span class="tag">ANALYSIS</span><strong>The purpose of a standard is not "mechanical service" but "making the best day's service happen on an ordinary day"</strong> — A standard is not a tool for erasing human differences; it builds the foundation on which differences swing upward. Because the form of "shu" exists, "ha" (ingenuity) and "ri" (the omotenashi that is uniquely one's own) can be tried safely.</div>

<h2>The other side of standardization</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Excess of form</strong> — Make everything a form and service turns into labor. What to standardize is only where the difference comes out badly. Where the difference comes out well (conversation, seasonal topics), leave it outside the form.</li>
<li><strong>The harm of old forms</strong> — An unupdated standard is a tomb that keeps enforcing a departed person's habits. A standard without the monthly check and the receptacle for "ha" can be worse than having none.</li>
<li><strong>The hollowing of checks</strong> — When the spot check turns into fault-finding, the front line moves to the side of hiding. The check exists not to defend the form but to confirm the form still fits the front line.</li>
<li><strong>Word forms and region and generation</strong> — At an inn where "Irasshaimase" is the form, the right word for an elderly local regular may be "Oh, you've come." This is exactly why the criterion layer is written.</li>
<li><strong>The important stories left unwritten</strong> — Wages, days off, the safety of the workplace — do not paper over the problems of the working environment with "service standards." Standards function on top of the foundation (the three basics of retention).</li>
</ul>

<p>What to do this week: choose one of the five places, and write the three layers — standard, example, judgment criterion — in just three lines. When it is written, show the three lines to the staff member who has worked longest, and ask, "Please fix whatever is different." The first step of shu-ha-ri always begins with another person's eyes.</p>

<li><strong>字</strong> ——剧本（一句话说）。</li>
<li><strong>移动</strong> — 程序（顺序、距离、手势）。</li>
<li><strong>时间</strong> — 截止日期（您必须在多少分钟内做出回应）。</li>
</ul>
<h2>2. 5 个起点</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>欢迎</strong> — 前 30 秒（距离、视线、单词）。</li>
<li><strong>报到</strong> — 等待时间/解释顺序。</li>
<li><strong>宣称</strong> — 接受、事实、补偿（参见恢复文章）。</li>
<li><strong>要求</strong> —“可以/不能/何时”回答。</li>
<li><strong>送行</strong> ——最后一句话（见小经验文章）。</li>
</ol>
<h2>3. 如何编写标准</h2>
<ul>
<li>不是手册<strong>“标准+范例+标准”</strong>分三栏写。</li>
<li>从“请...”<strong>“我说〜。例如：〜”</strong>的形状。</li>
</ul>
<h2>4. 维持标准</h2>
<ul>
<li>每月一次<strong>抽查</strong>（只有一个位置）。</li>
<li>将员工的建议转化为“标准更新”。</li>
<li>首先将“标准”传递给新员工（培训的核心）。</li>
</ul>

<h2>为什么现在值得关注？</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>客人期待的变化：</strong>“服务的基础——不区分人的‘标准’。”与五年前相比，客人的期望发生了变化。过去“有了更好”的服务，如今已变成“没有就难以被选择”。适应这些变化，是住宿设施持续经营的前提。</li>
<li><strong>与地区关系：</strong>住宿运营的举措提升的不只是单个旅馆，也包括整个地区的价值。通过与当地生产者和周边商户合作，旅馆的行动会成为“当地故事”。社区共同参与，才更容易长期持续。</li>
<li><strong>不增加员工负担：</strong>新举措不应增加员工负担。通过建立机制，用一张表或每次15分钟的学习来运转，就能在不增加员工负担的情况下持续改进。</li>
</ul>

<h2>避免失败的3个要点</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>从小处开始——先做好一件事：</strong>一开始就想全部改变，往往难以持续。先从一件事开始。可以从一间客房、一个货架或一句话开始；即使效果不理想，影响也小，更容易调整。</li>
<li><strong>记录——用一张表看清楚：</strong>把行动记录在一张表里，效果就能被看见。你会知道哪些做法有效、哪些需要调整，记录也会成为下一轮改进的依据。</li>
<li><strong>持续——每周一次，每次15分钟：</strong>新行动需要持续。每周留出15分钟，逐渐形成习惯，最后才会成为团队文化。</li>
</ol>
