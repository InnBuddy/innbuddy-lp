11:T1d46,<p>When people think of a papermaking experience, they picture holding a screen (su) and scooping fibers out of the water. That is the last step of papermaking, and only part of it. The branches of kōzo (paper mulberry) are steamed and stripped of their bark, bleached into white fibers, and beaten loose. Preparing the material takes most of the time in papermaking. What an inn in a washi village should convey first is this picture: “90% of the work is something other than paper.”</p><h2>If the world uses “tame-zuki,” Japan uses “nagashi-zuki”</h2><p>There are two broad ways of forming paper. In “tame-zuki,” the pulp is scooped onto the screen and held there while the water drains. This was the method used when papermaking technology came from China, and traditional papermaking around the world is basically tame-zuki as well. In Japan, “nagashi-zuki” developed independently, adding a mucilage called “neri,” made from plants such as tororo-aoi (sunset hibiscus). Neri slows the drainage of water, so the long fibers can be scooped again and again and sloshed back and forth on the screen to intertwine without being cut. This technique is one reason washi is thin and strong.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">FACT</span><strong>Nagashi-zuki is a technique that developed independently in Japan; traditional papermaking around the world is basically tame-zuki</strong> — By using the power of neri (mucilage) to slow the drainage of water and intertwining the long fibers without cutting them, a thin, strong paper is born.</div><h2>There is paper that has survived more than a thousand years</h2><p>The Shōsōin repository of Tōdaiji temple holds the “Shōsōin Documents,” a body of paper documents from about 1,300 years ago. They are paper historical materials without parallel in the world, and most of them are paper made from kōzo. While acidic Western paper made from wood pulp deteriorates within decades, washi is close to neutral and resists acidification. The durability of washi is rooted first of all in this capacity for preservation.</p><div class="stats"><span class="tag">DID YOU KNOW?</span><strong>The “Nihon Sandai Jitsuroku” records the recycling of waste paper by re-forming it in 886</strong> — Since that era, washi has been regenerated by “re-forming” it. Recycling is an old practice of washi (according to research on the Shōsōin Documents).</div><p>In 2014, “Washi, craftsmanship of traditional Japanese hand-made paper” (the three production areas of Sekishu-banshi, Hon-minoshi, and Hosokawa-shi) was inscribed on UNESCO’s Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage, and in December 2025, Echizen Torinoko-gami (from Echizen City, Fukui Prefecture; a smooth, lustrous paper made from ganpi) was added. What is noteworthy is that the unit of inscription is not “paper” but “a skill that continues.” What must be protected is not the product but the hands that keep making it and the fields that grow its materials.</p><h2>The great Florence flood carried washi into the world’s conservation studios</h2><p>In 1966, Florence, Italy, was struck by a major flood, and Renaissance-era manuscripts and artworks were badly damaged. It was washi that conservation specialists turned their attention to at that time. Investigation confirmed its suitability, and from then on washi came to be used in cultural-property conservation around the world. Today, the papers used in conservation at places such as the British Museum and the Louvre include Japanese washi, starting with the “tengujō-shi” of Hidaka Village, Kōchi Prefecture. Machine-made tengujō-shi, at a thickness of 0.02 millimeters, is the thinnest paper in the world. This translucent paper is used for lining old documents, and in the cleaning of Italian frescoes it is sometimes used as a material to absorb dirt.</p><p>Why is this story interesting? Because the world pays for washi not for its Japanese-style design but for its performance in preservation. And the essence is that this performance comes from the everyday techniques of the production area: “the long fibers of kōzo” and “nagashi-zuki.” Translated, it goes like this. The inn’s shōji screens, andon lanterns, and lampshades are lights made of the same material that the world’s museums trust. Whether you know that fact or not should change how you approach the chore of re-papering the shōji.</p><h2>Display the tools, tell the story with light</h2><p>Practice starts small. Hang one papermaking screen and frame (su and keta) in the corridor. Place a single washi lampshade in the lounge at night. Forming petals or leaves into handmade washi is the same idea as handmade paper abroad; in Sicily and Malta there are papers with olive leaves or lemon peel formed into them. As a souvenir to make, paper with the land’s flowers and grasses formed into it stays in the memory. The light of washi leaks through the gaps between the fibers and diffuses softly. Only when combined with “the courage to make it darker,” lowering the night lighting by one step, does washi light become the protagonist.</p><h2>Inns it suits, inns it doesn’t</h2><ul><li><strong>Inns it suits</strong> — Inns with fittings that go well with washi, such as shōji and andon. Inns that can partner with craftspeople in the production area. Inns that can secure a place for board-drying the formed paper.</li><li><strong>Inns it doesn’t suit</strong> — Inns where managing humidity and damage is difficult. Inns that would have to sell papermaking as “an experience completed entirely at the inn” (full-scale papermaking is the work of craftspeople).</li><li><strong>Seasonality</strong> — Kōzo is harvested in winter. The design can be split: tours of material preparation in winter, papermaking experiences year-round.</li><li><strong>Safety</strong> — The papermaking experience takes place at the water. Watch footing and cuffs, and in winter be mindful of the water temperature as well. Steaming, bark stripping, and beating are craftspeople’s processes involving burns and blades; limit the experience to forming and board-drying.</li></ul><h2>InnBuddy VIEW</h2><p>The assets of an inn in a washi village are a skill inscribed as UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage and a performance used in the world’s conservation studios. And both of them lie within everyday tools and handwork. Start small. Hang one screen, light one washi lamp, and write a single line saying this paper is a material that can last a thousand years.
